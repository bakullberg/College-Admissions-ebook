# 12. Understanding Financial Aid

The "sticker price" listed on a college's website is often not what any given family actually pays. This chapter explains the two forms most families will file, the two broad types of aid, and the number that actually matters.

## FAFSA and CSS Profile Basics

**The FAFSA** (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the form nearly every college and the federal government uses to determine eligibility for federal aid — grants, work-study, and federal loans — and most states and many colleges also use it for their own aid. It's free, it opens each fall for the following academic year, and every student planning to apply for any aid should file it, regardless of how "wealthy" your family believes itself to be — some aid, and nearly all federal loan eligibility, doesn't depend on financial need in the way people assume.

**The CSS Profile** is a more detailed form, run by the College Board, required by several hundred (mostly private) colleges in addition to the FAFSA. It asks for more granular financial information — including things the FAFSA doesn't, like home equity in some cases — which lets those schools award their own institutional aid more precisely. It also carries a small fee per school, though fee waivers are available for qualifying families.

Check every school on your list individually: some require only the FAFSA, some require both, and deadlines vary by school (see chapter 3's tracker, and the Appendix's month-by-month planner).

## Need-Based vs. Merit Aid

- **Need-based aid** is awarded according to your family's demonstrated financial need, calculated from your FAFSA (and CSS Profile, if required). It can include federal and state grants, institutional grants from the college itself, work-study, and loans.
- **Merit aid** is awarded based on your academic (or sometimes artistic or athletic) achievement, independent of financial need. Some merit aid is automatic based on GPA and test scores; some requires a separate scholarship application (see chapter 13).

Many students receive a combination of both. A school doesn't have to be need-blind or offer generous need-based aid to still offer meaningful merit money to a strong applicant — worth researching for every school on your list, not just the ones marketed as "affordable."

## What "Net Price" Really Means

The sticker price (tuition, fees, room, board) is the starting number — not the number that matters. **Net price** is what a specific family actually pays after all grants and scholarships (but *before* loans, since loans are money you pay back, not money that reduces your cost).

Every college is legally required to post a **net price calculator** on its website. Use it early, for every school on your list, with real financial information — a rough five-minute estimate is far more useful than none at all, and it can meaningfully reorder how "affordable" schools on your list actually look. A private school with a $70,000 sticker price and strong institutional aid can end up cheaper than a public school with a $30,000 sticker price and little aid to offer — the sticker price alone tells you almost nothing.

The real comparison always happens in chapter 14, once actual award letters are in hand — but understanding net price now means you build your list with realistic expectations from the start, instead of falling for (or ruling out) a school based on a number that was never the real one.
