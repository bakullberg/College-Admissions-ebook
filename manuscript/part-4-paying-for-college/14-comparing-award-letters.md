# 14. Comparing Award Letters

Once acceptances start arriving, so do financial aid award letters — and they are, frustratingly, not standardized. Two schools can present the same real cost in ways that look completely different on the page. This chapter is about reading past the formatting to the number that actually matters.

## Reading an Aid Offer Line by Line

Every award letter should let you identify, for one full academic year:

1. **Total cost of attendance** — tuition, fees, room, board, and often an estimate for books and personal expenses. If a letter doesn't state this plainly, find it separately on the school's website — you need it to do the math below.
2. **Grants and scholarships** — money that does not need to be repaid. This is the only category that actually reduces your real cost.
3. **Work-study** — a job opportunity, not a discount; it provides income but requires you to actually work the hours to receive it.
4. **Loans** (federal, and sometimes institutional or recommended private loans) — money you borrow and must repay, often with interest, after graduation.

The critical calculation: **cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships equals your real net price.** Work-study and loans are not deductions from your cost — they're separate categories that some letters blur together, deliberately or not, to make the "gap you need to cover" look smaller than it is.

## Loans vs. Grants

Treat every loan line on an award letter as debt, not aid — even when the letter's formatting lists it right alongside your grants as part of a single combined "award" figure. Some schools' letters make this distinction admirably clear; others bury it. When comparing two schools, recompute each one's real net price using grants and scholarships only, ignoring loans in that calculation, so you're comparing what you'd actually owe on graduation day, not what fills the same-looking blank on each page.

Within loans, know the difference between subsidized federal loans (interest doesn't accrue while you're in school), unsubsidized federal loans (interest accrues immediately), and private loans (typically higher rates, fewer borrower protections, and often requiring a co-signer). Federal loans should generally be exhausted before considering private ones.

## Requesting a Reconsideration

If your financial situation has changed since you filed the FAFSA or CSS Profile — a job loss, a medical expense, a change in family circumstances — or if a school you'd genuinely prefer offered meaningfully better aid than one you like just as much, it is entirely normal to ask for a second look. This is often called an **appeal** or **professional judgment review**.

A good appeal:

- Goes to the school's financial aid office directly, not admissions.
- States your situation clearly and specifically, with documentation where possible (a job loss letter, medical bills, updated income information).
- If based on a competing offer, includes the actual competing award letter — many schools will consider matching or improving an offer when shown a genuine, comparable one from a peer institution.
- Is polite, factual, and free of ultimatums — financial aid officers have real discretion, and a respectful, well-documented request is far more likely to succeed than a demanding one.

Not every appeal succeeds, but asking costs nothing, and schools generally expect a reasonable number of them each year. The worst outcome is typically just "no change" — rarely does asking make your existing offer worse.
