# 13. Scholarships

Scholarship money is real, but finding it takes more effort than typing "scholarships" into a search engine. This chapter covers where to actually look, how to prioritize your time, and how to avoid the scams that circle every scholarship season.

## Where to Actually Find Them

In rough order of return on your time:

1. **Your target colleges themselves.** The single highest-value source. Many schools award merit aid automatically based on your application (see chapter 12) — but many also have specific, named scholarships requiring a separate short application. Check each school's financial aid and scholarship pages directly.
2. **Your high school counseling office.** Local scholarships — from community organizations, local businesses, religious or civic groups — are often underused because they're not on the big national databases, which also means far less competition for them.
3. **Your (or a parent's) employer.** Many companies offer scholarships for employees' children; ask HR.
4. **Community and identity-based organizations** — local Rotary or Kiwanis clubs, religious organizations, cultural and identity-based nonprofits, professional associations related to your intended field.
5. **National scholarship search platforms** (e.g., the College Board's BigFuture, Scholarships.com, Fastweb) — useful for volume, but expect heavy competition and treat any "quick win" claims skeptically.

## How to Prioritize Applications

You cannot apply to every scholarship you find, so prioritize:

- **Local and specific scholarships first** — smaller applicant pools mean meaningfully better odds than a national scholarship with thousands of entrants.
- **Scholarships that reuse material you already have** — many scholarship essays overlap in theme with your personal statement or activities list; look for ones where you can adapt existing material rather than starting from scratch each time.
- **Renewable scholarships over one-time awards**, when the amounts are comparable — a $2,000/year renewable award is worth more over four years than a $5,000 one-time award.
- **Return on time invested** — a $500 scholarship requiring three essays may be worth less of your limited time than a $1,000 scholarship requiring a short form, especially as deadlines stack up during application season.

Set up a simple tracker for scholarships the same way you did for college applications (chapter 3) — amount, deadline, requirements, and status.

## Avoiding Scholarship Scams

A few reliable warning signs:

- **Any scholarship that charges a fee to apply.** Legitimate scholarships never charge you to enter.
- **"You've been selected" messages for a scholarship you never applied to** — a classic phishing pattern.
- **Guarantees of winning**, or pressure to act "within 24 hours."
- **Requests for a credit card or bank account number** as part of the "application" — there's no legitimate reason a scholarship application needs your payment information.
- **Vague sponsoring organizations** with no verifiable website, physical address, or track record of past winners.

If something looks off, search the scholarship's name alongside the word "scam" — most fraudulent ones have already been flagged publicly by other families who ran into them first. When in doubt, stick to scholarships listed by your school counselor, your target colleges, or well-established national platforms.
