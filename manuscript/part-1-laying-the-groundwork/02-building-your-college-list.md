# 2. Building Your College List

A good college list isn't the ten schools with the best name recognition — it's a list built from your own priorities, wide enough to include real options, and short enough to actually manage. This chapter walks through building one from scratch.

## Self-Assessment: Fit, Cost, Size, Location, Majors

Before you look at a single school, get honest with yourself about what you're actually looking for. Work through these five questions:

- **Fit.** Do you want a school known for a strong sense of community and school spirit, or one that's more independent and anonymous? Career-focused and pre-professional, or exploratory and liberal-artsy? Politically and socially active, or quieter?
- **Cost.** What can your family realistically afford, before aid? Have an honest conversation early — the "net price calculator" on any college's website will give you a real estimate in ten minutes, and it will save you from falling for a school you can't actually attend. See Part Four for the full picture on aid.
- **Size.** Small schools (under 5,000 undergrads) often mean small classes and close relationships with professors. Large schools (20,000+) usually mean more course and major options, bigger athletics and social scenes, and more anonymity if that's what you want.
- **Location.** Urban, suburban, or rural? Close to home or far away? Climate matters more than people expect — a New England winter is a real adjustment if you've never lived through one.
- **Majors.** If you know what you want to study, check that the program is strong and check specific requirements (some engineering or nursing programs, for example, require direct admission as a freshman). If you don't know yet, prioritize schools that make it easy to explore and switch majors.

Write your answers down. They become the filter for everything that follows.

## Research Tools and How to Use Them

- **College websites** — start here, especially the admissions and "cost of attendance" pages.
- **The Common Data Set (CDS)** — nearly every school publishes one; search "[school name] common data set" and you'll find admit rates, middle-50% test scores and GPA, and financial aid statistics, all in one standardized format. This is the single best tool for judging whether a school is a reach, target, or safety for you.
- **Net price calculators** — required by law on every college website; use them early, not after you've fallen in love with a school.
- **College search platforms** (e.g., BigFuture, Niche, Cappex) — useful for casting a wide net and filtering by size, location, and major, but treat their "match" scores as a starting point, not gospel.
- **Your school counselor** — especially useful for reading how your specific high school's applicants have historically fared at specific colleges.
- **Current students and alumni** — if you know anyone at a school on your list, ask them what a Tuesday actually feels like there. Marketing materials show you the brochure; people show you the reality.

## Narrowing a Long List to 8–12 Schools

It's normal to start with a long list — twenty or more schools you've heard good things about. Narrow it in two passes:

**First pass — cut on fit.** Remove any school that clearly fails one of your five self-assessment criteria. If you said you wanted a school under 8,000 students, take the 30,000-student state flagship off, no matter how good its reputation is.

**Second pass — build balance.** From what's left, sort schools into reach, target, and safety (using the Common Data Set numbers from your research). Aim for a final list of 8–12 schools that includes:

- 2–3 reaches
- 4–6 targets
- 2–3 safeties (that you'd genuinely be glad to attend)

Every school on the final list should pass one test: could you picture yourself happy there? If a school is only on your list because of its name, and you can't answer that honestly, cut it. A shorter list of schools you actually want is far more manageable — and far less stressful — than a long list padded with names you don't really want.
