# 1. Understanding How Admissions Works

Before you build a list or write a word of an essay, it helps to understand what's actually happening on the other side of the process — what an admissions office is trying to do, and how your application gets evaluated once it lands on someone's desk.

## Holistic Review, Explained

Most selective colleges use what's called **holistic review**: instead of adding up test scores and GPA into a formula, an admissions reader looks at your whole file — academics, activities, essays, recommendations, and context — and forms a judgment about you as a person and a prospective member of their community.

Holistic review typically happens in stages. A first reader summarizes your file and often assigns preliminary ratings (academic strength, extracurricular strength, personal qualities). Many schools then bring files to a committee, where readers advocate for applicants they found compelling. Context matters throughout: a B+ in AP Physics at a school with no other science APs offered reads differently than the same grade at a school with a dozen STEM electives. Readers are trained to evaluate you against the opportunities available to you, not against an abstract ideal applicant.

This is why two students with identical GPAs and scores can have very different outcomes — the file is read as a story, not a scorecard.

## What Colleges Actually Weigh

Ask ten admissions officers to rank what matters most and you'll get ten different answers, but most selective schools weigh some version of the following, roughly in order:

1. **Academic record** — the rigor of your course load and the grades you earned in it, usually the single heaviest factor.
2. **Standardized test scores** — where submitted or required; see chapter 4.
3. **Essays** — your voice, your reflection, and what you reveal that the rest of the file can't.
4. **Extracurricular involvement** — especially depth, initiative, and impact rather than a long list of light commitments.
5. **Letters of recommendation** — outside confirmation of who you are in a classroom or activity.
6. **Demonstrated interest, interviews, and "fit" factors** — how much the school believes you'd actually enroll if admitted, and how well you'd complement their specific community and institutional needs that year.

At the most selective schools, nearly every applicant is academically qualified — which means essays, activities, and recommendations often do the real work of differentiating one strong applicant from another.

## Reach, Target, and Safety

Every school on your list should fall into one of three categories:

- **Reach** — admission is a stretch even with a strong application; either the school is highly selective for everyone, or your stats fall below the middle 50% of admits.
- **Target (or "match")** — your academic profile lands squarely within the school's typical admitted range; you have a realistic, solid chance.
- **Safety** — your profile is comfortably above the school's typical admitted range, and you'd be happy to attend if it's where you end up.

A balanced list has schools in all three categories — not just reaches. The point of a safety isn't a backup you'll settle for; it's a school you'd be genuinely glad to attend if the reaches don't come through. Chapter 2 walks through how to sort your list this way.

## Public vs. Private

Public and private universities differ in more than just cost:

- **Public universities** are typically larger, funded partly by state tax dollars, and often give in-state residents both a tuition discount and, at many schools, an admissions edge. Many rely more heavily on GPA and test scores in a formula-driven first pass before any holistic review.
- **Private universities** are typically smaller (though not always), funded by tuition and endowment, and almost universally holistic in review. Private schools often have more institutional financial aid to offer, which can sometimes make the "sticker price" misleading — a private school can end up cheaper than a public one after aid.

Neither category is inherently better; the right mix depends on what you're looking for in cost, size, and program strength.

## Early Decision vs. Early Action vs. Regular Decision

- **Early Decision (ED)** is binding — if admitted, you commit to attend and withdraw all other applications. ED can meaningfully boost your admission odds at many schools because it signals certainty to the college, but it also removes your ability to compare financial aid offers, so it's a serious commitment, not just a strategic move.
- **Early Action (EA)** is non-binding — you apply early and hear back early, but you're free to say no and compare it against other offers. Some schools offer "Restrictive" or "Single-Choice" EA, which limits your ability to apply early elsewhere; read the fine print before you commit to any early round.
- **Regular Decision (RD)** is the standard round, usually with January deadlines and April decisions, with no binding commitment either way.

If you're confident about a first-choice school and its aid won't make or break your decision, ED can be a genuine advantage. If you need to compare financial aid packages, EA or RD keeps your options open.
