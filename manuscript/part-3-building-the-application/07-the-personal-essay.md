# 7. The Personal Essay

The personal essay is the one part of your application entirely in your own voice. It's also the part students dread most — because it feels like it has to be profound, and most 17-year-olds haven't lived a "profound" story yet. The good news: it doesn't need one. It needs to sound like you.

## Brainstorming Techniques

Don't start by trying to think of "your big story." Start by collecting small, specific material:

- **The running list from the preface.** If you started jotting down moments and details, go back to that list first — it's already full of raw material.
- **The "who knows you" test.** Ask a few people close to you — a parent, a friend, a coach — what they'd say makes you, you. Their answers often surface qualities you take for granted because you're too close to see them.
- **Specific over significant.** A small, vivid detail (the particular way your grandmother organizes her spice rack; the exact sound of the bell at your family's restaurant) is often better essay material than a large, vague event (a trip, a tournament win). Specificity is what makes an essay feel true; scale doesn't.
- **Work backward from a quality.** Pick one trait you want to demonstrate — persistence, curiosity, humor, care for others — and brainstorm three moments in your life where that trait clearly showed up, even in ordinary circumstances.

You don't need tragedy or triumph. Admissions readers read thousands of "how I overcame the big thing" essays every year; a well-told small story often stands out more.

## Working the Common App Prompts

The Common App offers seven prompts, plus an open "topic of your choice" option. The prompts are broad on purpose — they're meant to be doors into almost any story, not narrow assignments. A useful approach:

1. Don't start by picking a prompt. Start by picking your material (from brainstorming above).
2. Once you know your story, find the prompt it fits — usually more than one will work.
3. If nothing fits cleanly, use the open prompt. It exists precisely for stories that don't map onto the other six.

The prompt is a frame, not the point. Readers care about what the essay reveals about you, not how neatly it answers the literal question.

## Drafting and Revising for Voice and Authenticity

- **Write the first draft fast and messy.** Don't edit while drafting — get the whole story down, even badly, before you start refining it.
- **Read it out loud.** This is the single best test for voice. If it sounds like something you'd never actually say, revise it until it does.
- **Cut the thesaurus.** An essay full of words you wouldn't normally use doesn't sound smart — it sounds like it wasn't written by a 17-year-old, which is exactly what a reader is trying to get a sense of.
- **Show, then tell — briefly.** Ground the essay in a specific scene or detail, then step back and reflect on what it means to you. Essays that are all reflection and no scene feel abstract; essays that are all scene and no reflection feel unfinished.
- **Get feedback, but stay the author.** A teacher, parent, or counselor can catch what's unclear or where the voice slips — but if their edits start to sound like their writing instead of yours, that's a signal to pull back. Admissions readers read tens of thousands of essays a year; an essay that sounds adult-polished rather than teenager-honest is easy to spot.
- **Expect multiple drafts.** Four or five revisions is normal. The first draft finds the story; the later drafts find the voice.

The best personal essays don't try to impress — they try to be honest and specific. If a reader finishes it feeling like they know one true, particular thing about you, it's done its job.
