# 9. Letters of Recommendation

A great recommendation letter doesn't just confirm you're a good student — it tells an admissions reader something specific and vivid that the rest of your application can't show on its own. Getting one requires picking the right person and giving them what they need to write it well.

## Who to Ask

Most schools ask for one or two teacher recommendations, typically from core academic subjects (math, science, English, history, or a world language), plus a counselor letter that your school submits regardless. Some schools also accept or require a recommendation from an activity or extracurricular supervisor.

When choosing a teacher, prioritize:

- **A teacher who knows you well**, not just the teacher who gave you the highest grade. A B+ student a teacher can describe in vivid, specific detail produces a stronger letter than an A student the teacher barely remembers.
- **Recency and relevance**, generally — a junior-year teacher is often a good choice since they've seen your most recent work, though a teacher from an earlier year who knows you exceptionally well can also be a strong pick.
- **Variety across the two letters**, if you're submitting two — a teacher from a different subject area than the first paints a fuller picture than two letters saying similar things.

## How to Ask

Ask in person if you can, ideally in the spring of junior year or the very start of senior year — well before the fall deadlines when teachers get flooded with requests. A simple, direct ask works: explain that you'd value their recommendation and why you're asking them specifically (not just "because you teach my favorite class," but something concrete about what you think they'd be able to speak to).

Give them a clear, easy out. Something like, "Would you be able to write me a strong recommendation?" gives a teacher room to decline gracefully if they don't feel they know you well enough — and you want a letter from someone who said yes enthusiastically, not one who felt obligated.

## What to Give Recommenders

The single best thing you can do to help a recommender write a strong letter is to make their job easier. Provide:

- **A resume or activities list** — so they have your full context, not just what happens in their classroom.
- **A short "brag sheet"** — many schools' counselors provide a template for this; even without one, write up 2–3 specific moments or projects in that class or activity that you're proud of, and anything about your goals or interests that might help them connect your story to your application.
- **A list of your schools and deadlines**, plus how they'll be submitted (usually electronically through the Common App or Coalition App).
- **A thank-you**, and a check-in a week or two before each deadline (politely) to confirm the letter's been submitted — not to pressure them, but because teachers manage dozens of these and a gentle reminder is genuinely helpful, not rude.

The best letters are specific — an anecdote about a moment you handled a hard problem, or spoke up for a classmate, or persisted through a tough assignment. A resume and brag sheet give your recommender the raw material to write that kind of letter instead of a generic one.
