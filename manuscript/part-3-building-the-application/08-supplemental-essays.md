# 8. Supplemental Essays

If the personal essay is about you, supplemental essays are about you *and* the school — and they're often where applications quietly fall apart, because they're written last, under deadline pressure, and treated as an afterthought. Don't let yours be.

## "Why Us" Essays

The "why this school" essay is the most common supplement, and the most commonly done badly. Weak versions restate the school's marketing copy — "your beautiful campus," "world-class faculty," "strong sense of community" — language that could describe almost any college and tells the reader nothing.

A strong "why us" essay is specific and two-directional: it names concrete things about the school (a particular program, research opportunity, class, professor, tradition, or resource) *and* connects them to something concrete about you — a goal, an experience, an interest — so the essay could only have been written for this one school by this one applicant. A useful test: could you swap in a different college's name and have the essay still make sense? If yes, it's not specific enough yet.

Research each school properly before you write: read the department pages for your intended major, look for named programs or research centers, and if possible, talk to a current student or take a virtual tour. Ten extra minutes of research usually produces a noticeably better essay than ten more minutes of writing.

## Short-Answer Questions

Many schools pair a longer supplement with several very short-answer prompts — sometimes 50 words or fewer. These feel low-stakes, but they're often where a reader gets their clearest, fastest sense of your personality, since there's no room to hide behind elaborate sentence structure.

Treat short answers as an opportunity, not an afterthought:

- Be direct and concrete — a specific, vivid answer beats a vague, safe one every time at this length.
- Let some personality or humor through, where the prompt allows it — these are often the most memorable parts of a whole application.
- Don't try to cram in more "impressive" content than the space allows; a clean, well-chosen answer to a 50-word prompt reads better than an overstuffed one.

## Avoiding the Recycled-Essay Trap

With a long list of schools, it's tempting to write one "why us" essay and swap in the school name each time. Admissions readers can usually tell, and it undercuts an otherwise strong application — a generic essay signals lower genuine interest, which matters at schools that track "demonstrated interest" as part of their decision.

A better system:

1. Write one strong core essay about a theme relevant to most of your target schools (e.g., your academic interest and why you want to pursue it in college).
2. For each school, customize at least 30–40% of the essay with specific, researched details unique to that institution.
3. Read each final version once, specifically checking: does this sentence work only for this school, or could it be pasted into any application?

It's slower than a pure copy-paste approach, but supplemental essays are graded on exactly the specificity that approach destroys — the extra time is where the value is.
