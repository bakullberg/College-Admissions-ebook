# 6. Activities & Resume

The Common App activities section is small — ten entries, a short description each — but it does a lot of work in an application. This chapter is about presenting what you've actually done in a way that reads as substantial, not padded.

## Presenting Extracurriculars, Jobs, and Family Responsibilities as Strengths

Admissions readers aren't only looking for club presidencies and varsity letters. They're looking for evidence of character: initiative, responsibility, follow-through, impact on others. That evidence can come from anywhere:

- **A part-time job** shows reliability and time management — often more concretely than a club membership does. Don't undersell "worked 15 hours/week at a grocery store" — that's real commitment, and readers know it.
- **Family responsibilities** — caring for siblings, translating for parents, contributing to household income — are legitimate, valuable activities. If this is part of your life, it belongs on your list, described plainly and without apology.
- **Informal or unofficial activities** — a self-taught skill, a small business, a YouTube channel, volunteering that was never through an official program — all count. The Common App doesn't require an activity to be school-sanctioned to be worth listing.

The description matters more than the title. "Member" of a club for four years with no elaboration says less than "Ran weekly fundraising events that raised $3,000 for the local food bank" as a general member. Lead with what you did and what resulted, not just where you showed up.

## Showing Depth Over a Long List

A common mistake is trying to fill all ten activity slots to look well-rounded. Readers generally respond better to a shorter list with real depth than a long list of shallow commitments. A student who spent three years going deeper into one or two things — building skill, taking on more responsibility over time, producing something tangible — tells a clearer story than a student who joined eight clubs and attended a handful of meetings in each.

When you write your list, ask of each entry: did I grow in this? Did I take on more over time? Did something come out of it — a result, a skill, a change in someone else's experience? If the honest answer is no, that entry can be short or cut; better to leave a slot empty than to pad it with something that doesn't say anything about you.

A useful frame for your resume as a whole: pick one or two things you want to be known for — the thing a reader would say about you if asked to summarize your file in one sentence — and make sure your activities section makes that case clearly, backed by the depth to prove it's real.
