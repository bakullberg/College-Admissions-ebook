# 18. Getting Ready for Fall

Committing to a school on May 1 isn't the end of the process — it's the start of a different, more logistical one. The months between your deposit and move-in day are full of forms and small deadlines that are easy to lose track of after the emotional weight of decisions has lifted.

## Housing Forms

Most schools require a separate housing application, often with its own deadline in late spring or early summer, sometimes on a first-come, first-served basis for room and roommate preferences. It typically asks about:

- Roommate preferences (or a request to be matched, often via a lifestyle questionnaire)
- Dorm or residence hall preferences, if the school offers a choice
- Special accommodations, medical or otherwise, that need to be flagged early

Submit this as early as your school allows — later submissions often mean fewer choices in building, room type, and roommate matching.

## Orientation

Nearly every school requires an orientation program before or at the start of the fall semester, sometimes with a summer session as well. Orientation typically covers academic advising and initial course registration, so it's not optional or purely social — missing it can mean registering for classes late, after better sections and popular courses have filled. Register for your orientation date as soon as options open.

## AP Credit

If you took AP (or IB, or dual enrollment) courses, check your future school's specific credit policy — it varies enormously, even for the same exam score, from one college to the next. Some schools grant course credit, some grant only placement into a higher-level course without credit, and some grant nothing for scores below a 4 or 5. Have your official AP score reports sent directly from the College Board to your school, and confirm with your academic advisor (often during orientation) exactly how your scores will apply before you register for fall classes.

## The Gap Between High School Graduation and Move-In Day

This stretch — typically ten to fourteen weeks — has its own checklist worth starting early rather than all at once in August:

- **Final transcript** — your high school needs to send your official final transcript to your enrolling college; confirm with your counselor that this is scheduled, since it usually isn't automatic.
- **Health requirements** — immunization records, a physical exam, and sometimes specific forms required before you can move into housing; deadlines for these are often earlier than people expect.
- **Financial logistics** — confirm your final aid package, set up any required tuition payment plan, and confirm your deposit and first-semester bill are settled before the payment deadline.
- **Connecting with your roommate** — most schools share contact information before move-in; a conversation beforehand about who's bringing what (mini fridge, TV) and basic living habits (sleep schedule, guests) heads off a lot of awkward first-week friction.
- **Packing and logistics** — check your school's specific room dimensions and any prohibited-items list before buying anything for the room.
- **Emotionally, for you and your family** — this is a real transition, not just a logistical one. It's normal for excitement and anxiety to coexist right up through move-in day, for both you and the people who raised you.

By the time you arrive on campus, the application process that started with a spreadsheet in the fall of senior year has become a fully separate life. That's the whole point of everything in this book — not admission for its own sake, but arriving somewhere you're actually ready, and genuinely glad, to begin.
