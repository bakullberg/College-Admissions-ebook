# 16. Handling Decisions

Decisions arrive in three flavors — accept, deny, and waitlist — and each has its own next steps.

## Accept

Congratulations — and don't rush. An acceptance isn't a decision to enroll, it's an invitation to consider one. Log the offer in your tracker (chapter 3), note the financial aid award (chapter 14), and set it aside until you've heard from every school on your list. Chapter 17 covers how to make the final call once all your offers are in.

## Deny

A denial is disappointing, and it's fair to feel that disappointment. But as the preface and chapter 15 both note: a "no" reflects fit and circumstance in that specific applicant pool that year — an unknowable mix of institutional priorities, geography, and how your particular file compared to everyone else's that cycle — not a verdict on your ability or your worth. Give yourself a short window to feel it, then turn your attention back to the schools that did say yes. Most students end up genuinely happy at a school that wasn't their original first choice; a denial closes one door, not the whole hallway.

There is generally no productive path to appeal a denial at most schools — admissions decisions are final, and appeals rarely succeed outside of exceptional circumstances (like a serious clerical error). Energy spent trying to reverse a denial is usually better spent on the schools that admitted you.

## Waitlist

Being waitlisted means the school found you admissible but couldn't offer a spot yet — they're holding your file in case space opens up after enrolled students commit or decline (typically starting in May).

If you want to stay on the waitlist:

- **Confirm your spot** through whatever mechanism the school specifies (usually a simple online form).
- **Send a letter of continued interest**, reaffirming that you'd enroll if admitted, and — if genuinely true — stating that the school is your first choice. Schools use waitlists partly to protect their yield rate, so sincere, specific enthusiasm matters here.
- **Add any real updates** since you applied: a new grade, award, or accomplishment. Don't manufacture material that isn't there — a short, honest letter beats a padded one.
- **Commit to another school by May 1 regardless.** A waitlist offer can come as late as summer, sometimes very close to the start of the semester — you cannot afford to wait on an uncertain outcome without a confirmed, deposited plan already in place. If a waitlist spot opens later, you can typically still switch, often forfeiting your prior deposit, but you are never left without an enrolled option.

Waitlist admission rates vary enormously by school and year — some years, some schools admit hundreds off the waitlist; other years, none at all. Treat it as a genuine possibility worth pursuing with a short, sincere letter, but not as a plan to count on.

## Appealing a Financial Aid Offer

Covered in full in chapter 14 — but the short version: if your aid offer doesn't reflect your actual financial need, or a peer school gave you a meaningfully better offer, it's appropriate to ask the financial aid office (not admissions) for a review. This is a normal, expected part of the process, not an unusual or adversarial move.
