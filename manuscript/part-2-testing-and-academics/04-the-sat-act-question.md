# 4. The SAT/ACT Question

Testing used to be simple: everyone took the SAT or ACT, and everyone submitted a score. It isn't simple anymore. This chapter walks through deciding whether to test, how to prepare without spending a fortune, and how retesting and superscoring actually work.

## The Test-Optional Landscape

Most colleges today are **test-optional**, meaning you choose whether to submit scores, and an application without them is reviewed in full without penalty. A smaller number are **test-blind**, meaning they don't consider scores at all even if submitted. And a growing number — particularly among highly selective and public flagship universities — have reinstated **test-required** policies, so always check each individual school's current policy; it can change year to year.

Where a school is test-optional, the honest way to decide is to compare your score against that school's middle-50% range for admitted students (available on the Common Data Set — see chapter 2). As a rule of thumb:

- If your score falls **at or above** the school's 50th percentile, submit it — it strengthens your application.
- If your score falls **below the 25th percentile**, don't submit it — it's more likely to hurt you than help you, and the rest of your application will be read without it.
- If your score falls **in between**, it's a judgment call — factor in whether your GPA and course rigor are otherwise strong, and whether the score reflects your ability better than a bad testing day might suggest.

Test-optional does not mean test scores stopped mattering everywhere — it means the decision to submit is now in your hands, and it should be made deliberately, school by school, not as a blanket policy for your whole list.

## Prep Strategies That Don't Require a $2,000 Tutor

Expensive tutoring can help, but it isn't the only path to a strong score. In order of cost-effectiveness:

1. **Official free practice.** Khan Academy's SAT prep (built in partnership with the College Board) and the ACT's own free resources are genuinely strong and cost nothing.
2. **Official practice tests, taken under real timing.** A handful of full, timed practice tests will teach you more about pacing than weeks of untimed drilling.
3. **A prep book, used consistently.** One well-reviewed book, worked through cover to cover over 6–8 weeks, beats a stack of five books skimmed once.
4. **Small-group or online classes.** A middle-cost option if self-study isn't working for you — often a fraction of the price of private tutoring.
5. **Private tutoring**, if the budget allows and you have a specific, narrow weakness (like timing on the math section) a tutor can target directly.

Whatever the method, consistency over months beats cramming — plan for a study window of at least two to three months before your first real test date.

## Superscoring and When to Retest

**Superscoring** means a college takes your highest section scores across multiple test dates and combines them into a new composite — so a strong Math score from March and a strong Reading score from June can combine into a better composite than either single sitting produced. Most (though not all) schools that accept the SAT or ACT superscore it; check each school's policy, since it affects how much retesting is worth your time.

A reasonable retest plan:

- Take your first official test in the fall of junior year, so you have a baseline with time to react.
- If your score is below your target range, prepare and retest once, typically in the spring of junior year or fall of senior year.
- Two sittings are usually enough. A third rarely produces a meaningful jump unless something specific changed in your prep, and the returns on additional attempts drop fast.

Most importantly: decide your testing plan early enough that it doesn't collide with application deadlines. A test taken in November of senior year can still make it to schools with January deadlines — but leave no room for a bad day with no makeup date available.
