# 5. Your Senior Year Course Load

It's tempting to think the work is done once applications are submitted. It isn't — your senior fall course load is part of what colleges evaluate, and your senior spring grades can still affect your admission after you've been accepted.

## Why Senior Grades Still Matter After You've Applied

Colleges see your transcript in stages. When you apply, they see grades through junior year (and often a "first quarter" or "first semester" senior report). After decisions go out, most colleges require a **final transcript** showing your full senior year before you enroll. A significant drop in your grades — commonly called "senioritis" — can, in rare but real cases, result in a rescinded offer of admission. More commonly, a steep decline just looks bad if your school ever needs to explain a discrepancy, and it undercuts the momentum your application built.

The practical takeaway isn't "stress through senior spring the way you did junior year" — it's "don't check out completely." Finish the classes you signed up for with reasonable effort, even after decisions arrive.

## Choosing AP, IB, or Dual Enrollment

Admissions readers evaluate your course load in the context of what your school offers — the goal is to show you challenged yourself within your options, not to take every AP class in the catalog.

- **AP (Advanced Placement)** courses are the most widely available and widely recognized rigor signal; a solid spread of APs in your strongest subjects, plus your intended major if you have one, reads well.
- **IB (International Baccalaureate)** is a full-program commitment at schools that offer it, and it's well understood and respected by admissions offices, though it requires signing on earlier (often by sophomore year) than picking individual AP classes.
- **Dual enrollment** (community college or local university courses, often for both high school and college credit) is a strong option where APs aren't available in a subject, or where a student has exhausted their school's AP offerings in an area of interest.

Choose rigor in the subjects that matter most to your intended major or genuine interests, rather than spreading yourself thin across every available option. A strong B in an AP class you chose deliberately is a better signal than an A in a course that didn't challenge you at all.

## Balancing Rigor Against Burnout

The instinct to maximize your course load — five or six APs, a leadership role in three clubs, a part-time job, and a varsity sport — often backfires. Admissions readers can tell the difference between a demanding schedule handled well and one that's clearly stretched a student too thin, visible in dropping grades or a burned-out, hollow-sounding essay.

A more sustainable approach:

- Choose 2–4 rigorous courses that align with real interest or your intended major, not the maximum your school allows.
- Protect enough time to actually do the application work well — essays and recommendations require real hours, and a schedule with no slack for them shows in the final product.
- Remember that senior year is also the last year of high school. Some part of it should be about your life outside the application, not just the application.

The goal of your course load is to demonstrate genuine academic engagement — not to win an arms race against other applicants you'll never meet.
