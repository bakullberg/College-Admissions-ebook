    Quiz: Finding Your Fit¶

    Test your understanding of personal priorities, the three kinds of college fit, cost, and building a balanced list with these review questions.

    1. For a final list of eight to twelve colleges, what mix does the chapter give as a common guideline?¶

    1. 5–6 reach schools, 3–4 target schools, and 1 safety school
    2. 3–4 reach schools, 3–4 target schools, and 3–4 safety schools
    3. 2–3 reach schools, 4–6 target schools, and 2–3 safety schools
    4. 1 reach school, 4–6 target schools, and 5–6 safety schools
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's guideline for a balanced college list is 2–3 reaches, 4–6 targets, and 2–3 safeties. Targets form the core because they are your most likely choices in April. A list heavy with reaches risks leaving you with no good options, while a list with almost no reaches gives up a shot at a stretch school. The chapter stresses that these numbers are not rules, but the principle is that at least one affordable college you'd love should be very likely to admit you.

    2. In the chapter, what is a dealbreaker?¶

    1. A college trait that appears on every school's marketing materials
    2. A condition a college must meet or you won't consider it at all
    3. A reason a college might deny your application in the reading round
    4. A priority you are willing to give up in exchange for a lower price
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A dealbreaker is a condition a college must meet or you won't consider it. Examples in the chapter include a student who must live at home to care for a sibling and a family that cannot pay more than a set amount. The chapter advises naming dealbreakers first because they remove schools from your list outright. A priority you would give up is a trade-off, the opposite of a dealbreaker.

    3. What tool is every college required by law to post on its website to estimate what a family will actually pay?¶

    1. A school profile
    2. A cost of attendance worksheet
    3. A net price calculator
    4. A Common Data Set
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Every college is required by law to post a net price calculator, which estimates your net price from your family's financial information. The chapter recommends using it for every college on your list early in your search, before you get attached. The cost of attendance is the price before aid, not an estimate of what you will pay. A school profile describes a high school, and the Common Data Set reports admissions data.

    Concept Tested: Net Price Calculator

    See: Finding Your Fit

    4. According to the chapter, when do most four-year colleges require students to declare a major?¶

    1. On the application, before admission
    2. By the end of the first semester
    3. Before the first semester of classes begins
    4. By the end of the second year
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter says most four-year colleges don't require you to declare a major until the end of your second year, which is why applying undeclared is common and fully accepted. Choosing a major on the application is required only for restricted or capped programs, such as some engineering or nursing programs. That exception is why an undeclared student who might want a capped major should check how those programs admit students before applying.

    5. What is the main difference between a personal priority and a simple preference?¶

    1. Priorities have to be ranked, so you know which ones you'd give up to keep others
    2. Priorities apply only to safety schools, while preferences apply to reaches
    3. Priorities are set by parents, while preferences are set by students
    4. Priorities only cover academic needs, while preferences cover social life
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says priorities differ from preferences in one important way: you have to rank them. Almost everyone would like a beautiful campus, great food, and a low price, but no college has everything, so the real question is which ones you'd give up to keep the others. Priorities can be academic, social, financial, or practical, so they are not limited to academics. They belong to the student and apply to every school on the list.

    See: Finding Your Fit

    6. The chapter compares academic, social, and financial fit to the legs of a stool. What point does this comparison make?¶

    1. A college needs to match only two of the three to be a good choice
    2. Financial fit matters only after academic and social fit are settled
    3. Strong fit in one dimension doesn't make up for a failure in another
    4. Social fit is the most important leg because it affects whether students stay
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A college has to work on all three dimensions of fit. A perfect academic and social match still falls over if the cost is out of reach, and an affordable school with a great program still isn't a fit if you'd be miserable living there. That is why two of three is not enough. The chapter treats the three legs as equally necessary rather than ranking one above the others or saving cost for last.

    7. Why are indirect costs easy for families to overlook?¶

    1. They are usually covered in full by federal grants
    2. They don't appear on the college's bill, even though you still pay them
    3. They are paid only by out-of-state students
    4. They are listed only after a student enrolls
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Indirect costs are expenses in the cost of attendance that the college doesn't bill you for: books and supplies, transportation, and personal expenses. Because they don't show up on the bill, they're easy to forget, but students still pay them out of pocket during the year. Every student has indirect costs, not only out-of-state students, though distance can raise them. They are part of the published cost of attendance, so they are listed before enrollment.

    8. According to the chapter, which is a harm caused by the no single right school myth?¶

    1. It encourages students to choose majors they aren't interested in
    2. It can push families into Early Decision before they've compared costs
    3. It makes students skip the net price calculator for private colleges
    4. It leads students to apply to too many safety schools
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The no single right school myth is the belief that one "dream school" exists for each student. The chapter says it makes students pour their hopes into one college, often a highly selective one, turns every other acceptance into a consolation prize, and can push families into Early Decision commitments before comparing costs. The truth is that most students could thrive at many colleges. The chapter does not link the myth to applying to too many safeties.

    9. A family can pay about $30,000 a year. College X has a sticker price of $78,000 and an estimated net price of $27,000. College Y has a sticker price of $45,000 and an estimated net price of $38,000. Which conclusion fits the chapter's guidance?¶

    1. College Y fits the budget better because its sticker price is lower
    2. Both colleges fit equally, because net price calculators are only estimates
    3. Neither college fits, because both sticker prices are above $30,000
    4. College X fits the budget, because net price is what the family would actually pay
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Sticker price is the published cost before aid, while net price is sticker price minus grants and scholarships. College X's net price of $27,000 is within the family's $30,000, and College Y's $38,000 is not. Judging by sticker price would cut the wrong school, just as Priya almost cut Hillcrest College. The chapter says calculators give estimates, not promises, but they are accurate enough to keep you from making that mistake.

    Concept Tested: Financial Fit

    See: Finding Your Fit

    10. A college admits 40% of applicants and has a middle 50% SAT range of 1250–1400. A student's SAT is 1230, just below the range, but their GPA is near the top of the college's GPA range. How should the student classify this college?¶

    1. As a target leaning toward reach, placed in the more cautious category
    2. As a target leaning toward safety, because the GPA outweighs the test score
    3. As a clear reach, because any number below the range rules out a target
    4. As a safety, because the admission rate is above 30%
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. This mirrors the chapter's example of a student whose SAT falls just below a range while the GPA sits near the top. Most counselors would call it a target leaning toward reach, with the rest of the application deciding the outcome. The chapter's rule is that when a college falls between categories, you place it in the more cautious one. It isn't a safety, since that requires numbers above the 75th percentile, and the strong GPA keeps it from being a clear reach.
