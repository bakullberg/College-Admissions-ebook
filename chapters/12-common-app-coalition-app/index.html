    Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter walks section by section through the Common App and Coalition App, from profile to supplements, plus fees and waivers, submission, and sending transcripts and scores. This is where all the earlier work comes together in the application itself. Students finish able to complete, preview, and submit an application with transcripts and scores sent correctly.

    Where Everything Comes Together¶

    Every earlier chapter built a piece of your application: your list, your test plan, your activities, your essays, your recommenders. This chapter is where those pieces go into the form itself. Most of the Common App is straightforward. But a few spots trip up nearly every applicant, such as a misspelled name, a missing dual-enrollment course, a self-reported grade that doesn't match the transcript, or a recommender never assigned to a college. Mistakes like these can delay or derail an application that's otherwise strong.

    This chapter walks through the application in the order you'll meet it. It starts with the two shared platforms, the Common App and the Coalition App, and the Common App's dashboard, where you add colleges. Then it goes section by section through the core application: profile, family, education, testing, activities, and writing, plus the courses-and-grades section some colleges require. Next come each college's own questions and writing supplements, and assigning recommenders. The chapter ends with fees and waivers, previewing and submitting, confirming submission, and sending official transcripts and test scores.

    Platforms update their layouts every year, so section names and the order of questions may differ slightly from what's described here. The logic stays the same.

    The Platforms¶

    The Common App Dashboard¶

    The Common App dashboard is the home screen of your Common App account. It lists every college you've added, along with each college's deadlines and the status of each part of your application for that college. Chapter 5 covered creating the account. The dashboard is where you'll manage it.

    The Common App is organized into a few main areas, usually shown as tabs:

    • Dashboard: your colleges, deadlines, and a checklist of what's complete and what's missing for each.
    • My Colleges: each college's own questions, writing supplements, recommenders, and submission steps.
    • Common App: the core sections that go to every college, including profile, family, education, testing, activities, and writing.
    • College Search: where you find colleges and add them to your list.

    The dashboard's job is to show you, at a glance, what's left. Each college has a status for every required component, such as the core application, the college's questions, the writing supplement, recommendations, and payment. A green check means complete. Anything else needs your attention. Look at the dashboard each time you log in, and compare it with your own tracker from Chapter 5. When the two disagree, the dashboard is usually showing you something you've missed.

    Worked Example: Reading the Dashboard¶

    In mid-October, Priya's dashboard shows her eight Common App colleges. (Her state flagship isn't there, since it uses its own application.) For College C, her Early Action target, every item has a green check. For Hillcrest College, her Regular Decision target, she sees "Writing supplement: in progress" and "Recommenders: not invited."

    The first is expected, because her Why Hillcrest essay isn't finished yet. The second is a surprise. She'd invited her teachers through the Common App in September, but she'd added Hillcrest after assigning them, so they were never assigned to Hillcrest. She fixes it in My Colleges in two minutes. Without the dashboard, she'd have learned about it in January from a missing-documents email.

    Adding Colleges¶

    Adding colleges means putting each college from your final list into your Common App account, so its questions, supplements, and deadlines appear in your application. You add colleges through the College Search tab, up to 20, as Chapter 5 noted. When you add a college, you'll choose your application round (such as Early Action or Regular Decision) and your term of entry, and some colleges ask you to choose a program or campus right away.

    Adding a college early has two benefits. First, you see that college's requirements: its deadlines, its member questions, its writing supplements, and how many recommendations it requires. That's how you build the supplement plan from Chapter 10. Second, it lets you assign recommenders to that college, which you'll need to do for every college on your list. You can remove a college you decide not to apply to, though once you've submitted an application, it stays on your record. Double-check the application round for each college as you add it. Choosing Early Decision by mistake, or missing an Early Action option, is an easy error that's hard to undo once submitted.

    Worked Example: Choosing the Round¶

    Marcus adds ten colleges to his Common App. For eight of them, the round is simple: Regular Decision, the only round he's using. For Lakeshore University, the menu offers Early Decision I, Early Decision II, Early Action, and Regular Decision. He has no plans to commit before comparing aid offers, which Chapter 1 explained Early Decision would require, so he selects Early Action. He then checks the dashboard, which now shows Lakeshore's November 1 deadline in place of the January date. He updates his tracker to match.

    The Coalition App¶

    The Coalition App is a second shared application platform, now run on the Scoir platform, that a smaller group of colleges accepts. Some colleges accept both the Common App and the Coalition App, and a few accept only the Coalition App alongside their own institutional application. Like the Common App, it has core sections that go to every college, plus college-specific questions and essays, and a set of essay prompts for its personal statement.

    For most students, the Common App covers most or all of their list, and there's no reason to complete both platforms for the same college. Colleges that accept both treat them equally. Use the Coalition App when a college on your list accepts it but not the Common App, or when you're already using it for other colleges. Check each college's admissions page for the platforms it accepts, and record your choice in your tracker. Everything this chapter says about accuracy, previewing, and confirming submission applies equally to the Coalition App.

    Worked Example: Deciding Between Platforms¶

    Ana's list includes one college that accepts the Common App, the Coalition App, and its own application. The other seven accept the Common App. Since her Common App is already complete for seven colleges, adding the eighth there takes minutes, while the Coalition App would mean entering her profile, activities, and essay all over again. She uses the Common App. If one of her colleges had accepted only the Coalition App, she would have used it for that one college, copying her activities and essay from her saved documents.

    The Core Common App Sections¶

    The core sections of the Common App are filled out once and sent to every college. The table below previews them in the order they usually appear. Each gets its own short section after the table.

    Section What it covers What to watch for
    Profile Your name, address, contact information, demographics, citizenship, and fee waiver eligibility Your legal name exactly as on school records
    Family Household, parents' or guardians' education and jobs, siblings Complete parent education; it affects first-generation status
    Education Your high school, other schools, college courses, grades, current courses, honors Every school and college course you've taken
    Testing Self-reported SAT, ACT, AP, IB, and other scores Report only where you've decided to submit
    Activities Up to 10 activities The 150-character descriptions (Chapter 8)
    Writing Personal essay and optional additional information Paste and preview carefully
    Courses and Grades A full self-reported transcript, for colleges that require it Enter exactly what's on your transcript

    The Profile Section¶

    The profile section is the first section of the Common App, covering your personal information: legal name and any preferred name, birthdate, address, phone, email, citizenship, language, and optional demographic questions. It also includes fee waiver questions, covered later in this chapter. Everything you enter here is copied into every application you send, so a typo here becomes a typo everywhere.

    The most important detail is your legal name. It must match your official documents, including your transcript, test registrations, and eventually your financial aid forms, exactly. Colleges match incoming documents to your file partly by name. A mismatch, such as "Priya S. Raman" on the Common App and "Priya Sunita Raman" on the transcript, can leave documents unmatched and your file "incomplete." You can enter a preferred name separately. Use the email address from Chapter 5, and double-check your phone number and address. Demographic questions are optional, so answer what you're comfortable answering.

    Worked Example: The Unmatched Transcript¶

    Casey's legal name is "Casey Lin Nguyen-Park," but Casey filled out the profile as "Casey Park" from habit. Casey's high school sent the transcript under the legal name. Three weeks later, one college's portal still showed "Transcript: not received," even though the counselor had sent it. The college's operations staff, as Chapter 1 described, had received a transcript for a "Casey Nguyen-Park" that didn't match any applicant.

    A short email to admissions fixed it. But Casey updated the profile to the legal name immediately, before submitting to the remaining colleges, and added "Casey" as the preferred name.

    The Family Section¶

    The family section asks about your household: who you live with, your parents' or guardians' names, education, and occupations, and your siblings. It may seem routine, but colleges use it in several ways. It's how they learn whether you're a first-generation student (Chapter 2), whether you have legacy connections at their college, and something about your family's circumstances.

    Answer completely and accurately. For each parent or guardian, report the highest level of education they actually completed, such as high school, some college, an associate degree, or a bachelor's degree, and list any colleges they attended. Don't decide for yourself whether you "count" as first-generation. Each college applies its own definition to your answers. If your family situation doesn't fit the form neatly, for example a guardian who isn't a parent, a parent you have no contact with, or a blended family, the form usually allows for it. You can also add a brief explanation in the additional information section.

    Worked Example: Reporting Parent Education¶

    Ana enters her mother's education as "Associate degree" and lists the community college she attended. She enters her father's education as "High school." She doesn't add any note about being first-generation. Her colleges will see from these answers that neither parent completed a bachelor's degree. In her counselor meeting, Ana confirmed that this is what most of her colleges use to identify first-generation applicants, and that her counselor will also mention it in the counselor letter.

    The Education Section¶

    The education section covers your schooling: your current high school (identified by its CEEB code, a six-digit code your counselor can confirm), any other high schools you've attended, any college courses you've taken, your grades and GPA, your current-year courses, your honors, and your future plans. This is also where you connect your counselor to your application, as Chapter 5 described.

    Two rules matter most. First, list everything. That includes every high school you've attended, even briefly, and every college course you've taken, including dual enrollment and summer programs at a college. A college that later finds an unlisted course on a college transcript may treat it as an omission. Second, enter your grade information exactly as your school reports it. That means your GPA, its scale, whether it's weighted, and your class rank, if your school reports one. Your school profile will show the same information, and the two should match.

    The current courses list matters too. It's how colleges see your senior schedule, which Chapter 7 explained they read closely. Enter every course you're taking this year with its full name and level, such as "AP Calculus AB," not "Calc."

    Worked Example: The Dual-Enrollment Courses¶

    Marcus fills out the Education section and almost skips "Colleges and universities." Then he remembers his two community college courses from Chapter 7. He adds the community college, the dates, and both courses, Introduction to Programming and Discrete Mathematics. He also checks that his counselor knows to send the community college transcript separately, and adds a line to his tracker. Leaving them off would have hidden two of his strongest rigor signals. It would also have created a discrepancy when the college transcript arrived later.

    The Testing Section¶

    The testing section is where you report your scores on the SAT, ACT, AP exams, IB exams, and English proficiency tests such as the TOEFL, if you choose to. The scores you enter here are self-reported scores, covered next. Because the section goes to every college, but your submission decision differs by college (Chapter 6), most applications also ask, in each college's own questions, whether you want that college to consider your scores. Use that question to carry out your college-by-college decision.

    Report scores exactly as they appear on your official score reports, including section scores and test dates. If you're superscoring, enter each test date's section scores as the form asks, and let colleges that superscore combine them. Report AP and IB scores selectively, as Chapter 7 suggested. Strong ones help, and leaving off weaker ones is usually fine.

    Self-Reported Scores¶

    Self-reported scores are test scores you enter yourself on an application, rather than having them sent officially by the College Board or ACT. Many colleges accept them for admission review and require an official score report (Chapter 6) only if you enroll. That saves you the cost of sending official reports to every college.

    Self-reporting comes with one firm rule: accuracy. If you're admitted and enroll, the college will compare your official report with what you reported, and a discrepancy can lead to a rescinded offer. Enter scores straight from your College Board or ACT account, not from memory.

    Worked Example: Following Each College's Rule¶

    Priya, from Chapter 6, enters both of her SAT test dates in the Testing section: June (680 Reading and Writing, 600 Math) and October (660 and 650). Then she uses each college's own testing question to carry out her decisions:

    • College A (test-required): she answers "Yes, consider my scores" and sends official reports from her College Board account.
    • College C (test-optional, superscores): "Yes." She self-reports only for now.
    • College B (test-optional): "No, do not consider my scores." College B will review her application without them.

    One Testing section, filled out once, supports three different decisions, because the per-college question controls what each college sees.

    The Activities Section¶

    The activities section is where you list up to 10 extracurricular activities in the format Chapter 8 described: activity type, position, organization, a 150-character description, grade levels, timing, and hours. By this point, you've already done the hard part, which is writing and ordering your entries. Your job here is to transfer them accurately.

    Copy your entries from your activities resume into the form, then check three things. First, fit: the form counts characters strictly, and some symbols count differently than you expect, so check that nothing is cut off. Second, order: you can rearrange entries, so make sure the order matches what you decided in Chapter 8. Third, consistency: your hours and grade levels should match what your recommenders and essays say. A reader who sees "4 years" in your activity entry and "since junior year" in your essay will notice.

    Worked Example: The Truncated Description¶

    Priya pastes her literary magazine description into the activities form: "Lead staff of 8; started first online issue; read 200+ submissions/yr; edit and lay out 2 print issues/yr." That's 106 characters in her document. The form shows the full text, but in the preview, the "+" displays oddly. She changes "200+" to "over 200," which reads clearly at the same length. The issue is small, but this is exactly what the preview step later in this chapter is designed to catch.

    The Writing Section¶

    The writing section holds your personal essay, from Chapter 9, and an optional additional information box. You'll choose your prompt and paste your essay into a text box with a 650-word limit.

    Paste carefully. Formatting such as italics, bold, and special characters may not carry over from a word processor. Paragraph breaks sometimes disappear or double. After pasting, read the essay in the box itself and in the application preview, and fix anything that changed. Check the word count in the box, which is the one colleges see. Keep the final version in your essay drafts folder.

    You may have heard that the Common App asks about disciplinary history. It used to. The Common App dropped its criminal history question in 2018 and its school discipline question starting with the 2021–22 application, but individual colleges can still ask either one in their own member questions. If a college asks, answer honestly. A brief explanation that shows what you learned is far better than an omission discovered later.

    The Additional Information Section¶

    The additional information section is an optional space, up to 650 words, within the Writing section, for context that the rest of your application doesn't explain. It's not a second personal essay. Use it only when you have something important to clarify, such as:

    • A gap in your schooling, or a semester with unusual grades.
    • A family circumstance, such as caring for a family member, a parent's illness or job loss, or homelessness.
    • A health issue or disability that affected your record, if you choose to share it.
    • A school situation, such as a change in grading policy, a school closure, or a course you couldn't take because it wasn't offered.
    • A brief clarification, such as an activity that needed more than 150 characters to make sense.

    Write it plainly and briefly, in factual sentences rather than essay style. A few sentences is often enough. An empty additional information section is completely normal and not a red flag. Filling it with more accomplishments, or a second essay, doesn't help and can dilute the rest of your file.

    Worked Example: A Brief, Factual Note¶

    Jordan's sophomore spring grades dropped from A's and B's to C's, then recovered by junior year. The reason was a family move mid-semester, which meant switching to a school with a different curriculum. Jordan writes three sentences in the additional information section:

    "In March of my sophomore year, my family moved from Ohio to Iowa for my mother's job. I finished the semester at a new school with a different math and science sequence, which explains my lower grades that spring. My grades returned to A's and B's the following fall."

    It's factual, brief, and explains a pattern a reader would otherwise wonder about. Jordan doesn't need to say more.

    Self-Reported Academic Records¶

    A self-reported academic record is a transcript you enter yourself, listing every high school course and grade, which some colleges require in place of, or in addition to, an official transcript at the time you apply. Colleges use self-reported records because they're faster to review and easier to compare than official transcripts in many different formats. Some colleges collect them through the Common App's Courses and Grades section. Others use a separate system, which they'll link to after you apply. If you're admitted, the college checks your self-report against your official transcript, so the two must match. Check each college's requirements, and record which ones require a self-report in your tracker.

    The Courses and Grades Section¶

    The courses and grades section is the Common App's version of a self-reported academic record. It appears in your application only if at least one of your colleges requires it. You enter every course you took from 9th grade on, with its level (such as honors or AP), credits, and the grade for each term, exactly as your transcript reports them.

    This section is tedious, often an hour or two of careful typing, and accuracy matters more than speed. Do it with your unofficial transcript (Chapter 5) open next to you, not from memory. Enter courses with the names and grades on your transcript, even if you think a grade was unfair or a course name is odd. Don't round grades, convert them, or calculate your own GPA here unless the form asks you to. Discrepancies between your self-report and your official transcript can raise questions, and in serious cases can lead to a rescinded offer.

    Worked Example: Checking Against the Transcript¶

    Marcus finishes his Courses and Grades section, then checks every entry against his unofficial transcript, line by line. He finds two errors. He'd entered his sophomore chemistry grade as B+, but his transcript says B. And he'd listed "Intro to Computer Science" in 9th grade, but the transcript calls it "Computer Applications I." He fixes both. Neither would have changed his admission decision. But if they'd been discovered after admission, both would have looked like attempts to make his record look better than it was.

    Each College's Section¶

    Member Questions¶

    Member questions are the questions each college adds to the Common App for its own applicants, beyond the core sections. They appear under each college in the My Colleges tab. They cover things like your intended major and program, your interest in financial aid or specific scholarships, whether you want your test scores considered, how you heard about the college, family connections to the college, residency, and sometimes short-answer questions.

    Answer them as carefully as the core sections, because they often control important things: which program you're applying to, whether you're considered for merit scholarships, and whether your scores are seen. Some questions can't be changed after submission, especially major and program choices at colleges with capped majors (Chapter 3). Read each one, and if you're unsure what a question means, check the college's admissions website or email the admissions office before submitting.

    Worked Example: The Question That Mattered¶

    For one public university, Marcus's member questions ask him to choose a first-choice major and an optional second-choice major. The university's CS program is capped, so in Chapter 3 he decided it was a reach for him. If he lists only CS and isn't admitted to the program, the university may deny him entirely. If he lists a second choice, such as Mathematics, the university can admit him to the second program instead.

    He lists Computer Science first and Mathematics second, after confirming on the university's website how second choices are handled. One member question made the difference between a possible denial and a possible admission in a related field.

    Writing Supplements¶

    Writing supplements are the supplemental essays and short-answer questions each college adds to its section of the Common App, which you planned and wrote in Chapter 10. They appear under each college in My Colleges.

    Paste each supplement from your drafts folder, and check three things before moving on: that you're answering the right prompt (some colleges offer a choice), that you're within the word limit shown in the box, and that every college name and detail is correct. That last check is the recycled-essay trap from Chapter 10. It's worth one more careful read for every college, especially when you've adapted essays across colleges. Work through supplements one college at a time, so you stay in the right frame of mind for each college's details.

    Assigning Recommenders¶

    Assigning recommenders is the step of inviting your teachers and counselor through the Common App and connecting them to each college that needs their letters. It happens in My Colleges, under each college's recommenders section, after you've completed the FERPA release from Chapter 11. You invite each recommender once by email, then assign them to each college individually. Recommenders you invite aren't automatically assigned to colleges you add later, which is what happened to Priya with Hillcrest. Check every college's recommender status on the dashboard after adding a new college, and don't invite teachers until you've asked them in person, as Chapter 11 described.

    Fees and Waivers¶

    Application Fees¶

    An application fee is the charge a college sets for processing your application, paid when you submit. Fees typically range from nothing to around $90, and some colleges charge nothing at all. On the Common App, you pay each college's fee separately when you submit to that college. Across a list of eight to twelve colleges, fees can add up to several hundred dollars, so include them in your family's budget conversation from Chapter 3. If cost is a concern, fee waivers can help.

    Fee Waivers¶

    A fee waiver is a request to have an application fee waived because paying it would be a financial hardship. On the Common App, you answer fee waiver eligibility questions in the profile section. Students who meet the criteria, such as qualifying for free or reduced-price school lunch, receiving certain public benefits, or having a household income within the platform's guidelines, can have fees waived at member colleges, and your counselor may be asked to confirm eligibility. Students who received an SAT or ACT fee waiver can also use them for college application fees at many colleges. And many colleges waive fees for first-generation students, for students who visit, or during certain periods. Check each college's website and ask your counselor. Fee waivers are confidential, and using one has no effect on your admission.

    Previewing and Submitting¶

    Application Preview¶

    The application preview is the PDF of your completed application, exactly as a college will see it, which the Common App generates before you submit. Preview your application for every college before submitting, even if you've already submitted to others. Each college's version includes its own questions and supplements, and formatting can shift between the entry form and the PDF. Line breaks, special characters, and essay paragraphs sometimes display differently.

    Read the preview slowly, top to bottom. Check your name, contact information, and every date. Read your activities and essay as a reader would. Confirm that every section you expected is present. The preview is your last chance to catch errors before a reader does. The checklist below walks you through a full pre-submission review.

    Application Submission¶

    Application submission is the final step of sending your completed application to a college. On the Common App, you submit to each college separately. You'll review the preview, answer a short set of certification questions affirming that your application is your own work and accurate, pay the fee or apply a waiver, and click submit. Afterward, check the dashboard to make sure every part for that college, including any writing supplement, shows as complete.

    Submit early, ideally a few days before your personal due date from Chapter 5, not on deadline night. Servers slow down near major deadlines, payment systems sometimes fail, and you may discover a last-minute problem. Submitting a few days ahead turns any of those into a minor annoyance. Once you submit, you generally can't change your submitted application for that college, though you can usually update your core application for colleges you haven't submitted to yet.

    Worked Example: Submitting Early¶

    Priya's personal due date for College C's November 1 Early Action deadline is October 27. She finishes on October 25, previews the PDF, and submits that evening. On October 31, a classmate tells her the Common App is running slowly and a friend's payment failed twice. Priya isn't affected. She'd already received her confirmation and, on October 29, an email from the college with her applicant portal login.

    Submission Confirmation¶

    Submission confirmation is the evidence that a college received your application: a confirmation on your dashboard, a confirmation email from the Common App, and usually, within days to a couple of weeks, an email from the college with instructions for its applicant portal (Chapter 5). Confirmations can take a few minutes to a few hours to appear, so don't panic if one isn't instant. Do check back, and don't consider an application done until you have confirmation. Save each confirmation email and a screenshot of the dashboard in that college's folder, update your tracker, and set a reminder to check the college's portal about a week later for missing documents.

    Sending Official Documents¶

    Institution Codes¶

    Institution codes are the identification numbers testing agencies, financial aid systems, and application platforms use to send your documents to the right college. Each college has several, one for each system:

    Code Used for Example: University of Iowa
    SAT code (College Board) Sending official SAT and AP scores 6681
    ACT code Sending official ACT scores 1356
    Federal School Code Sending your FAFSA to the college (Chapter 14) 001892
    CEEB code (your high school) Identifying your high school on applications Your counselor can confirm it

    Find each college's codes on its admissions website, usually on a "how to send scores" page, or by searching in your College Board, ACT, or FAFSA account. Record them in your tracker. A score report sent with the wrong code goes to the wrong college and can't easily be redirected.

    Sending Official Transcripts¶

    Sending official transcripts is the step of having your high school send your official transcript to each college, along with the school report and counselor letter. You don't send it yourself. You request it, following your school's process. Many schools send transcripts electronically through the Common App or through services such as Naviance, Scoir, or Parchment, once you've added colleges and connected your counselor. Others use a request form. Ask your counselor in early fall how your school handles requests and how much lead time it needs, then make the request well before each supporting document deadline. Remember that dual-enrollment courses need a separate transcript from the college that granted the credit (Chapter 7). After each deadline, check each college's portal to confirm the transcript arrived and was matched to your file.

    Sending Test Scores¶

    Sending test scores means ordering official score reports from the College Board or ACT for the colleges that require them, as Chapter 6 described. Log in to your testing account, choose the test dates to send (using Score Choice where allowed), enter each college's code, and pay the fee or apply a fee waiver. Order reports at least two to three weeks before each college's supporting document deadline, since they take time to be delivered and matched to your file. For colleges that accept self-reported scores, wait. You'll send an official report only to the college where you enroll. Record each order in your tracker.

    Worked Example: Casey Sends Scores to Iowa¶

    Casey, an Iowa resident applying Early Action to the University of Iowa, plans to submit an ACT score. From Iowa's admissions website, Casey learns that Iowa's supporting documents deadline for Early Action is November 9, and finds Iowa's ACT code, 1356. On October 15, Casey logs in to MyACT, selects the September test date, enters code 1356, and pays for the report. Casey records "ACT sent Oct 15, code 1356" in the tracker and sets a reminder for November 1 to check Iowa's applicant portal. On October 28, the portal shows the score as received.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • The Common App dashboard shows each college's status. Check it every time you log in. Adding colleges brings in each college's requirements, so double-check the application round.
    • The Coalition App is a second shared platform. Use whichever platform covers your list. Colleges treat them equally.
    • In the profile section, use your legal name exactly as on school records. The family section determines first-generation and legacy information. The education section must list every school and college course.
    • The testing section holds self-reported scores. Use each college's own question to carry out your submission decision. Self-reported scores must be exact.
    • The activities section and writing section hold the work from Chapters 8 and 9. Paste carefully and check formatting. The additional information section is for brief, factual context, and leaving it empty is normal.
    • A self-reported academic record, including the Common App's courses and grades section, must match your transcript line by line.
    • Member questions can control your program and scholarship consideration. Writing supplements need a final check for the right prompt, length, and college name. Assigning recommenders must be done for every college.
    • Application fees add up. Fee waivers are available and confidential.
    • Read every application preview, complete application submission days early, and save each submission confirmation.
    • Use the right institution codes. Request official transcripts through your school, and send test scores only where required.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    Your Common App profile says 'Taylor Kim,' but your transcript says 'Taylor A. Kim-Morales.' What could happen, and how do you fix it?

    Colleges may not be able to match your transcript to your file, leaving it marked incomplete. Change the profile to your legal name exactly as on school records, and list your preferred name separately.

    You invited two teachers in September and added a new college in October. Why might that college show 'recommenders not invited'?

    Recommenders aren't automatically assigned to colleges you add later. Go to My Colleges and assign them to the new college.

    One college on your list is test-optional, and you decided not to submit scores there. Your SAT is in the Testing section. How do you keep that college from considering it?

    Answer that college's member question about testing with "do not consider my scores." The per-college question controls what each college sees.

    What belongs in the additional information section, and what doesn't?

    It's for brief, factual context the rest of the application doesn't explain, such as a move, an illness, a family responsibility, or a school policy. It's not for a second essay or more accomplishments, and leaving it empty is normal.

    Why should you submit a few days before the deadline instead of the night of?

    Servers slow down, payments can fail, and last-minute problems happen near deadlines. Submitting early turns those into minor annoyances and gives you time to confirm submission.
