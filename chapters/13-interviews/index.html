    Interviews¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers the kinds of college interviews, how to prepare and practice, what questions to ask, and how to follow up. Interviews are optional at many schools but can add a real voice to an application. After this chapter, students can prepare for any interview format and leave a good impression.

    A Conversation, Not a Test¶

    Many colleges never interview applicants. Of those that do, most treat the interview as optional, and it's rarely what decides an application. But an interview does something nothing else in your file can: it lets a real person hear you think and talk. A good one adds warmth and color to an application. A poorly prepared one is an easy miss to avoid.

    The good news is that interviewers aren't trying to catch you out. They aren't testing facts or looking for polished speeches. Mostly, they want to have a genuine conversation with a curious, engaged person. This chapter explains the different kinds of interviews, then covers how to prepare, practice, ask good questions, and follow up.

    Interviews¶

    A college interview is a conversation, usually 30 to 60 minutes long, between an applicant and someone representing the college: an admissions officer, an alum, or sometimes a current student. Colleges use interviews for two reasons. Some use them to learn more about you, as one more piece of evidence in holistic review (Chapter 2). Others use them mainly to help you learn about the college, and to show interest in you. Many do both.

    Policies vary widely, and the right approach depends on the college's policy:

    • Not offered: many large public universities and some private colleges don't interview at all. You don't need to do anything.
    • Optional: offered but not required. These are almost always worth doing if available. An interview is a low-risk way to add a positive data point, and at colleges that track demonstrated interest (Chapter 2), declining one can read as a mild signal of lower interest.
    • Recommended or required: a few colleges strongly encourage or require interviews where they can arrange them. Schedule them early, as soon as you apply or as the college instructs.

    Interviews rarely make or break an admission. Readers weigh them lightly compared with your academic record, essays, and recommendations. But in a close decision, an interviewer's report describing you as curious, warm, and engaged can help, and a report describing you as unprepared or uninterested can hurt.

    Check each college's interview policy on its admissions website and record it in your tracker. Some colleges require you to request an interview by a certain date, often weeks before the application deadline. Others contact you after you apply.

    Worked Example: Sorting Interview Policies¶

    Priya checks interview policies for her ten colleges:

    • Six colleges don't offer interviews, including her state flagship. Nothing to do.
    • Three colleges offer optional alumni interviews after you apply. She plans to accept any she's offered.
    • College A, her top reach, offers optional evaluative interviews with alumni, but applicants must request one by October 15 for the Regular Decision round.

    Without checking, Priya would have missed College A's request deadline, which falls two months before its application deadline. She requests the interview on October 3 and adds it to her tracker.

    Kinds of Interviews¶

    Interviews differ in two ways: who conducts them and what they're for. The table below previews the types covered in this section.

    Type Who conducts it Main purpose Counts in your file?
    Admissions officer interview Admissions staff Evaluate you and answer questions Usually yes
    Alumni interview A graduate of the college Evaluate you, share their experience Usually yes, as one input
    Evaluative interview Officer or trained alum Assess you for admission Yes
    Informational interview Student, alum, or staff Help you learn about the college Little or not at all
    Virtual interview Any of the above, by video Same as in person Same as in person

    The Admissions Officer Interview¶

    An admissions officer interview is an interview conducted by a member of the college's admissions staff, on campus, at a regional event, or by video. Admissions officers often know their region's high schools and may be the same person who reads your file. Their interview notes usually go straight into your application. These interviews tend to be more structured, with questions about your academics, interests, and reasons for applying. Treat them as you would any conversation with someone who has a say in your admission: prepared, honest, and engaged.

    The Alumni Interview¶

    An alumni interview is an interview conducted by a graduate of the college who volunteers to meet applicants in their area, usually at a coffee shop, library, or by video. Selective colleges with many applicants often use alumni interviewers because there are far more applicants than admissions staff could meet. Alumni interviewers are trained by the college and write a short report afterward. That report is one input among many, and readers know alumni vary in style. Alumni interviews tend to be relaxed and conversational, and many interviewers enjoy talking about their own time at the college. Let them. It's a chance to learn something real about the place.

    The Evaluative Interview¶

    An evaluative interview is an interview whose report becomes part of your application and can influence the admission decision. Both admissions officer and alumni interviews are usually evaluative. The interviewer is forming an impression of you, often rating qualities like curiosity, maturity, and fit, and writing it down. That doesn't mean it's adversarial. It means you should prepare: know your application, know why you want this college, and be ready to talk about both naturally.

    The Informational Interview¶

    An informational interview is a conversation meant mainly to help you learn about the college, with little or no role in your evaluation. It may be with a current student, an alum, or staff, and it's often called an "information session" or "chat." Colleges sometimes describe their interviews this way to lower the pressure. Still, treat every interview as if it might count. A poor showing can leave a negative impression that finds its way back, and a strong one never hurts. Use informational interviews to ask the specific questions your school-specific research raised.

    The Virtual Interview¶

    A virtual interview is an interview held by video call instead of in person. Many colleges now offer them, and they're treated the same as in-person interviews. The conversation is the same, but the logistics matter more:

    • Test your camera, microphone, and internet connection the day before, not the minute the call starts. Also test the specific platform, such as Zoom or Teams.
    • Choose a quiet, well-lit spot with a plain background. Light from in front of you, not behind.
    • Close other apps and silence notifications, including your phone.
    • Look at the camera when you speak, at least some of the time, so you appear to be making eye contact.
    • Join a few minutes early, and have the interviewer's contact information handy in case the connection fails.

    Worked Example: Fixing the Setup¶

    Marcus has a virtual alumni interview with Lakeshore University on a Thursday evening. On Wednesday, he does a test call with his cousin and discovers three problems. The restaurant's back office is noisy. The window behind him turns him into a silhouette. And his laptop's battery lasts only 40 minutes. He moves to his bedroom, turns his desk so the window is in front of him, and plugs in his charger. On Thursday, the call starts on time and runs 45 minutes without a problem. The test call took fifteen minutes and saved him from a stressful start.

    Preparing¶

    Interview Preparation¶

    Interview preparation is getting ready to have a natural, engaged conversation about yourself and the college. You're not memorizing answers. Good preparation has four parts.

    Know your own application. Reread your activities list, personal essay, and supplements for that college. Be ready to talk about any of it naturally, especially your intended major, your most important activities, and why you're interested in this college.

    Research the college specifically. Use your notes from Chapter 10's school-specific research: a program, a class, a professor, a tradition, a student organization. You should be able to say, in your own words, what draws you to this college in particular.

    Prepare for common questions. Most interviews include some version of these:

    • Tell me about yourself.
    • Why are you interested in this college?
    • What do you want to study, and why?
    • What do you do outside of class? Which activity matters most to you?
    • Tell me about a challenge you've faced, or a mistake you learned from.
    • What's something you've read, watched, or learned recently that interested you?
    • What would you contribute to our campus?
    • Do you have any questions for me?

    For each, think of one or two specific stories or examples you could share. Specific stories make answers memorable, just as they do in essays. Don't write scripts. Rehearsed answers sound rehearsed.

    Handle logistics. Confirm the date, time, time zone, and location or link. Plan what to wear: neat and comfortable, a step up from everyday school clothes, but not a suit. Plan to arrive ten minutes early. Bring nothing but yourself, perhaps a copy of your activities resume if the interviewer might want it, and a short list of your questions.

    Interviewers are listening for a few things, which is why preparation focuses where it does:

    • Genuine curiosity and engagement. Do you talk about your interests with real energy?
    • Self-awareness. Can you reflect honestly on a challenge or a mistake, not just list accomplishments?
    • Fit. Does the way you talk about yourself suggest you'd contribute something to their community?
    • Conversational ease. Not polish, just the ability to have a normal two-way conversation, rather than a monologue or a string of one-word answers.

    Worked Example: From Script to Story¶

    Ana's first attempt at answering "Why do you want to study education?" sounds like a speech: "I have always been passionate about helping others, and I believe education is the key to a better future." When she practices it aloud, it feels hollow.

    She asks herself what actually made her want to teach, and answers with her essay's story in conversational form: "Honestly, it started with my little brother. When he was struggling to read, I sat with him every night for a couple of weeks, and the moment he finally got it, I wanted to feel that again. Now I tutor at my church, and I've noticed I'm good at seeing when someone's lost before they say so." That answer is specific, sounds like her, and invites a follow-up question, which is exactly what makes an interview a conversation.

    The Practice Interview¶

    A practice interview is a mock interview with a parent, teacher, counselor, or friend, done out loud, before the real one. The goal isn't to memorize answers. It's to get comfortable hearing yourself talk about your story before it matters, and to find which answers come out vague or rambling. Ask your practice partner to use the common questions from the previous section, plus one or two surprises. Afterward, ask them: Which answer was most interesting? Where did I ramble? Did I sound like myself? One or two practice sessions are plenty. More than that can make you sound rehearsed. The practice deck below gives you questions to try, with a timer.

    Questions to Ask the Interviewer¶

    Questions to ask the interviewer are the questions you prepare for the end of the interview, when nearly every interviewer asks, "Do you have any questions for me?" Always have two or three ready. Having none can read as a lack of real interest. Good questions are ones you couldn't answer from the college's website. Questions about the interviewer's own experience are usually a safe, effective choice: What surprised you most about the college? What do you wish you'd known as a first-year? Where did you spend most of your time outside class? You can also ask a specific question from your research: I read about the peer-mentoring program in CS. How common is it for first-years to join? Avoid questions about admission odds, and avoid questions any brochure answers, such as "How big is the campus?"

    After the Interview¶

    Interview Follow-Up¶

    Interview follow-up is the short, sincere thank-you you send your interviewer within a day or two after the interview, usually by email. Thank them for their time, mention one specific thing you enjoyed talking about, and, if it's true, say that the conversation increased your interest in the college. Keep it to three or four sentences. Don't use it to add new accomplishments or ask about your chances. If the interviewer gave you their email, use it. If not, ask the admissions office whether they can pass a note along, or send your thanks through the scheduling email. Then record the interview and follow-up in your tracker, and note anything you learned that might help you decide in the spring.

    Worked Example: Priya's Thank-You¶

    After her College A interview, Priya emails her alumni interviewer that evening:

    "Dear Ms. Patel, thank you for meeting with me today. I loved hearing about the late-night arguments in your first-year writing seminar. It made me even more excited about the seminar program. I'm grateful for your time and your advice about trying the student newspaper. Best, Priya Raman."

    It's brief, specific, and genuine. It reminds the interviewer of their conversation and confirms Priya's interest, which is all a follow-up needs to do.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Interviews are conversations, not tests. They rarely decide an admission but can help in a close call. Check each college's policy and request deadline.
    • Admissions officer interviews and alumni interviews are usually evaluative interviews that go into your file. Informational interviews mainly help you learn. Treat every one as if it counts.
    • Virtual interviews count the same as in-person ones. Test your setup the day before.
    • Interview preparation means knowing your application, researching the college, and preparing specific stories for common questions, without scripts.
    • One or two practice interviews, done out loud, build comfort without making you sound rehearsed.
    • Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer that a website couldn't answer.
    • Send a short, specific interview follow-up within a day or two.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    A college offers optional interviews and tracks demonstrated interest. Should you do one?

    Yes, almost always. Optional interviews are low-risk chances to add a positive impression, and at colleges that track demonstrated interest, declining can read as lower interest.

    What's the difference between an evaluative interview and an informational interview?

    An evaluative interview produces a report that becomes part of your application. An informational interview is mainly for you to learn about the college. Still, treat both as if they count.

    Rewrite this interview answer to be more specific: 'I like math because it's challenging and interesting.'

    One possible answer: "Last spring I spent a whole weekend on one geometry proof our teacher said was optional. When it finally clicked, I wanted more problems like it." Any answer with a specific moment and a reason works.

    Name two good questions to ask an alumni interviewer and one to avoid.

    Good: "What surprised you most about the college?" or "What do you wish you'd known as a first-year?" Avoid: questions a website answers, or "What are my chances?"

    What should an interview thank-you note include, and what shouldn't it?

    Include thanks, one specific detail from the conversation, and genuine interest. Don't add new accomplishments or ask about admission chances. Keep it to a few sentences.
