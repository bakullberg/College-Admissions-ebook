    Quiz: Interviews¶

    Test your understanding of interview types, preparation and practice, questions to ask, and follow-up with these review questions.

    1. What does the chapter say usually happens to an admissions officer's interview notes?¶

    1. They are used only to answer the applicant's questions
    2. They are shared with the applicant's high school counselor
    3. They usually go straight into the applicant's file
    4. They are kept only if the applicant enrolls
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. An admissions officer interview is conducted by a member of the college's admissions staff, on campus, at a regional event, or by video. These officers often know their region's high schools and may be the same person who reads your file, and their interview notes usually go straight into your application. That's why the chapter says to treat these more structured interviews as a conversation with someone who has a say in your admission.

    2. When should you send an interview follow-up?¶

    1. After you receive your admission decision
    2. Within a day or two after the interview
    3. On the morning before the interview
    4. Only if the interviewer asks you to write
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Interview follow-up is a short, sincere thank-you sent within a day or two after the interview, usually by email. It should thank the interviewer, mention one specific thing you enjoyed discussing, and say, if true, that the conversation increased your interest. Waiting until a decision arrives defeats the purpose. The chapter doesn't make follow-up depend on being asked, and Emily sends hers the same evening.

    3. Why do selective colleges with many applicants often use alumni interviewers?¶

    1. Alumni interviews replace the need for recommendation letters
    2. Alumni decide the final admission outcome for each applicant
    3. Alumni are the only people allowed to evaluate applicants
    4. There are far more applicants than admissions staff could meet
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter says selective colleges with many applicants often use alumni interviewers because there are far more applicants than admissions staff could meet. Alumni are trained by the college and write a short report afterward, which is one input among many. Admissions officers also interview, so alumni aren't the only evaluators. The report doesn't decide the outcome or replace recommendation letters.

    4. How much weight do interviews usually carry in an admission decision?¶

    1. They are weighed lightly but can help or hurt in a close decision
    2. They usually decide the outcome on their own
    3. They have no effect on any application at any college
    4. They count more than the academic record at most colleges
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says interviews rarely make or break an admission, and readers weigh them lightly compared with the academic record, essays, and recommendations. But in a close decision, a report describing you as curious, warm, and engaged can help, and one describing you as unprepared can hurt. That's why the chapter calls optional interviews low-risk and almost always worth doing, even though they aren't usually decisive.

    5. A college describes its interview as an informational "chat." Why does the chapter still advise treating it as if it counts?¶

    1. Colleges require a written summary from the applicant afterward
    2. The interviewer will be the student's first-year advisor
    3. Informational interviews are secretly scored the same as evaluative ones
    4. A poor showing can leave a negative impression that finds its way back
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. An informational interview is mainly meant to help you learn about the college, with little or no role in your evaluation. Colleges sometimes describe interviews this way to lower the pressure. The chapter still says to treat every interview as if it might count, because a poor showing can leave a negative impression that finds its way back, while a strong one never hurts. The chapter doesn't claim these chats are secretly scored.

    6. Why does the chapter suggest limiting yourself to one or two practice interviews?¶

    1. Practice partners need to be scheduled weeks in advance
    2. More practice than that can make you sound rehearsed
    3. Colleges can tell how many times you practiced
    4. Practice interviews must be done with an admissions officer
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A practice interview is a mock interview done out loud with a parent, teacher, counselor, or friend. Its goal is to get comfortable telling your story and find which answers come out vague or rambling, not to memorize answers. The chapter says one or two sessions are plenty, and more can make you sound rehearsed. Interviewers want a genuine conversation, not polished speeches, and any trusted adult or friend can be a practice partner.

    7. During a test call the day before a virtual interview, Sam notices a bright window behind them turns them into a silhouette. What fix does the chapter's advice suggest?¶

    1. Rearrange the setup so the light comes from in front
    2. Turn off the camera and use audio only
    3. Move the interview to a noisy room with overhead lights
    4. Wear darker clothing so the face stands out
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. For virtual interviews, the chapter says to choose a quiet, well-lit spot with a plain background and to have light in front of you, not behind. That's exactly how Marcus fixed his silhouette problem: he turned his desk so the window was in front of him. Turning off the camera removes the face-to-face connection, and a noisy room creates a new problem. Virtual interviews count the same as in-person ones.

    8. At the end of an alumni interview, the interviewer asks if you have questions. Which question best follows the chapter's advice?¶

    1. "How big is the campus?"
    2. "How many students attend the college?"
    3. "What are my chances of getting in?"
    4. "What do you wish you'd known as a first-year?"
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Good questions are ones you couldn't answer from the college's website, and questions about the interviewer's own experience are usually a safe, effective choice. The chapter lists "What do you wish you'd known as a first-year?" as an example. Questions about campus size or enrollment are answered by any brochure, and the chapter says to avoid questions about admission odds. Having no questions can read as a lack of real interest.

    9. A college's website says applicants must request an optional interview by October 15, while its application is due January 1. What should a student who wants an interview do?¶

    1. Wait for the college to contact them after January 1
    2. Request it before October 15 and add it to the tracker
    3. Wait until submitting the application in December
    4. Skip it, since optional interviews don't affect decisions
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says some colleges require you to request an interview by a certain date, often weeks before the application deadline, which is what happened with Emily's College A. Checking the policy and requesting early is the only way not to miss it. Waiting until December or January would be too late for this college. Optional interviews are almost always worth doing, since they're a low-risk chance to add a positive impression.

    10. Asked "Why do you want to study education?", Applicant 1 says, "I have always been passionate about helping others." Applicant 2 describes reading with a younger brother every night until he could read, and now tutoring at church. What makes Applicant 2's answer stronger?¶

    1. It avoids any personal detail, which keeps it professional
    2. It is longer, and interviewers reward longer answers
    3. It uses a specific story that sounds genuine and invites a follow-up question
    4. It was memorized word for word, so it sounds polished
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Interviewers listen for genuine curiosity, self-awareness, fit, and conversational ease. The chapter says specific stories make answers memorable, just as they do in essays, and Ana's revised answer works because it sounds like her and invites a follow-up, turning the interview into a conversation. Applicant 1's answer sounds like a speech. The chapter warns against scripts, since rehearsed answers sound rehearsed, and length alone isn't the point.
