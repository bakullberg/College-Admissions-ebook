    The Personal Essay¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers writing the Common App personal essay from brainstorming and topic choice through structure, authentic voice, reflection, drafting, feedback, and revision. The personal essay is the one place the student's own voice comes through directly. This chapter takes students from a blank page to a revised, proofread draft.

    The Part of the Application in Your Own Voice¶

    Every other part of your application describes you from the outside. Your transcript is a list of grades. Your activities list is 150-character summaries. Your recommendations are other people's words about you. The personal essay is the one place where a reader hears you directly, in your own voice, telling them something they couldn't learn anywhere else.

    That's also why students dread it. It can feel like the essay has to be profound, a story of tragedy overcome or a life-changing moment, and most seventeen-year-olds haven't lived that kind of story. The good news is that it doesn't need one. The strongest essays are often about small, specific things, told honestly, with real thought about what they mean. A reader should finish your essay feeling they know one true, particular thing about you.

    This chapter takes you from a blank page to a finished draft. You'll start with what college essays are and what the personal essay is for, then learn the prompts and the word limit. Next you'll brainstorm, collecting material and finding a personal story, while avoiding the topics readers see too often. Then come the writing techniques that make an essay work: showing instead of telling, an authentic voice, a clear structure, a strong opening, and real reflection. The chapter ends with the process of drafting, getting feedback, revising, and proofreading.

    Throughout, you'll follow Ana as she writes her essay. Chapter 8 ended with her noticing a possible topic in her activities resume: the nightly reading sessions with her younger brother.

    College Essays¶

    A college essay is any piece of writing a college asks you to submit as part of your application. There are two main kinds:

    • The personal essay, a longer essay about yourself, usually sent to every college through a shared platform like the Common App. This chapter covers it.
    • Supplemental essays, shorter essays that individual colleges add, often asking why you want to attend or about a specific interest. Chapter 10 covers them.

    Colleges ask for essays because holistic review, as Chapter 2 described, tries to understand applicants as people, not just numbers. Essays give readers three things nothing else in the file can:

    1. Your voice. How you think, what you notice, and how you put things into words.
    2. Your reflection. Your ability to look at your own experience and understand what it means.
    3. What's missing. A part of your life, your values, or your thinking that the rest of the application doesn't show.

    How much essays matter depends on the college. At formula-based public universities like Iowa, the essay may not be required for basic admission at all, though honors programs and scholarships may ask for one. At holistic colleges, the essay feeds a reader's personal rating. At the most selective colleges, where nearly every applicant has strong grades, essays often help decide among otherwise similar files. It's rare for an essay alone to win admission, but a strong one can tip a close decision, and a careless one can raise doubts.

    Because you'll write many essays, one personal essay plus supplements for several colleges, organization matters. That's where your essay drafts folder comes in.

    The Essay Drafts Folder¶

    The essay drafts folder is the part of your filing system, from Chapter 5, where you keep every essay draft. Create a subfolder for your personal essay and one for each college's supplements. Save each significant version as a new file with a clear name and version number, such as "Personal_Essay_v3_2026-09-14," rather than overwriting the old one. You'll often want to recover a paragraph you cut, compare versions, or reuse a strong passage in a supplement. Keep a short "notes" file in the folder too, with the prompts, word limits, and feedback you've received.

    The Personal Essay¶

    The personal essay, also called the personal statement or the Common App essay, is the main essay you submit with your application. You write it about yourself, in response to one of several broad prompts, and send it to every college that receives your Common App. It's typically the longest piece of writing in your application, and the only one most of your colleges will all read.

    Its job is simple to state and hard to do: show the reader who you are through a story or idea that matters to you, told in your own voice, with honest reflection on what it means. A personal essay is not:

    • A résumé in paragraphs. Your activities list already covers what you've done.
    • An academic paper. You aren't proving a thesis with sources.
    • A list of virtues. Saying "I am hardworking and compassionate" proves nothing.
    • A story about someone else. Writing about a grandparent or a coach is fine, but the essay must ultimately reveal you.

    The best way to understand the difference is to see two openings for the same topic.

    Worked Example: Two Essays, One Topic¶

    Two students write about working at a family restaurant.

    Essay 1 opens: "Working at my family's restaurant has taught me many valuable lessons about hard work, responsibility, and customer service. These skills will help me succeed in college and beyond."

    Essay 2 opens: "Every Friday at 6:15, the bell over the kitchen door rings eleven times in four minutes, and I've learned to hear in it which orders will go wrong."

    Essay 1 tells the reader what to think, in words any student could have written. Essay 2 drops the reader into a specific moment only this student could describe, and it raises a question, how can a bell tell you which orders will go wrong?, that makes the reader want to continue. By the end, Essay 2 can reach the same lessons about responsibility. But the reader will believe them, because they've seen them happen. The rest of this chapter is about how to write the second kind of essay.

    The Prompts and the Limits¶

    The Common App Essay Prompts¶

    The Common App essay prompts are the set of essay questions the Common App provides each year for the personal essay. You choose one. The prompts have stayed largely the same for several years. There are seven, and in summary they ask you to write about:

    1. A background, identity, interest, or talent so central to you that your application would feel incomplete without it.
    2. An obstacle, setback, or failure, and what you learned from it.
    3. A time you questioned or challenged a belief or idea.
    4. Something someone did for you that made you grateful in a surprising way.
    5. An accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked personal growth.
    6. A topic, idea, or concept so engaging that you lose track of time.
    7. Any topic of your choice.

    Read the exact wording on the Common App website, since prompts are occasionally revised, but understand what they're for. The prompts are broad on purpose. They're doors into almost any story, not narrow assignments. Most good essays fit more than one prompt, and prompt 7 exists for stories that don't fit the others neatly. Readers care about what your essay reveals, not about how precisely it answers the literal question.

    That leads to the most useful piece of advice about prompts: don't start with them. Start with your material. Once you know your story, find the prompt it fits. Students who start with a prompt often end up writing what they think the prompt wants instead of what's true about them.

    Worked Example: Finding the Prompt After the Story¶

    Ana's essay idea is about reading with her younger brother every night in 7th grade, when he was struggling and she realized she was good at teaching. She checks it against the prompts:

    • Prompt 1 (background or identity): possible, since being the family's translator and helper is central to who she is.
    • Prompt 5 (growth): strong. Teaching her brother changed how she saw herself.
    • Prompt 2 (obstacle): possible, though the obstacle was her brother's, not hers.

    Ana chooses prompt 5, because her story is really about a realization. But the choice barely matters. Her story would work under any of the three. That's typical, and it's why the story should come first.

    The Essay Word Limit¶

    The essay word limit is the maximum length a college or platform allows for an essay. For the Common App personal essay, it's 650 words, with a minimum of 250. The application box enforces the maximum, so you can't go over.

    650 words is shorter than it sounds, about a page and a third, double-spaced. That length shapes the essay in two ways. First, there's room for one story, told well, not three stories told briefly. Students who try to cover their whole life end up with an essay that's broad and thin. Second, every sentence has to earn its place. Long introductions, repeated points, and general statements about what you learned use up words that could go to specific detail and reflection.

    Most strong essays use most of the space, roughly 550 to 650 words. An essay much shorter than that is usually missing reflection. It's also fine to draft long and cut later. Many students' first drafts run 900 words or more, and cutting them down is part of the revision process.

    Finding Your Story¶

    Brainstorming¶

    Brainstorming is the process of generating many possible essay ideas and details before choosing a topic. It works best when you resist the urge to find your "big story" right away and instead collect small, specific material. A few techniques work well:

    • Start with your running list. If you began a list of essay material as one of the five first steps in Chapter 5, read through it first. It's already full of raw material.
    • Ask people who know you. Ask a parent, a friend, a coach, or a sibling what they'd say makes you you. Their answers often surface qualities you take for granted.
    • Look for specific, not significant. A small, vivid detail, like the way your grandmother organizes her spices or the sound of a bell in your family's kitchen, is often better material than a large, vague event like a trip or a tournament. Specificity is what makes an essay feel true.
    • Work backward from a quality. Choose one trait you'd want a reader to know about you, such as curiosity, persistence, humor, or care for others. Then list three ordinary moments when that trait showed.
    • Mine your activities resume. Your resume from Chapter 8 lists where your time goes. The moments inside those activities are often essay material.
    • Use object and place prompts. List ten objects in your room that matter to you, or five places where you feel most like yourself. Write a sentence about why for each.

    Brainstorming should feel loose. Write everything down, including ideas that seem silly. You're collecting, not judging.

    Worked Example: Ana's Brainstorm¶

    Ana spends forty-five minutes brainstorming. She asks her mother and her best friend what makes her her. Her mother says "you notice when someone is confused before they say it." Her friend says "you explain things better than teachers." She lists objects (a worn copy of Charlotte's Web, her phone's translation app, a whiteboard in the kitchen) and moments:

    • Translating a doctor's explanation for her father and realizing she didn't understand it herself.
    • The food pantry family who came back to thank her in Spanish.
    • Reading Charlotte's Web with her brother night after night until he could read it alone.
    • Learning to edit videos from free tutorials for her church.
    • Burning dinner the first night she cooked alone.

    She doesn't choose yet. But she notices that her mother's and friend's comments, and three of her five moments, are all about explaining things to people.

    The Essay Material List¶

    The essay material list is the running record you keep of possible essay material: moments, details, quotes, objects, and ideas, collected over weeks or months. It's different from a one-time brainstorm because it builds up. The best material rarely shows up when you sit down to write. It shows up in the shower, on the drive home, or in the middle of a shift. Keep the list on your phone so you can add to it right away, and save a copy in your essay drafts folder. When you're ready to choose a topic, you'll have far more to choose from than any single brainstorming session could produce. Write down specifics, not summaries: "Dad's face when the doctor said 'benign' and I had to look up the word," not "translating at the doctor."

    The Personal Story¶

    A personal story is the specific experience, moment, or idea at the center of your essay, told from your own point of view and revealing something about who you are. It's the raw material the essay is built from, and its quality depends less on how dramatic it is than on how specific and meaningful it is to you.

    A good personal story has three qualities:

    1. It's yours. Only you could tell it this way. It comes from your life, your perspective, and your details.
    2. It's specific. It's grounded in particular moments, places, and details, not a summary of a whole period.
    3. It reveals something. It shows a quality, a value, a way of thinking, or a change in you that the reader wouldn't otherwise know.

    Notice what's not on the list: drama, achievement, or uniqueness of experience. Many students have worked at a restaurant or cared for a sibling. What makes a story distinctive is how you see it and what you make of it.

    Worked Example: Testing Three Stories¶

    Ana tests her three strongest ideas against the three qualities:

    Story Hers? Specific? Reveals something new?
    Translating at the doctor's office Yes Yes, one appointment Partly. Her activities list already shows she translates.
    Reading Charlotte's Web with her brother Yes Yes, nightly sessions, one book Yes. It shows how she learned to teach, which isn't on her list.
    Burning dinner the first night Yes Yes Not much. It's funny, but the meaning is thin.

    The reading story passes all three tests and reveals something new: how she figured out, at twelve, that patience and noticing confusion are skills. It becomes her topic, and the translation story may appear briefly inside it.

    The Small Moment Essay¶

    A small moment essay is a personal essay built around one brief, specific experience, sometimes only a few minutes long, which the writer explores in detail and then reflects on. Instead of summarizing years of your life, it zooms in on a single scene, like a conversation, a mistake, or an ordinary afternoon, and uses it to reveal something larger. Small moment essays work well because 650 words is exactly enough to render one moment vividly and reflect on it, and because a small, true moment often stands out among essays about big events. The moment doesn't need to be dramatic. It needs to be one you can describe in detail and that genuinely means something to you.

    Overused Essay Topics¶

    Overused essay topics are subjects that admissions readers see so often that essays about them tend to blend together. They're not forbidden. Any topic can work if it's told with specific detail and genuine reflection. But they make standing out harder, so if you choose one, you need a fresh angle.

    The table below lists common overused topics, why they're risky, and how a strong essay on the same subject might work.

    Topic Why it's risky How it can work
    The big game or performance Readers have seen thousands of winning shots and comeback stories Focus on an unexpected, specific moment, such as what you noticed on the bench, not the win
    The service trip Often reads as privileged or as "I helped them" Write about something you were wrong about, or skip the trip for service close to home
    A grandparent's death Common and often more about the grandparent than you Center a specific thing you learned or do differently because of them
    Immigrant or moving story told as summary Easy to fall into general statements Anchor it in one object, ritual, or conversation
    "I learned hard work from my job/sport" The lesson is predictable Show one specific skill or insight the reader wouldn't expect
    Résumé essay Repeats the activities list Choose one activity and go deep
    Trauma told for its own sake Can overwhelm the essay and leave little room for you If you write about hardship, focus on your response and reflection, and share only what you're comfortable sharing

    A useful test: if a hundred students could write your essay with only the names changed, you need a more specific angle. If only you could have written it, you're fine, whatever the topic.

    Essay Topic Selection¶

    Essay topic selection is the decision about which story or idea your personal essay will be about. It draws on everything above: your material list, the qualities of a good personal story, the prompts, and awareness of overused topics. Ask four questions of each candidate:

    1. Does it reveal something important about me that the rest of my application doesn't?
    2. Can I tell it with specific scenes and details?
    3. Do I have something to say about what it means, not just what happened?
    4. Do I want to write about it? Energy shows in the writing.

    If two topics pass, try writing a quick, messy paragraph about each. The one that's easier to write about, the one where details keep coming, is usually the right choice. Don't agonize too long. A good topic written well beats a perfect topic chosen in late December.

    Writing Techniques¶

    Show, Don't Tell¶

    Show, don't tell is the writing principle of letting readers see a quality through specific actions, scenes, and details, instead of stating it directly. Telling names the quality: "I am patient." Showing puts it in front of the reader: "I read the same page of Charlotte's Web to my brother eleven nights in a row, until he stopped looking at the pictures and started looking at the words." The reader concludes that you're patient, and believes it, because they saw it.

    Showing works because readers trust evidence more than claims. Every applicant claims to be hardworking, curious, and kind. Only you can show the specific moment when you were.

    To show, use:

    • Specific actions: what you did, step by step.
    • Concrete details: what you saw, heard, or held.
    • Dialogue, in small doses: what someone actually said.
    • Numbers and particulars: "eleven nights," not "many nights."

    The principle isn't absolute. A personal essay also needs reflection, where you step back and tell the reader what the experience meant. The common pattern is show first, then tell briefly. Ground the essay in scenes, then reflect on them. An essay that's all telling feels empty. An essay that's all showing feels unfinished.

    Worked Example: From Telling to Showing¶

    Ana's first draft includes this sentence: "Helping my brother learn to read taught me that I am a patient and caring person who enjoys helping others."

    She revises it by asking, what did that actually look like?

    "Every night at 8:30, after the dishes, Mateo and I sat on the kitchen floor with Charlotte's Web. He'd guess at words from the pictures. I'd cover the pictures with my hand. He'd cry. We'd try again. On the eleventh night, he read the book's first line, the one about Papa and the ax, without looking up at me."

    The revision never uses the words "patient" or "caring." It doesn't need to. The exercise below lets you practice the same move.

    Authentic Voice¶

    Authentic voice means writing that sounds like you, with the words, rhythm, and personality of the way you actually think and talk, polished but not replaced. Readers spend much of their time trying to get a sense of the real person behind the application. An essay that sounds like a seventeen-year-old thinking carefully earns trust. An essay that sounds like a thesaurus or an adult editor makes them wonder who wrote it.

    Voice is easy to lose in two ways. The first is trying to sound impressive: big words you wouldn't normally use, formal phrasing, sentences that perform intelligence rather than show it. "The pedagogical experience engendered within me a profound appreciation for literacy" is grammatically fine, and no one believes a student wrote it. The second is over-editing by others: when parents, tutors, or friends rewrite sentences in their own voice.

    The best test of voice is simple: read your essay out loud. If a sentence sounds like something you'd never say, revise it until it does. Your voice can be serious, funny, quiet, or energetic. It just has to be yours. Humor is fine if it's natural for you. Don't force it if it isn't.

    Worked Example: Finding the Voice¶

    Ana's second draft has a sentence she's proud of: "This formative experience profoundly illuminated my innate predisposition toward educational endeavors." When she reads it aloud to her father on Sunday morning, he laughs and says, "That's not you." She asks what would sound like her. He says, "You always say you like it when someone finally gets it."

    Her revision: "I found out I like the exact second someone finally gets it, the way Mateo's whole face changed on the eleventh night." It uses ordinary words, sounds like her, and is more vivid than the original.

    Essay Structure¶

    Essay structure is the way an essay's parts are arranged: where it begins, how it moves, and where it ends. A personal essay doesn't need the five-paragraph format from English class. It needs a shape that carries the reader through your story and into your reflection. Three structures work well for most personal essays:

    • Narrative arc: a single story told mostly in order, with an opening scene, a turning point, and reflection on what changed. It's the most common structure and the easiest to control.
    • Montage: several short scenes or images connected by a common thread, such as an object, a place, or a quality, with reflection tying them together. It works well when no single moment tells the whole story.
    • Idea-driven: an essay organized around a question or idea you care about, using examples from your life to explore it. It works well for prompt 6 and for intellectually curious writers.

    Whichever you choose, most strong personal essays share an underlying pattern. They open in a specific moment, move through the story with concrete detail, reach a turn where something shifts or becomes clear, and close with reflection that reaches beyond the story. A rough word budget for a 650-word narrative might be: opening scene, 80–120 words; story and detail, 250–300; the turn, 80–120; reflection and ending, 150–200.

    Worked Example: Outlining Ana's Essay¶

    Ana chooses a narrative arc and outlines her essay with a rough word budget:

    1. Opening scene (≈100 words): the kitchen floor, 8:30 p.m., Mateo guessing from pictures.
    2. The story (≈275 words): the nightly routine; her covering the pictures; his frustration and hers; noticing that he got stuck on the same kinds of words; making flash cards on the back of grocery receipts.
    3. The turn (≈100 words): the eleventh night, when he reads a line without looking up.
    4. Reflection (≈175 words): what she learned about noticing confusion; how that shows up now in translating for her parents and tutoring at church; why she wants to study education.

    The outline adds up to about 650 words. It also shows her where the translation story fits, as a brief connection in the reflection rather than a competing topic. The explorer below shows how each part of a structure does its job.

    The Opening Hook¶

    An opening hook is the first sentence or two of an essay, written to draw the reader in and make them want to keep reading. Readers often go through many essays in a sitting, so the opening shapes how attentively they read the rest.

    The strongest hooks usually do one of three things:

    • Start in a scene: drop the reader into a specific moment. "Every night at 8:30, after the dishes, Mateo and I sat on the kitchen floor."
    • Raise a question: say something surprising enough that the reader needs to know more. "I've learned to hear in the kitchen bell which orders will go wrong."
    • Offer a striking detail: begin with an unexpected, concrete image. "My most important textbook is a grocery receipt covered in the word 'thought.'"

    Avoid the openings readers see constantly: dictionary definitions ("Webster's defines courage as..."), famous quotations, sweeping statements ("Since the dawn of time, humans have..."), and summaries of what the essay will say ("In this essay, I will describe..."). A good hook doesn't have to be clever. It just has to be specific. And it's often easier to write last. Many writers find their best opening sentence somewhere in the middle of their first draft.

    Worked Example: Trying Three Openings¶

    Ana drafts three openings for her essay:

    1. "Literacy is one of the most important skills a person can have." This is general, and could open any essay about reading.
    2. "Have you ever taught someone to read?" This asks a question, but a generic one. Many readers will simply answer "no" and move on.
    3. "On the eleventh night, my brother read a sentence about an ax without looking up at me." This starts in a specific, slightly strange moment, and raises questions: What ax? Why eleven nights?

    She chooses the third, which means her essay now opens at the turning point and flashes back. That's a small structural change her outline can absorb. A strong hook sometimes reshapes the essay around it.

    Reflection and Insight¶

    Reflection and insight are the parts of an essay where you step back from the story and explain what it means: what you learned, how you changed, what it shows about how you think, or why it still matters to you. Reflection is what turns a story into a personal essay. Without it, a reader knows what happened but not who you are.

    Reflection is the part students most often leave thin, partly because it's hard. Good reflection avoids two traps. The first is the generic lesson: "This taught me that hard work pays off." Readers have seen it thousands of times, and it could follow any story. The second is the oversized claim: "This experience changed my life forever." It's rarely true, and readers know it.

    Strong reflection is specific to your story and honest about its size. It might name a small realization, a question you still have, a way you act differently now, or a connection between this moment and other parts of your life. Ask yourself:

    • What did I understand afterward that I didn't before?
    • Where does that understanding show up in my life now?
    • What's still unresolved or surprising to me about it?
    • Why does this matter to who I want to be?

    Reflection doesn't have to wait until the last paragraph. Brief moments of thought woven through the story often read better than one big lesson at the end.

    Worked Example: Deepening Reflection¶

    Ana's first-draft reflection reads: "Teaching Mateo taught me that helping others is rewarding, and I want to keep helping people in the future."

    She asks herself the four questions and revises:

    "What I learned on the kitchen floor wasn't really about reading. It was that confusion has a look, a small pause before someone guesses, and that if you catch it in that pause, you can help before they give up. I notice that pause everywhere now: in my father's face when a doctor speaks too fast, in the families at the food pantry intake table, in my own face in the mirror before a chemistry test. I don't know yet whether I'll be a teacher. I do know I want to spend my life catching people in that pause."

    The revision connects the story to her present life, names a specific insight, admits what's uncertain, and points to the future without overclaiming.

    From Draft to Final¶

    Drafting¶

    Drafting is the stage of writing where you get the whole essay down on the page for the first time. The most useful advice about drafting is to write the first draft fast and messy. Don't edit while you draft. Get the entire story down, even badly, from beginning to end, before you start refining anything. Students who try to make each sentence perfect before writing the next often stall for weeks.

    A good drafting session looks like this:

    1. Start with your outline and word budget, if you have one.
    2. Set a timer for 45 to 60 minutes and write without stopping to fix things.
    3. Overwrite. Put in every detail you remember. It's easier to cut than to add.
    4. Mark gaps with brackets, such as [better word] or [what did he say?], instead of stopping to fill them.
    5. Stop and save as version 1 in your essay drafts folder.

    Then step away for at least a day. Distance lets you see the draft the way a reader will. Plan for several drafts. Four or five versions are normal for a strong personal essay. The first draft finds the story, and later drafts find the voice and the meaning. That's why starting in summer, or at least early in senior fall, matters so much.

    Feedback on Essays¶

    Feedback on essays is input from other readers, such as a teacher, counselor, parent, or friend, about what's working and what isn't in your draft. Good feedback helps you see your essay as a stranger would: where it's unclear, where it drags, where the voice slips, and where the meaning isn't coming through.

    Feedback works best when you choose your readers and your questions deliberately:

    Reader Good for Watch out for
    English teacher Clarity, structure, and writing quality Turning it into an academic essay
    School counselor What admissions readers look for; whether it adds to your application Limited time; ask early
    Parent or family member Whether it sounds like you; factual accuracy Rewriting in their voice; protectiveness about family details
    Friend Whether it's interesting and sounds like you Too much praise or too little honesty

    Two or three readers are usually enough. More than that, and you'll get conflicting advice that pulls the essay in too many directions. Ask specific questions instead of "Is it good?": What do you think this essay says about me? Where did you lose interest? Is there a sentence that doesn't sound like me? What's one thing you want to know more about?

    Above all, stay the author. Take feedback as information, not instructions. If someone's suggested edits start to sound like their writing instead of yours, that's a signal to pull back. Admissions readers can tell when an essay sounds adult-polished rather than teenager-honest, and it costs you their trust. Having someone else write or substantially rewrite your essay isn't just unwise. It's dishonest, and the Common App asks you to certify that your application is your own work.

    Revision¶

    Revision is the process of rethinking and reworking a draft: changing its content, structure, focus, or meaning, not just fixing sentences. It's where most of an essay's quality comes from. Revision is different from editing. Editing makes sentences correct. Revision makes the essay better.

    Revise in passes, from big to small:

    1. Big picture: Does the essay reveal what I want it to? Is the story the right one? Is there enough reflection?
    2. Structure: Does the order work? Does the opening hook? Is there a clear turn? Does the ending land?
    3. Scenes and detail: Where am I telling instead of showing? Where do I need a specific detail?
    4. Voice: Read it aloud. Which sentences don't sound like me?
    5. Length: Cut to 650 words, starting with general statements, repeated points, and long setup.

    When cutting, a useful rule is to remove anything the reader already knows or could guess. Cut sentences that restate the previous one, introductions that warm up before the essay starts, and lessons the reader already understood from the scene.

    Worked Example: Ana's Five Drafts¶

    Ana's essay goes through five versions over seven weeks:

    • Draft 1 (August 10, 910 words): the whole story in order, with a long introduction about her family and a one-sentence lesson at the end.
    • Draft 2 (August 24, 820 words): she cuts the introduction and starts on the kitchen floor. Her English teacher says the reflection is too general.
    • Draft 3 (September 7, 760 words): a new reflection about "the pause." Her father points out the sentence that doesn't sound like her.
    • Draft 4 (September 21, 690 words): a new hook that opens on the eleventh night, plus voice fixes. Her counselor says the essay adds something her application didn't show.
    • Draft 5 (October 1, 642 words): she cuts 48 words of repetition and tightens the ending.

    The story never changed after Draft 1. What changed was where it started, what it meant, and how it sounded. That's what revision is.

    Editing and Proofreading¶

    Editing and proofreading are the final checks of an essay, after revision is done, for errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation, word choice, and formatting. Editing looks at sentences: clarity, wordiness, and word choice. Proofreading is the last pass for typos and mechanical mistakes. Errors don't usually sink an essay, but they can distract a reader and suggest carelessness.

    Proofread in ways that make you see the words fresh. Read the essay aloud, slowly. Read it on paper or in a different font. Read it backward, sentence by sentence, to catch typos your brain auto-corrects. Have one careful reader do a final proofread. Then paste the essay into the Common App box and read it there, since formatting such as italics, paragraph breaks, and special characters sometimes changes when you paste. Check the word count in the box itself. Finally, save the exact submitted version in your essay drafts folder, labeled "FINAL — submitted."

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A college essay is any essay a college asks for. The personal essay is the main one, sent to every Common App college, and its job is to show the reader who you are in your own voice. Keep every version in an essay drafts folder.
    • The Common App essay prompts are broad on purpose. Choose your story first, then the prompt. The essay word limit is 650 words, enough for one story told well.
    • Brainstorming and a running essay material list collect specific moments. A good personal story is yours, specific, and revealing. A small moment essay can be the strongest kind.
    • Overused essay topics can work with a fresh, specific angle. Essay topic selection asks whether a story reveals something new, has scenes, has meaning, and makes you want to write.
    • Show, don't tell: let scenes prove your qualities, then reflect briefly. Keep an authentic voice by reading aloud.
    • Choose an essay structure (narrative arc, montage, or idea-driven), open with a specific opening hook, and give real space to reflection and insight.
    • Drafting fast and messy, feedback from two or three chosen readers, revision in passes from big to small, and careful editing and proofreading take an essay from blank page to final.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    Why is it usually better to choose your story before choosing a Common App prompt?

    The prompts are broad on purpose, and most good stories fit more than one. Starting with the prompt tempts you to write what you think it wants instead of what's true about you. Prompt 7 accepts any topic anyway.

    Rewrite this as showing: 'I am curious about how things work.'

    One possible answer: "By age ten, I had taken apart three toasters, two remote controls, and, by accident, my mom's hair dryer." Any revision that uses specific actions and details instead of naming the quality works.

    An essay's reflection reads: 'This experience taught me that hard work pays off.' What's wrong with it, and how would you improve it?

    It's a generic lesson that could follow any story. Improve it by naming a specific insight tied to this story, where it shows up in your life now, and anything still unresolved.

    Your draft is 890 words. What should you cut first?

    Start with general statements, long setup, and repeated points, including sentences that restate what the scene already showed. Protect the specific scenes and the reflection.

    A parent rewrites several of your sentences so they sound more polished. What's the risk?

    The essay may lose your authentic voice and sound adult-written, which costs readers' trust. Substantial rewriting by someone else is also dishonest. Take feedback as information, and keep the writing yours.
