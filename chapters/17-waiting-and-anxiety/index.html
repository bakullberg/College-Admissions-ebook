    Waiting and Managing Anxiety¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers what happens to an application after submission and healthy ways to get through the wait, from portal checking to limiting comparison, self-care, and support. It covers the part of the process students control least. After this chapter, students understand how applications are reviewed and have concrete strategies for managing uncertainty.

    The Part You Can't Control¶

    For months, the college process has been a list of things to do. Then you submit your last application, and the list stops. For most students, that's when it gets hardest. The work is done, the outcome is out of your hands, and the decisions won't arrive for weeks or months. Many students find the waiting harder than the writing.

    This chapter covers that stretch. It starts with what actually happens to your application after you submit it, so the wait feels less like a black box, and with the signals you'll see along the way: your application status, your portals, and decision release dates. Then it turns to you, with practical ways to manage uncertainty, cope in healthy ways, keep comparison and social media in check, take care of yourself, lean on the people around you, and mark the milestones you've already reached.

    What Happens After You Submit¶

    The Application Review Process¶

    The application review process is the sequence of steps an admissions office follows from the moment your application arrives to the moment a decision is released. Chapter 1 traced one file's journey through the office, and Chapter 2 explained how readers evaluate it. Here's what that looks like on the calendar:

    1. Processing (days to a few weeks). Operations staff match your application, transcript, school report, recommendations, and scores to your file and mark it "complete." In the busy weeks around January deadlines, this alone can take two or three weeks.
    2. First read (weeks to months). Usually your regional admissions officer reads your full file in the context of your high school, writes a summary, and assigns ratings. At formula-based universities, many files are decided by formula at this stage.
    3. Committee review. At many colleges, a committee discusses files, especially close calls, and your first reader may advocate for you.
    4. Shaping the class. Late in the process, the office balances the whole class by major, region, and institutional priorities (Chapter 2). This is why strong applicants are sometimes turned down for reasons that have nothing to do with anything they did.
    5. Decisions released on the college's published date, or on a rolling basis.

    Two facts make the wait easier to live with. First, nothing about your admissions file changes after you submit, except your mid-year grades and, rarely, an update you send. The waiting period really is just waiting. More worrying doesn't move the outcome. Second, the timeline is long on purpose. Reading tens of thousands of files carefully takes months. A long wait isn't a sign that something's wrong with your application.

    There are still a few tasks during the wait. They just aren't admissions tasks. Your counselor sends your mid-year report. You may need to complete financial aid forms or verification (Chapter 14). Scholarship deadlines continue (Chapter 15). And you still have senior-year classes, which matter as much as ever (Chapter 7). Keep those on your tracker so you're not left with nothing but the waiting.

    Worked Example: Emily's Calendar After Submission¶

    Emily submits her last Regular Decision application on January 9. She maps what happens next:

    When What's happening Anything for Emily to do?
    Mid-December (already passed) College C's Early Action decision: admitted Compare later
    January Files processed and marked complete Check each portal once, about a week after each deadline
    Late January–February Mid-year report sent by counselor; reading season Keep grades up; finish CSS Profile and verification if asked
    February–March Committees meet; class is shaped Apply for spring scholarships
    Mid-March–early April Regular Decision releases Read each decision; start comparing award letters
    May 1 Commitment deadline Choose (Chapter 19)

    Seeing the calendar helps. Emily realizes that from mid-January to mid-March, nothing at all will happen with her admissions decisions, and she can stop checking for news that won't come.

    Application Status¶

    Your application status is the current state of your application at a college, shown in its applicant portal, such as "submitted," "incomplete — missing documents," "complete — under review," or "decision available." The most important status to watch for is incomplete. A missing transcript, score report, or recommendation can hold your file out of review until it arrives. Check each college's status about a week after you submit and again after its supporting document deadline. Once it says "complete" or "under review," there's nothing more to watch for until decisions. A status that doesn't change for weeks is normal. It doesn't mean your file is being ignored or that the news is bad.

    Checking Portals Regularly¶

    Checking portals regularly means logging in to each college's applicant portal on a sensible schedule to catch missing documents, requests for information, and decisions, without checking compulsively. A good rhythm is once a week until every file is complete, then once a week or less during reading season, plus checking the morning of each published decision date. Daily or hourly checking rarely turns up anything new. It mostly produces anxiety. Most colleges also email you when something changes, so make sure portal and college emails reach the dedicated inbox from Chapter 5 and aren't filtered as spam.

    Decision Release Dates¶

    A decision release date is the day, and often the exact time, a college publishes admission decisions for a round, usually in the applicant portal. Most colleges announce their dates in advance on their admissions websites or in an email to applicants. Typical windows follow the rounds from Chapter 1: mid-December for Early Decision and many Early Action rounds, January to February for some Early Action rounds, and mid-March to early April for Regular Decision. Rolling admission colleges release decisions continuously, often within a few weeks of a complete application.

    Knowing the dates turns an open-ended wait into a schedule. Put each college's release date in your calendar as it's announced. And decide in advance where and with whom you'll open each decision. Some students want family nearby. Others prefer privacy and share news afterward. Avoid opening decisions in the middle of a school day if a disappointing result would be hard to absorb in public.

    Worked Example: Planning the Big Weeks¶

    By early March, Marcus has four Regular Decision release dates announced, all within nine days of each other in late March. He makes a plan:

    • He'll check each portal the evening of its release date, at home, after dinner. Not in class, not on the bus.
    • His parents will be home for the two dates he's most nervous about.
    • He'll wait to post anything online until he's heard from all four colleges.
    • The Saturday after the last release, he and his cousin have tickets to a basketball game, whatever happens.

    The plan doesn't change any decision. It changes how those nine days feel: less like waiting for a verdict, more like a schedule he's chosen.

    Getting Through the Wait¶

    Managing Uncertainty¶

    Managing uncertainty means accepting that some outcomes are out of your control and focusing your energy on what you can still influence. Uncertainty is the default condition of applying to college, not a sign that you're doing something wrong. Even students with excellent applications don't know what will happen, because decisions depend on the whole applicant pool and each college's needs that year.

    A useful habit is to sort your worries into two piles. The first pile is what you can still control: your grades, your financial aid forms, your scholarship applications, how you spend your time, and how you treat yourself. The second pile is what you can't: who else applied, how a committee reads your file, what a college needs this year. Give your energy to the first pile and let the second one go, as well as you can. That doesn't mean you won't care. It means caring doesn't have to take over your life.

    It also helps to prepare for every outcome in advance, not just the best one. Revisit your college list and remind yourself why each target and safety is on it (Chapter 3). If you'd be genuinely glad to attend several of your colleges, no single decision can take everything from you. The sorter below lets you practice dividing worries this way.

    The Waiting Period¶

    The waiting period is the stretch between submitting your applications and receiving decisions. It's usually about four to eight weeks for early rounds, and two to three months for Regular Decision. It's a strange time. The application work that filled your fall is over, but the outcome is still unknown. Many students describe it as feeling stuck. They aren't busy enough to forget about college, and they can't do anything to change it.

    The waiting period is easier when you treat it as part of senior year, not a pause before it. It's the last winter and spring you'll spend with these friends, these teachers, and your family under one roof. Students who fill it with things unrelated to admissions, such as a sport, a job, a new hobby, time with friends, or a project they've put off, tend to look back on it more kindly than students who spent it refreshing portals. That's not avoidance. It's accurate, because there's nothing left to do about your applications.

    Worked Example: Ana's Winter¶

    After submitting her last application in early January, Ana notices she's checking her phone constantly and sleeping badly. She talks with her counselor, who suggests she plan her winter on purpose. Ana commits to three things:

    • Tutoring at the church two afternoons a week, which she'd cut back on during application season.
    • A Friday movie night with her brothers.
    • One hour on Sunday for "college tasks only": checking portals, scholarship applications, and aid forms. She doesn't check them at any other time.

    By February, the Sunday hour is usually enough, and the rest of the week belongs to her life again. She still thinks about decisions, but they no longer fill every day.

    Healthy Coping Strategies¶

    Healthy coping strategies are the habits that help you handle stress and uncertainty in ways that support your well-being rather than harm it. Unhealthy coping, such as constant checking, endless comparison, skipping sleep, withdrawing from people, or numbing stress with screens or substances, tends to make anxiety worse over time. Healthy coping includes:

    • Structure: a predictable routine, with set times for college tasks and everything else.
    • Movement: exercise, sports, walks, dancing, anything that gets you moving.
    • Connection: time with friends and family, especially in person.
    • Absorption: activities that hold your full attention, such as music, art, games, building things, or reading.
    • Perspective: writing down worries, talking them through, or reminding yourself of the options you already have.
    • Help: reaching out when stress becomes too much.

    Different strategies work for different people. Try a few, and notice which ones actually leave you feeling better afterward, not just distracted in the moment.

    Limiting Comparison¶

    Limiting comparison means deliberately reducing how much you measure your process and results against other students'. During the waiting period, comparison is everywhere: who applied where, who heard back, who got in early. It's almost always misleading. You see only part of anyone else's story. Their colleges, finances, priorities, and applications are different from yours. And a classmate's acceptance doesn't lower your chances, since you weren't competing for the same seat in the way it feels. Notice when a conversation turns into a scoreboard, and step out of it. A simple line helps: "I'm trying not to talk about decisions until I hear from everywhere. How's your week?"

    Social Media Boundaries¶

    Social media boundaries are limits you set on what you see and share online during the admissions process. Social media magnifies comparison. Acceptances get posted, rejections rarely do, and online communities devoted to admissions results can make anyone feel behind. Consider muting admissions-related accounts, forums, and hashtags during the waiting period, and setting time limits on apps that leave you anxious. When your own decisions arrive, think before posting. A celebration is fine, but remember that friends may be opening disappointing news the same day. Many students wait until they've heard from all their colleges.

    Self-Care Routine¶

    A self-care routine is a regular set of habits that protect your physical and mental health: sleep, food, movement, and time to rest and enjoy things. It sounds basic, and it is, which is why it slips first under stress. Aim for consistent sleep, at least eight hours for most teenagers, plus regular meals, some daily movement, and time each week for something you enjoy just for itself. A routine matters most on hard days. When a disappointing decision arrives, the routine is what keeps you steady.

    Support Network¶

    Your support network is the people you can lean on during stressful times: family, friends, teachers, your school counselor, coaches, mentors, faith communities, and professional counselors or doctors. Talk about the anxiety instead of just sitting with it. Most people are glad to help, and friends going through the same wait often understand best. Tell your family what kind of support you want. Some students want parents to ask about decisions. Others want them to wait to be told. If stress becomes overwhelming, or you notice signs like persistent hopelessness, trouble functioning, or thoughts of harming yourself, reach out right away to a trusted adult, your counselor, or a doctor. In the United States, you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time for free, confidential support.

    Celebrating Milestones¶

    Celebrating milestones means deliberately marking the progress you've made, not just the final outcome. Submitting your applications is a real accomplishment. So is finishing your FAFSA, writing an essay you're proud of, or getting through a hard week. Students often save all their celebrating for acceptances, which makes the whole process feel like a test with only one grade. Mark the milestones along the way. Go out to dinner the night you submit your last application, tell your recommenders thank you, or simply notice how much you've done since August. Whatever the decisions say, the work you did to get here was real.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • The application review process runs from processing through reading, committee, and class-shaping to release. It takes months, and nothing in your file changes during the wait.
    • Watch your application status for "incomplete." Check portals regularly but sensibly, about once a week, plus release dates.
    • Put each decision release date on your calendar, and plan where and with whom you'll open decisions.
    • Managing uncertainty means sorting worries into what you can and can't control, and preparing for every outcome.
    • Treat the waiting period as part of senior year. Use healthy coping strategies, practice limiting comparison, set social media boundaries, and keep a self-care routine.
    • Lean on your support network, and reach out for help if stress becomes too much (988 in the U.S.).
    • Celebrate milestones along the way, not just acceptances.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    Your portal has said 'complete — under review' for six weeks. Is that a bad sign?

    No. Reading season takes months, and status often doesn't change until decisions are released. "Complete" means there's nothing more for you to do.

    A classmate posts an Early Action acceptance to a college where you applied Regular Decision. Does that affect your chances?

    Not in any way you can measure. Decisions depend on the whole pool and the college's needs. Comparing yourself to one classmate mostly adds stress.

    Sort these into 'can control' and 'can't control': your mid-year grades; how many students applied; finishing your CSS Profile; how a committee reads your essay.

    Can control: mid-year grades, finishing the CSS Profile. Can't control: how many students applied, how a committee reads your essay.

    How often should you check applicant portals during reading season?

    About once a week, plus the morning of each release date. More frequent checking rarely shows anything new and tends to increase anxiety.

    Name two healthy coping strategies and one unhealthy one.

    Healthy: a routine with set times for college tasks, exercise, time with friends, absorbing hobbies, talking worries through. Unhealthy: constant portal checking, endless comparison, skipping sleep, withdrawing from people.
