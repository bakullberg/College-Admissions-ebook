    Quiz: Waiting and Managing Anxiety¶

    Test your understanding of what happens after you submit and healthy ways to get through the wait with these review questions.

    1. About how long does the chapter say the waiting period lasts for Regular Decision applicants?¶

    1. About one week
    2. About four to eight days
    3. About six months
    4. About two to three months
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The waiting period is the stretch between submitting applications and receiving decisions. The chapter says it's usually about four to eight weeks for early rounds and two to three months for Regular Decision. The long timeline is deliberate, since reading tens of thousands of files carefully takes months. A long wait isn't a sign that something's wrong with your application.

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    2. Which application status does the chapter call the most important one to watch for?¶

    1. Submitted
    2. Complete — under review
    3. Incomplete
    4. Decision available
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter says the most important status to watch for is "incomplete," because a missing transcript, score report, or recommendation can hold your file out of review until it arrives. Check each college's status about a week after you submit and again after its supporting document deadline. Once a file says "complete" or "under review," there's nothing more to watch for until decisions are released.

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    3. How much sleep does the chapter suggest most teenagers aim for as part of a self-care routine?¶

    1. At least eight hours
    2. Exactly ten hours
    3. At least five hours
    4. At least six hours
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A self-care routine protects your physical and mental health through sleep, food, movement, and time to rest. The chapter suggests consistent sleep of at least eight hours for most teenagers, plus regular meals, daily movement, and time each week for something you enjoy. It notes that self-care slips first under stress, and that a routine matters most on hard days, such as when a disappointing decision arrives.

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    4. According to the chapter, what fact about your file can make the waiting period easier to live with?¶

    1. Colleges let you rewrite your essays during reading season
    2. Checking your portal often can speed up the review
    3. Your file is decided within a few days of submission
    4. Nothing in your file changes after you submit, except mid-year grades and rare updates
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter says nothing about your admissions file changes after you submit, except your mid-year grades and, rarely, an update you send. The waiting period really is just waiting, so more worrying doesn't move the outcome. Review takes months, not days, and checking a portal more often doesn't speed anything up. Essays can't be rewritten after submission. Your remaining tasks are aid forms, scholarships, and senior classes.

    Concept Tested: Application Review Process

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    5. Why does the chapter say comparing yourself with classmates during the wait is almost always misleading?¶

    1. Comparison is useful only after May 1
    2. You see only part of anyone else's story, and their colleges and priorities differ from yours
    3. Colleges admit only one student from each high school
    4. Classmates usually lie about where they applied
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that comparison is misleading because you see only part of anyone else's story, and their colleges, finances, priorities, and applications are different from yours. A classmate's acceptance doesn't lower your chances, since you weren't competing for the same seat in the way it feels. The chapter doesn't claim classmates lie or that colleges take one student per school. It suggests stepping out of scoreboard conversations.

    Concept Tested: Limiting Comparison

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    6. Why does the chapter encourage celebrating milestones, not just acceptances?¶

    1. Celebrating early guarantees better decisions
    2. Saving all celebration for acceptances makes the process feel like a test with only one grade
    3. Colleges ask applicants to report their milestones
    4. Milestones replace the need for a support network
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says students often save all their celebrating for acceptances, which makes the whole process feel like a test with only one grade. Submitting applications, finishing the FAFSA, and writing an essay you're proud of are real accomplishments worth marking. Celebrating doesn't change any decision, and it doesn't replace leaning on your support network. Whatever the decisions say, the work you did to get here was real.

    Concept Tested: Celebrating Milestones

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    7. A student has three Regular Decision release dates in the same week. Which plan best follows the chapter's advice?¶

    1. Open each decision during lunch at school, so friends can react together
    2. Check every portal hourly that week, starting several days early
    3. Open each decision at home in the evening with chosen people nearby, and plan something fun afterward
    4. Post each result online immediately, before hearing from the others
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter suggests putting each release date on your calendar and deciding in advance where and with whom you'll open each decision. It advises avoiding the middle of a school day if bad news would be hard to absorb in public. Marcus's plan, opening decisions at home after dinner, with parents there for the big ones and a game afterward, is the model. Hourly checking mostly produces anxiety, and many students wait to post until they've heard from everywhere.

    Concept Tested: Decision Release Dates

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    8. Lee is admitted to a top-choice college on a day when several friends are also getting decisions. What does the chapter's advice on social media suggest?¶

    1. Post the decision in admissions forums to compare results
    2. Post right away, since acceptances encourage everyone
    3. Think before posting, since friends may be opening disappointing news the same day
    4. Never tell anyone about the acceptance until graduation
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Social media boundaries are limits on what you see and share during the process. The chapter says a celebration is fine, but to think before posting, because friends may be opening disappointing news the same day, and many students wait until they've heard from all their colleges. Admissions forums and results communities magnify comparison, so the chapter suggests muting them. It doesn't say to keep good news secret forever.

    Concept Tested: Social Media Boundaries

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    9. A student sorts worries into "can control" and "can't control." Which item belongs in the "can control" pile?¶

    1. Finishing spring scholarship applications
    2. How a committee member reacts to an essay
    3. How many other students applied this year
    4. Which majors a college needs to fill this year
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Managing uncertainty means sorting worries into what you can still control and what you can't, then giving your energy to the first pile. The chapter lists grades, financial aid forms, scholarship applications, how you spend your time, and how you treat yourself as things you control. Who else applied, how a committee reads your file, and what a college needs this year are all out of your hands.

    Concept Tested: Managing Uncertainty

    See: Waiting and Managing Anxiety

    10. Student 1 spends the winter refreshing portals, skipping sleep, and staying home alone. Student 2 sets one weekly hour for college tasks, joins a pickup soccer game, and has dinner with friends on Fridays. Which analysis fits the chapter?¶

    1. Student 2 uses healthy coping, while Student 1's habits tend to make anxiety worse
    2. Student 1 is more prepared, since checking portals catches decisions sooner
    3. Both students will feel the same, since anxiety can't be managed
    4. Student 2 is avoiding the process, which the chapter warns against
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Healthy coping strategies include structure, movement, connection, and absorbing activities. Student 2 has all of these, much like Ana's planned winter. The chapter lists constant checking, skipping sleep, and withdrawing from people as unhealthy coping that tends to make anxiety worse. It also says filling the wait with life outside admissions isn't avoidance, since there's nothing left to do about your applications, and frequent checking rarely shows anything new.
