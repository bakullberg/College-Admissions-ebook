    3. 20. Getting Ready for Fall

    Quiz: Getting Ready for Fall¶

    Test your understanding of the steps from deposit to move-in, avoiding summer melt, and pulling the whole process together with these review questions.

    1. What is a living learning community?¶

    1. An online forum where admitted students find roommates
    2. A student health plan offered to students who live on campus
    3. A residence hall floor or building where students who share an academic interest or identity live together
    4. A summer program that replaces orientation for first-year students
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A living learning community, or LLC, is a residence hall floor or building where students who share an academic interest or identity live together, often with linked courses, faculty involvement, and special events. Examples include communities for engineering, the arts, first-generation students, honors, or future teachers. The chapter says LLCs can help first-year students feel at home faster, and they often require a short spring application through the housing portal.

    2. Which subjects most often have placement tests, and when are they usually taken?¶

    1. Only subjects without AP credit, usually after the first semester
    2. Only English, usually as part of the housing application
    3. Every subject in the major, usually during the first week of classes
    4. Math, world languages, chemistry, and sometimes writing, usually online before orientation
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Placement tests help a college place new students at the right level, most often in math, world languages, chemistry, and sometimes writing. They're usually taken online before orientation. They don't affect admission, but they matter, since placing higher can save a semester and placing too high can mean struggling. AP scores and transfer credit may exempt you from some placement tests, so check first.

    Concept Tested: Placement Tests

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    3. Why does the chapter urge students to register for the earliest orientation date that works?¶

    1. Course registration often happens at orientation, so a late date can mean popular sections are full
    2. Early orientation dates come with a larger merit scholarship
    3. Orientation is optional, so early dates are only for social events
    4. Students who attend early orientation skip placement tests
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Orientation prepares new students for their first semester and typically includes advising, course registration, placement testing, and campus tours. The chapter stresses that orientation isn't optional and isn't just social. At many colleges it's where you register for first-semester classes, so a late orientation date can mean registering after popular sections fill. Placement tests are usually taken online before orientation, and orientation dates don't affect scholarships.

    Concept Tested: Orientation

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    4. Why should a family decide about health insurance before the college's waiver deadline?¶

    1. Health insurance is required only for students in living learning communities
    2. The waiver deadline is also the deadline for the housing application
    3. Many colleges charge for their student health plan automatically unless it's waived by a deadline
    4. Students without the college's plan can't move into housing
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Colleges require proof of health insurance or enrollment in the college's student health plan. The chapter says many colleges charge for their plan automatically unless you waive it by a deadline, so families keeping their own insurance need to act in time. Housing depends on health forms and immunizations, not on choosing the college's plan. The chapter doesn't tie insurance to living learning communities or to the housing application deadline.

    Concept Tested: Health Forms and Immunizations

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    5. Kai committed in May, took three AP exams, and earned credit in a dual-enrollment course at a community college. What should Kai do so the credit counts before registration?¶

    1. List the AP scores on the housing application instead of sending a report
    2. Send only the AP scores, since dual-enrollment credit transfers automatically
    3. Send an official AP score report, using the free send by June 20, and request the community college's official transcript
    4. Wait for the college to request AP scores after the first semester begins
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. AP credit transfer means getting AP, IB, and dual-enrollment credit recognized by your college. Send your official AP score report from the College Board, using the one free score send if you choose your college by June 20, and request an official transcript from the college that granted dual-enrollment credit. Then confirm in your portal how each was applied. Credit that hasn't posted by registration can mean signing up for a class you didn't need.

    Concept Tested: AP Credit Transfer

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    6. A student's renewable scholarship requires full-time enrollment of at least 12 credits. While drafting a first-semester schedule, the student considers taking 10 credits to ease the transition. What does the chapter's advice suggest?¶

    1. Plan at least 12 credits, with backup sections, to keep the scholarship
    2. Skip registration until the scholarship office contacts the student
    3. Take 10 credits, since scholarship rules don't apply in the first semester
    4. Take 20 credits, to get ahead before any aid is checked
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says to keep scholarship and aid requirements in mind when registering, since many require full-time enrollment. A typical first semester is 12 to 16 credits, so planning at least 12 keeps the award while staying in a normal range. The chapter also advises drafting backup choices, since some sections fill. Dropping below 12 risks losing the scholarship, and an overloaded 20-credit schedule works against a balanced first semester.

    Concept Tested: Course Registration

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    7. A student committed to the University of Iowa wants to choose a room online in June rather than be assigned one by staff. Which plan meets Iowa's requirements described in the chapter?¶

    1. Wait to apply for housing until the final transcript is sent
    2. Submit the housing application in August, just before On Iowa!
    3. Schedule orientation first, then apply for housing in July
    4. Finish the housing application by May 1 and schedule orientation by May 15
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter says that at Iowa, students who finish their housing application by May 1 and schedule orientation by May 15 can choose their own rooms online in June, while students who apply later are assigned by staff. Earlier applicants also get earlier room selection times. Casey submitted the housing application in January, right after admission. Applying in July or August would miss the window, and housing doesn't wait on the final transcript.

    Concept Tested: Housing Application

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    8. In July, Ana got a bill $1,800 higher than expected and briefly wondered whether to enroll at all. One call revealed a missing verification document had kept her state grant from posting. What does this example show about summer melt?¶

    1. Most students who melt have changed their minds about college
    2. Summer melt usually comes from small, fixable obstacles that feel like dead ends without help
    3. Unexpected bills are a sign the college has withdrawn the student's aid
    4. Students should wait for colleges to fix billing errors on their own
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says summer melt usually isn't a change of heart. It's a pile-up of small obstacles, such as a missed email, an unexpected bill, or a verification request, without a counselor nearby, since school is out. Ana's bill felt like a dead end but took ten minutes to diagnose. The grant hadn't been withdrawn, only delayed. The protection is to respond quickly and know who to call, rather than waiting.

    9. A student builds a roadmap for a private college that, unlike Iowa, requires the CSS Profile, two supplemental essays, and teacher recommendations. Using the capstone roadmap, what does the chapter suggest this student do?¶

    1. Start over and relearn the process, since private colleges work differently
    2. Keep the same sequence of steps, but find this college's version of each, such as its aid forms and supplements
    3. Copy Iowa's roadmap exactly, since every college follows the same steps and deadlines
    4. Skip the steps Iowa didn't require, since they are optional everywhere
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The capstone application roadmap applies the whole process to one real college. The chapter says the sequence doesn't change. Once you understand why each step exists, applying it to any college is a matter of finding that college's version of each step, not relearning the process. This college adds the CSS Profile, supplements, and recommendations that Iowa doesn't require. Copying Iowa's specifics would miss those, and the requirements aren't optional just because Iowa didn't have them.

    Concept Tested: Capstone Application Roadmap

    See: Getting Ready for Fall

    10. A student needs a college's housing deadline. A friend who enrolled last year says it's June 1, this book's example lists a different date, and the college's housing website for this year says May 15. Which source should the student rely on?¶

    1. The friend, since they went through the process most recently
    2. The book, since its facts were checked carefully
    3. Whichever date is latest, to leave the most time
    4. The college's website for the current year, recording the date and when it was confirmed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Verifying current policies means checking each college's rules and deadlines for your own application year, not relying on last year's information, a friend's experience, or even a book. The chapter says policies change constantly, so confirm dates on the college's own website or studentaid.gov, email the office when in doubt, and record what you confirmed and when. Choosing the latest date could mean missing the real deadline and losing housing options.

    Concept Tested: Verifying Current Policies
