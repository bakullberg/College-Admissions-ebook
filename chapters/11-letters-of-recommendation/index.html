    Letters of Recommendation¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers choosing and asking teacher and counselor recommenders, preparing a brag sheet and recommender packet, the FERPA waiver, and following up with thanks. It shows students how to make it easy for recommenders to write a strong letter. After this chapter, students can choose recommenders, ask at the right time, and hand over everything a writer needs.

    Someone Else's Words About You¶

    Almost everything in your application comes from you: your activities list, your essays, your self-reported courses. Recommendation letters are different. They're other people's words about you, written by adults who have watched you learn and work. That's what makes them valuable to readers, and also what makes them feel out of your control.

    You can't write your own letters, and you shouldn't try to see them. But you have far more influence over them than most students realize. You choose who writes them. You choose when to ask and how. You also give your recommenders the material they need, so they can write a specific, vivid letter instead of a generic one.

    This chapter covers what recommendation letters are and the two kinds most colleges ask for, teacher and counselor letters. Then it walks through the process step by step: choosing recommenders, timing your request, asking well, preparing a brag sheet and recommender packet, tracking deadlines, signing the FERPA waiver, and saying thank you. It ends with the optional additional recommendation, and when one helps.

    Letters of Recommendation¶

    Letters of recommendation are written evaluations of you from teachers, your school counselor, and sometimes other adults, submitted directly to colleges as part of your application. Most selective colleges ask for one or two teacher letters plus a counselor letter. Many less selective and formula-based colleges ask for none. Iowa, for example, doesn't require letters for general admission. Check each college's requirements and record them in your tracker, as Chapter 5 described.

    Letters matter because they give readers outside confirmation of who you are. Your transcript says you earned an A in chemistry. A teacher's letter can say that you were the student who asked why the textbook's explanation didn't match the lab results, then stayed after class to work it out. Holistic readers, as Chapter 2 described, often use letters to inform the "personal qualities" part of their rating.

    The strongest letters share one trait: specificity. They describe particular moments, projects, and habits that only someone who knows you could describe. A letter that says "Priya is a hardworking, intelligent student" tells a reader almost nothing, since nearly every letter says something similar. A letter that describes how Priya rewrote a classmate's struggling thesis statement in a peer workshop without taking it over tells them a lot.

    You can't control what a recommender writes. But you can make specificity much more likely by choosing someone who knows you well and giving them concrete material to work with. That's the strategy behind everything in this chapter.

    Worked Example: Generic and Specific Letters¶

    Here are two passages a reader might see about the same student.

    Generic: "Marcus is one of the most talented students I have taught. He is diligent, intelligent, and a pleasure to have in class. I recommend him without reservation."

    Specific: "When our robotics team's navigation code failed two days before regionals, Marcus didn't rewrite it himself. He sat with the two sophomores who'd written it and asked them questions until they found the bug on their own. Then he stayed until 9 p.m. to help them test it."

    Both passages are positive. Only the second shows the reader something they'll remember: leadership that builds other people up. Notice that the specific letter depends on the teacher having seen that moment, or having been reminded of it. That's what a brag sheet is for.

    Teacher Recommendations¶

    A teacher recommendation is a letter from a teacher who has taught you, usually in a core academic subject, describing you as a student and classmate. Teachers can speak to things no other part of your application shows: how you think in discussion, how you handle difficulty, how you treat classmates, and how you grew over a year.

    Most colleges that require teacher letters ask for one or two, and many prefer teachers from core academic subjects, which are English, math, science, social studies, and world languages, from 11th or 12th grade. Some colleges, especially for engineering or arts programs, ask that one letter come from a teacher in a related subject. On the Common App, teachers complete a short form rating you on several qualities and then upload a letter. You'll invite them through the application, as Chapter 12 explains.

    Worked Example: What a Teacher Can See¶

    Ana's English teacher, Ms. Ortiz, taught her in 11th grade. In her letter, Ms. Ortiz can describe things that don't appear anywhere else in Ana's application:

    • In a unit on Their Eyes Were Watching God, Ana noticed that three quieter classmates hadn't spoken all week and asked whether the class could try small-group discussions.
    • Ana's essays improved sharply between September and March, and her revision notes showed she read every comment.
    • Ana translated a permission form for a classmate's parents without being asked.

    Each of those details confirms a theme of Ana's application, noticing confusion and helping people understand. That's why a letter from a teacher who knows her well is so valuable.

    The Counselor Recommendation¶

    The counselor recommendation is a letter from your school counselor that places you in the context of your school and your life, usually submitted along with the school report and your transcript. Colleges that require letters almost always require it. Unlike teachers, counselors see your whole high school record: your course choices compared with what the school offers (including the course-rigor rating from Chapter 2), your activities, and any circumstances at home or at school that affected your path.

    The counselor letter is especially important for explaining context: a family responsibility, a move, an illness, a change in grades, or the fact that you're the first in your family to apply to college. Counselors who work with hundreds of students, as many do, need your help to write a personal letter. Meet with your counselor (Chapter 5), complete any questionnaire or brag sheet they provide, and tell them anything you'd like colleges to understand.

    Choosing and Asking¶

    Choosing Recommenders¶

    Choosing recommenders means deciding which teachers, and occasionally which other adults, you'll ask to write letters. The most important rule is simple: choose the teacher who knows you best, not the one who gave you the highest grade. A B+ student a teacher can describe in vivid detail gets a stronger letter than an A student the teacher barely remembers.

    Weigh these factors:

    • How well they know you. Have you talked with them outside class, sought their help, or worked on a project with them?
    • Recency. A junior-year teacher has seen your most recent and mature work. A teacher from earlier can work if they know you exceptionally well, especially if they taught you again later.
    • Subject variety. If you need two letters, choose teachers from different areas, such as one humanities and one STEM, so the letters show different sides of you.
    • Relevance to your goals. If you're applying to engineering, a math or science letter makes sense. If you're applying undecided, choose the two who know you best.
    • Their reliability. A teacher known for meeting deadlines and writing thoughtful letters is worth a lot. Your counselor or older students can tell you who they are.

    Worked Example: Priya Chooses Two Teachers¶

    Priya considers four teachers:

    Teacher Subject and year Grade Knows her well? Notes
    Mr. Okafor AP U.S. History, 11th A Yes. She went to his office hours weekly and he advised her research paper. Taught the paper she's submitting to one college
    Ms. Chen AP English Language, 11th A Somewhat. Large class, few one-on-one talks. Also the literary magazine's faculty advisor
    Mr. Grant AP Chemistry, 11th B Yes. She struggled, came for help often, and improved a lot. Can speak to persistence
    Mrs. Diaz Spanish III, 11th A Somewhat Good relationship, little depth

    Priya's instinct is to ask Ms. Chen, since English is her intended major. But Mr. Okafor knows her work best, and Mr. Grant can describe her persistence in her hardest class, which shows a side of her that her A's don't. She asks Mr. Okafor and Mr. Grant. Ms. Chen still knows her through the literary magazine, so Priya keeps her in mind as a possible additional recommender. The interactive below lets you compare your own options the same way.

    Recommendation Request Timing¶

    Recommendation request timing is when you ask teachers to write your letters. Ask in the spring of junior year or at the very start of senior year, well before fall deadlines. Teachers who write letters often receive many requests, and some limit how many they accept. The earliest askers get the most time and attention, and asking in spring also gives teachers the summer to think. A good rule is to ask at least six to eight weeks before your first deadline. For a November 1 Early Action application, that means mid-September at the latest. Chapter 1's worked example on planning backward from a deadline showed exactly this. If your school has its own timeline for requests, such as a counselor's form due in May, follow it.

    Asking for a Recommendation¶

    Asking for a recommendation is the conversation, ideally in person, in which you request a letter from a teacher. Keep it simple and direct. Say you'd value their recommendation, and explain why them in a specific sentence: what you think they can speak to. Then give them an easy way to decline. Ask, "Would you feel comfortable writing me a strong recommendation?" That gives a teacher who doesn't know you well enough room to say so gracefully. You want a letter from someone who says yes with enthusiasm, not one who feels obligated. Follow up the same day with a short email confirming the request and saying when you'll send your materials.

    Worked Example: Priya Asks Mr. Grant¶

    In May of junior year, Priya stops by Mr. Grant's room after class:

    "Mr. Grant, I'm applying to colleges this fall, and I'd really value a recommendation from you. AP Chem was my hardest class, and you saw me work through it, especially stoichiometry, where I came in four or five times. I think you could speak to how I handle things that don't come easily. Would you feel comfortable writing me a strong recommendation?"

    Mr. Grant says yes. That evening, Priya sends a short email thanking him, saying her first deadline is November 1, and telling him she'll send a brag sheet and list of colleges by the end of August.

    Giving Recommenders What They Need¶

    The Brag Sheet¶

    A brag sheet is a short document, usually one or two pages, that you give a recommender to remind them of your accomplishments, your goals, and specific moments from their class or activity that they might mention. It builds on your activities resume from Chapter 8, but it's tailored to each recommender. Many counselors provide a brag sheet template. If yours doesn't, you can write your own.

    A useful brag sheet for a teacher includes:

    • Two or three specific moments from their class you're proud of, such as a project, a breakthrough, a discussion, or a time you helped someone.
    • What you learned or how you grew in the class.
    • Your goals: intended major, kinds of colleges, and what you hope to study.
    • Anything you'd like them to know that might help them connect their letter to the rest of your application, such as a theme from your essay.

    Write it in plain language, and keep it brief. Teachers are busy, and the goal is to jog their memory with specifics, not to write the letter for them.

    Worked Example: A Brag Sheet Entry¶

    Here's the "moments" section of Priya's brag sheet for Mr. Grant:

    Moments from AP Chemistry I'm proud of:

    • Stoichiometry, October. I failed the first quiz (58%). I came to your room four mornings that month, and you had me explain each step out loud. I got an 89 on the unit test.
    • Lab report on reaction rates, February. My group's results didn't match the expected curve. Instead of adjusting the numbers, I suggested we write about why: our water bath temperature drifted. You read part of our discussion section to the class.
    • Study group, spring. I started a Thursday study group for the AP exam, with six classmates.

    Each entry gives Mr. Grant a specific, accurate detail he can use, and a reason to remember Priya as more than a B on a gradebook.

    Recommender Deadlines¶

    Recommender deadlines are the dates by which your recommenders must submit their letters for each college. They're usually the college's supporting document deadline, as Chapter 5 described, which can be a week or two after the application deadline. Give each recommender a list of your colleges with each deadline, and ask them to submit by your earliest deadline whenever possible. Most letters submitted through the Common App go to all the colleges you've assigned at once, so one early submission covers everything. Track each letter's status in your tracker, and send a polite check-in a week or two before each deadline if it hasn't been submitted. Teachers manage dozens of letters, so a gentle reminder is helpful, not rude.

    The Recommender Packet¶

    The recommender packet is the complete set of materials you give each recommender at once: a short cover note thanking them, your brag sheet for them, your activities resume, a list of your colleges with deadlines and how letters will be submitted, and any school-specific forms. Packaging everything together saves recommenders from having to search through separate emails and makes it far more likely they'll write a strong letter on time. Send it as one email with attachments, or as one shared folder, by the date you promised when you asked, ideally at least four to six weeks before your first deadline. Keep a copy in your filing system's Recommendations folder (Chapter 5).

    Worked Example: Priya's Packet for Mr. Grant¶

    On August 28, Priya emails Mr. Grant one message, "Recommendation materials — Priya Raman," with:

    1. A three-sentence note thanking him again.
    2. Her brag sheet for AP Chemistry (one page).
    3. Her activities resume (one page).
    4. A table of the six colleges that require teacher letters, each with its deadline, starting with the earliest (November 1).
    5. A note that letters are submitted through the Common App, and that he'll get an invitation email in early September.

    She records "Packet sent Aug 28" in her tracker and sets a reminder for October 18 to check whether his letter appears in the Common App as submitted.

    The FERPA Waiver¶

    The FERPA waiver is a choice you make on your application about whether to give up your right to see your recommendation letters in the future. FERPA, the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, is a federal law that gives students the right to view their education records after they enroll at a college. On the Common App, you complete the FERPA release before you can invite recommenders, and you decide whether to waive your right to see the letters.

    Most counselors advise waiving it. When you waive your right, colleges know your recommenders wrote freely, knowing you'd never read the letter. That makes the letters more credible. Some teachers also prefer to write only for students who have waived. Not waiving doesn't disqualify you, but it can make a reader wonder about the letter. Your decision is shared with your recommenders and colleges, applies to every college on your list, and can't be changed once you've invited your first recommender, so make it carefully before you invite anyone.

    Thank-You Notes¶

    Thank-you notes are short, sincere messages you send your recommenders after they submit your letters, and again when you hear your decisions. Writing a letter takes a teacher real time, often an evening or more, and is usually done on top of their regular work. A handwritten note, or at least a personal email, thanking them for their time and telling them where you're applying is the right thing to do. Then follow up in the spring to tell them where you were admitted and where you've decided to go. Teachers rarely hear how things turned out, and they genuinely want to know.

    The Additional Recommendation¶

    An additional recommendation is an optional letter beyond what a college requires, often from a coach, employer, activity advisor, religious leader, or mentor who knows you outside the classroom. Some colleges invite one, some limit them, and some don't accept them at all. Check each college's policy.

    The rule is simple: send one only if it adds something new. A letter from your employer describing how you trained new staff, or from a coach describing how you led younger teammates, can show a side of you that teacher letters can't. A third teacher letter that repeats the first two adds nothing and adds to the reader's workload. When in doubt, don't send it. More letters aren't better. Different letters are.

    Worked Example: Is a Third Letter Worth It?¶

    Marcus's two teacher letters come from his junior-year math and chemistry teachers. He's considering two possible additional letters:

    • His robotics coach, who saw him mentor sophomores on the navigation code and lead the programming team for two years.
    • His English teacher, who gave him an A and likes him.

    The robotics coach can describe leadership and teamwork that neither classroom teacher saw. That letter adds something new. The English teacher's letter would mostly repeat what his other teachers say. Marcus asks the coach and only sends the letter to the colleges that invite an additional recommendation.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Letters of recommendation give readers outside confirmation of who you are. The strongest are specific, and you can make specificity more likely.
    • Most selective colleges want one or two teacher recommendations, usually from 11th- or 12th-grade core subjects, plus a counselor recommendation that explains your context.
    • Choosing recommenders: pick teachers who know you well, ideally recent and from different subjects. The highest grade doesn't matter as much as how well they know you.
    • Request timing: ask in spring of junior year or the very start of senior year, at least six to eight weeks before your first deadline.
    • Asking: ask in person, say why you're asking them, and give them an easy way to decline.
    • Give each recommender a recommender packet: a brag sheet with specific moments, your resume, and your colleges with recommender deadlines. Check in politely before deadlines.
    • Sign the FERPA waiver to waive access. It makes letters more credible.
    • Send thank-you notes, and tell recommenders how things turned out.
    • An additional recommendation is worth sending only if it adds something new.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    You earned an A in a large lecture-style class where the teacher barely knows you, and a B+ in a smaller class where the teacher has seen you struggle and improve. Who should you ask?

    The B+ teacher who knows you well. They can write a specific, vivid letter. The A teacher can only confirm your grade, which your transcript already shows.

    Your first application deadline is November 1. When should you ask your teachers, and when should they have your packet?

    Ask by spring of junior year, and no later than mid-September. Send the recommender packet by late August or early September, at least four to six weeks before the deadline.

    Why do most counselors recommend waiving your FERPA right to see your letters?

    Waiving tells colleges your recommenders wrote freely, which makes the letters more credible to readers. Some teachers also prefer to write only for students who waive.

    What should a brag sheet include?

    Two or three specific moments from the class or activity, what you learned or how you grew, your goals, and anything that helps the recommender connect their letter to your application.

    A college accepts optional additional letters. You could ask a third teacher who'd say similar things, or your manager at work. Which is better, and why?

    The manager, because that letter shows a side of you (responsibility, work ethic, leadership at work) that teacher letters can't. An additional letter should add something new, not repeat the others.
