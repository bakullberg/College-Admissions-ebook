    3. 16. Comparing Award Letters

    Quiz: Comparing Award Letters¶

    Test your understanding of work-study and loans, reading award letters, out-of-pocket cost, four-year projections, and reconsideration requests with these review questions.

    1. What is the first-year annual limit on federal Direct Loans for a dependent undergraduate, and how much of it can be subsidized?¶

    1. $5,500 total, with up to $3,500 subsidized
    2. $7,500 total, with up to $5,500 subsidized
    3. $20,000 total, with up to $10,000 subsidized
    4. $6,500 total, with up to $4,500 subsidized
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. For dependent undergraduates, the first-year Direct Loan limit is $5,500, of which at most $3,500 can be subsidized. The limit rises to $6,500 in the second year and $7,500 in the third year and beyond, with a $31,000 cap overall. $20,000 is the new annual cap on Parent PLUS loans, not student loans. The chapter calls these modest limits a protection, since they keep most undergraduates' federal debt manageable.

    Concept Tested: Federal Direct Loans

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    2. Who is legally responsible for repaying a Parent PLUS loan, and what is its 2026–27 interest rate?¶

    1. The college, at 4%
    2. The student, at 6.52%
    3. The parent, at 9.07%
    4. The student and parent equally, at 6.52%
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Parent PLUS loans are borrowed by parents in their own names, so parents, not students, are legally responsible for repaying them. For 2026–27 the rate is 9.07%, with an origination fee of about 4%, which is much higher than the 6.52% rate on undergraduate Direct Loans. The 4% figure is the origination fee, not the rate. The chapter calls Parent PLUS the most misleading line on many letters, because it's family debt, not aid.

    Concept Tested: Parent PLUS Loans

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    3. What is the current name of the federal award letter template that many colleges use, formerly called the Shopping Sheet?¶

    1. The FAFSA Submission Summary
    2. The Common Data Set
    3. The Student Aid Report
    4. The College Financing Plan
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Many colleges use a version of a federal template, once called the Shopping Sheet and now the College Financing Plan, which separates grants from loans and shows a net cost. Colleges aren't required to use a standard format, though, which is why award letters vary so much. The FAFSA Submission Summary shows your Student Aid Index after filing, and the Common Data Set is a college's standard admissions and aid report.

    Concept Tested: Award Letter

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    4. An award letter lists $2,500 in Federal Work-Study. What does that number actually represent?¶

    1. A grant the student will receive whether or not they work
    2. A ceiling on what the student can earn by working a campus job
    3. A discount subtracted from the student's August bill
    4. A loan that must be repaid after graduation
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says the work-study amount is a ceiling, not a payment. You earn it only by finding a work-study job and working the hours, and you're paid by paycheck as you work. It isn't subtracted from your bill, it isn't guaranteed, and it isn't a loan. That's why Priya budgeted her August payment without it and planned to use her earnings for books and personal expenses.

    Concept Tested: Work-Study

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    5. What guideline does the chapter mention for keeping total student debt at graduation manageable?¶

    1. Borrow only what a private lender will approve
    2. Keep total student debt below your expected first-year salary
    3. Keep total student debt below one year's cost of attendance
    4. Borrow no more than your family's Student Aid Index
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says a useful guideline many advisors use is to keep total student debt below your expected first-year salary, which keeps monthly payments manageable on a typical ten-year plan. It also advises comparing debt alongside each college's graduation rate, since debt without a degree is the hardest kind to repay. The other options aren't benchmarks the chapter gives, and private loans should come only as a last resort.

    Concept Tested: Total Debt at Graduation

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    6. Why does the chapter warn about a college that "front-loads" aid?¶

    1. It requires students to accept all loans in the first year
    2. It gives aid only to students who apply Early Decision
    3. It counts work-study as part of the first-year grant
    4. It is generous in year one and less so afterward, so four years can cost much more than the first letter suggests
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Aid renewal isn't automatic. You must refile the FAFSA each year and meet academic and scholarship conditions, and need-based aid is recalculated annually. A college that front-loads aid is generous in year one and less so afterward, so it can cost much more over four years than its first letter suggests. That's why the chapter suggests asking each college whether your grant usually stays the same if your family's finances don't change.

    Concept Tested: Aid Renewal

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    7. An award letter shows: cost of attendance $50,000; grant $22,000; scholarship $3,000; work-study $2,500; subsidized loan $3,500; unsubsidized loan $2,000; Parent PLUS loan $9,000. If the student accepts work-study and both student loans, what is the out-of-pocket cost?¶

    1. $17,000
    2. $25,000
    3. $19,500
    4. $8,000
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. First, net price = \(50,000 − (\)22,000 + $3,000) = $25,000. Then out-of-pocket cost = net price − work-study − student loans = $25,000 − $2,500 − $5,500 = $17,000. The Parent PLUS loan isn't subtracted, because it's a way for the family to pay the out-of-pocket cost, not a reduction in it. Subtracting it gives $8,000, which understates what the family must cover. $25,000 is the net price, not the out-of-pocket cost.

    Concept Tested: Out-of-Pocket Cost

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    8. A college's cost of attendance is $40,000 in year one, and the student receives $25,000 in grants that stay flat. If the cost rises 3% a year, what is the projected net price in year two?¶

    1. $16,200
    2. $41,200
    3. $15,450
    4. $15,000
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A four-year cost projection raises the cost of attendance, here by 3% a year, and checks how aid behaves. In year two, the cost is $40,000 × 1.03 = $41,200. With grants flat at $25,000, the net price is $41,200 − $25,000 = $16,200, up from $15,000. Raising the first-year net price by 3% gives $15,450, which misses that the whole cost rises. $41,200 is the cost of attendance, not the net price.

    Concept Tested: Four-Year Cost Projection

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    9. A student's first-choice college offers $4,000 a year less in grants than a comparable college. Which reconsideration request best follows the chapter's advice?¶

    1. A message to the admissions office saying the student will enroll elsewhere unless the offer is matched
    2. A polite email to the financial aid office with the competing award letter attached, sent in early April
    3. A phone call to the admissions officer on April 30 asking for more merit aid
    4. No request, since colleges never change an aid offer once it's sent
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A good aid reconsideration request goes to the financial aid office, not admissions. It explains your situation specifically, includes documentation such as a competing award letter, and is polite and factual, never an ultimatum. It should be sent soon after you receive your offer, well before May 1. Requests are normal and colleges expect them, and the worst likely outcome is "no change." Marcus's request to Lakeshore earned him $2,000 more a year.

    Concept Tested: Aid Reconsideration Requests

    See: Comparing Award Letters

    10. An award letter reads: "Cost of attendance $60,000. Grant $35,000; Work-Study $3,000; Subsidized Loan $3,500; Unsubsidized Loan $2,000; Parent PLUS $11,000. Total financial aid: $54,500. Remaining balance: $5,500." What does sorting the lines reveal?¶

    1. The net price is $54,500, since that is the total aid offered
    2. The family will pay nothing, since aid covers almost all of the cost
    3. The real net price is $25,000, and the low balance depends on $16,500 in loans
    4. The family will pay only $5,500, since that is the remaining balance
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Sorting each line into free, earned, and borrowed money shows $35,000 in grants, $3,000 in work-study, and $16,500 in loans, including the Parent PLUS loan. The real net price is $60,000 − $35,000 = $25,000. The $5,500 "remaining balance" assumes the family borrows heavily and the student earns the full work-study amount. The chapter calls this free money versus borrowed money, and advises ignoring the letter's totals and using your own.

    Concept Tested: Free Money Versus Borrowed Money
