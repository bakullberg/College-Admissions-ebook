    Scholarships¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers finding automatic, competitive, outside, and local scholarships, deciding which are worth applying for, reusing essays, keeping renewable awards, and avoiding scams and award displacement. It extends the aid picture with money students apply for or earn automatically. After this chapter, students can build a focused scholarship list and spot a scam.

    Money You Find, Money That Finds You¶

    Chapter 14 covered the aid that comes from filing forms: federal, state, and college aid based on your family's finances and your record. Scholarships add another layer. Some arrive automatically with your admission letter. Others you have to find, apply for, and win. Scholarship money is real, but finding it takes more than typing "scholarships" into a search engine, and some of what you'll find is designed to take your money rather than give it.

    This chapter is about spending your scholarship time where it pays off. You'll start with what scholarships are and the main kinds: automatic merit awards, competitive scholarships, and outside scholarships, including the local ones students most often overlook. Then you'll learn how to judge eligibility, estimate whether an application is worth your time, prioritize a list, and reuse essays without falling into the recycled-essay trap. The chapter ends with three things that protect the money you win: renewal rules, scam warning signs, and award displacement.

    Scholarships¶

    Scholarships are financial aid you don't have to repay, usually awarded for merit, talent, or specific qualifications rather than financial need alone. Like grants, they're gift aid, so every scholarship dollar lowers your net price (Chapter 14). Unlike most grants, many scholarships require you to do something to earn them, such as meeting a GPA standard, submitting an essay, or competing against other applicants.

    Scholarships come from two broad places, and the difference shapes your strategy:

    • College scholarships come from the colleges themselves, as part of institutional aid. Many are automatic. Some require a separate application.
    • Outside scholarships come from anyone else: businesses, community groups, foundations, religious organizations, professional associations, employers, and national programs.

    The table below previews the kinds of scholarships covered in this chapter. Each gets its own short section after it.

    Kind Who awards it How you get it Competition
    Automatic merit A college Admission plus meeting its criteria None beyond admission
    Competitive A college or outside group A separate application, often with essays Moderate to very high
    Outside Businesses, foundations, organizations A separate application Varies widely
    Local Community groups near you A separate application, often through your school Usually low

    Worked Example: Where Priya's Scholarship Money Comes From¶

    By April, Priya has scholarship offers from three sources:

    • Hillcrest College: a $6,000-per-year merit scholarship, awarded automatically with her admission (Chapter 14).
    • Her state flagship: a $3,000-per-year honors college scholarship, awarded after a separate application and a short essay.
    • Her town's Rotary Club: a $1,500 one-time scholarship, for which she competed with about 20 students from her county.

    The Hillcrest award came with no extra effort. The flagship award took one essay. The Rotary scholarship took an application, an essay, and an interview. All three lower what her family pays, but they took very different amounts of work. Matching effort to likely payoff is what this chapter is about.

    Automatic Merit Scholarships¶

    Automatic merit scholarships are awards a college gives to admitted students who meet published criteria, usually GPA and test scores, with no separate application. You're considered simply by applying, and the award typically appears in your admission letter or aid offer. Many public universities and private colleges use them to attract strong students. Iowa's merit scholarships, described in the case study, are an example. Check each college's scholarship page for its criteria and deadlines. Some automatic awards require you to apply by an early deadline, such as Early Action, or to submit test scores. At test-optional colleges, an automatic award with a score cutoff is one of the strongest reasons to submit a score (Chapter 6).

    Competitive Scholarships¶

    Competitive scholarships are awards you must apply for, usually with essays, recommendations, interviews, or a portfolio, and that are judged against other applicants. They include colleges' named scholarships and honors awards as well as many outside scholarships. Some are small and local. Others, such as large national programs, attract tens of thousands of applicants for a handful of awards. Competitive scholarships reward the same strengths admissions does, which are specific essays, strong recommendations, and depth in activities, so your application materials are a head start. Because they take real time, the expected-value thinking later in this chapter matters most for them.

    Outside Scholarships¶

    Outside scholarships, also called private scholarships, are scholarships from organizations other than your college or the government: businesses, foundations, civic and religious groups, professional associations, employers, and national programs. They usually follow you to whichever college you attend, though some are restricted to certain colleges, states, or majors.

    Outside scholarships vary enormously. Some are worth $500, others $10,000 or more. Some take a short form, others multiple essays. Some are renewable, many are one-time awards. Where to look, roughly in order of payoff for your time:

    1. Your high school counseling office, which often keeps a list of local and regional scholarships.
    2. Your parents' or your own employers. Many companies offer scholarships for employees' children. Ask the human resources office.
    3. Community and identity-based organizations, such as Rotary, Kiwanis, and Lions clubs, religious organizations, cultural associations, and nonprofits serving first-generation and other students.
    4. Professional associations in your intended field, such as nursing, engineering, or education.
    5. National scholarship search engines, useful for volume but heavily competitive.

    Outside scholarships also have a catch that surprises many families: winning one can reduce the aid your college offers, through award displacement. That's covered at the end of this chapter. The money is still worth winning, but you need to know how your college will treat it.

    Worked Example: Ana's Outside Scholarship List¶

    Ana's counselor gives her a list of 30 local and regional scholarships. Ana also finds six more through her mother's employer, her church, and a nonprofit for first-generation students. She adds them to a scholarship tab in her tracker with the amount, deadline, requirements, and eligibility for each.

    When she checks eligibility, 14 of the 36 don't fit. Some require a major she isn't planning, residence in another county, or a parent in a specific union. That leaves 22, still far too many to apply for well. The rest of this chapter shows how she narrows them.

    Local Scholarships¶

    Local scholarships are scholarships offered by organizations in your own community, such as civic clubs, local businesses, credit unions, churches, school foundations, and community foundations, usually for students from a specific town, county, or high school. They're often the best-kept secret in scholarship searching. They're rarely listed on national search engines, so far fewer students apply, sometimes only a handful. A $1,000 local award with 15 applicants can be a much better use of your time than a $10,000 national award with 20,000 applicants. Your school counselor, your local community foundation's website, and local newspapers and organizations are the best places to find them. Many have spring deadlines, so check in January.

    Scholarship Search Engines¶

    Scholarship search engines are free websites, such as the College Board's BigFuture scholarship search, Scholarships.com, and Fastweb, that match you to scholarships based on a profile you fill out. They're useful for finding national and regional awards you'd never hear about otherwise. Use them with three cautions. First, they're crowded. Large national awards on these sites attract enormous applicant pools. Second, profiles can generate marketing. Use your dedicated email (Chapter 5), and read privacy settings before sharing details. Third, never pay for a search. The best search tools are free, and any service charging to "find" scholarships for you is a warning sign, as you'll see in the scams section.

    Choosing Which to Apply For¶

    Scholarship Eligibility¶

    Scholarship eligibility is the set of requirements you must meet to be considered for a scholarship: GPA, test scores, financial need, intended major, residence, school, background or identity, activities, or affiliation with an organization. Read the full eligibility rules before you start an application, not after. Many students waste hours on scholarships they can't win because of a requirement buried on page two, such as a county residence rule or a minimum GPA. Eligibility also determines your odds. The narrower the eligibility, the fewer the applicants. A scholarship for "first-generation students from Johnson County planning to study education" may have only a few eligible applicants, and Ana is one of them.

    The Expected Value of Applying¶

    The expected value of applying is a way of estimating whether a scholarship is worth your time by combining its amount, your chance of winning, and the hours it takes. The idea is simple: a likely $1,000 can be worth more than an unlikely $10,000. To compare scholarships, estimate the expected dollars per hour of work:

    \[ \text{Expected value per hour} = \frac{\text{Award amount} \times \text{Chance of winning}}{\text{Hours to apply}} \]

    Your chance of winning is an estimate. For local awards, divide the number of awards by the likely number of applicants. Your counselor may know how many students applied last year. For national awards, assume the odds are low, often well under 1%, unless you're exceptionally well matched. The numbers don't need to be precise. Even rough estimates reveal which applications deserve your evenings.

    Worked Example: Comparing Three Scholarships¶

    Marcus estimates the expected value of three scholarships:

    Scholarship Amount Estimated chance Hours Expected value per hour
    National tech scholarship $10,000 0.5% (1 in 200) 8 \(10,000 × 0.005 ÷ 8 ≈ **\)6**
    County community foundation $2,000 10% (3 awards, ~30 applicants) 4 \(2,000 × 0.10 ÷ 4 = **\)50**
    Local restaurant association $1,000 25% (1 award, ~4 applicants) 2 \(1,000 × 0.25 ÷ 2 ≈ **\)125**

    The national scholarship sounds the most exciting, but per hour, it's the weakest use of his time. The local restaurant association award, which he's eligible for through his family's business, is worth about twenty times as much per hour. Marcus applies for all three, but he writes the local applications first and gives them his best effort, and he spends no more than his eight planned hours on the national one.     Scholarship Prioritization¶

    Scholarship prioritization is choosing which scholarships to apply for, and in what order, given limited time. Expected value is the core of it. A few other rules sharpen the list. Start local and specific, where applicant pools are smallest. Favor scholarships that reuse material you already have, since essay reuse lowers the hours and raises the value per hour. Favor renewable awards when amounts are similar. A $2,000-per-year renewable award is worth $8,000 over four years, more than a $5,000 one-time award. Check deadlines, since many outside scholarships fall in spring, after college applications, while some college scholarships fall in fall, before them. Track every scholarship in your tracker, as Chapter 5 described, with amount, deadline, requirements, and status.

    Scholarship Essay Reuse¶

    Scholarship essay reuse is adapting essays you've already written, especially your personal essay and supplemental essays, for scholarship applications. Scholarship prompts often overlap with application prompts: a challenge you've overcome, your goals, a community you belong to, why you chose your field. Reuse follows the same rule as essay adaptation in Chapter 10. Reuse the material, not the exact text. Start from your best existing essay, rewrite at least a third of it to answer the scholarship's specific prompt, and make sure it speaks to the sponsor's purpose. A community foundation that funds future teachers wants to hear about your path to teaching. Keep a "scholarship essays" subfolder in your drafts folder, and note which version went where.

    Worked Example: One Essay, Three Scholarships¶

    Ana's personal essay about teaching her brother to read becomes the starting point for three scholarship essays:

    • Future teachers scholarship ("Why do you want to teach?", 500 words): she keeps the kitchen-floor scene and expands the reflection about "the pause" into her plans for a teaching career.
    • First-generation scholarship ("Describe a challenge your family has faced and your role", 400 words): she shifts the focus to translating for her parents, with the reading story as a brief second example.
    • Church community scholarship ("How have you served your community?", 300 words): she leads with the food pantry and connects it to the same theme of helping people understand.

    Each essay answers its prompt directly. None is a copy. All three took about a third of the time new essays would have.

    Keeping the Money¶

    Renewable Scholarships¶

    Renewable scholarships are awards that continue for more than one year, usually up to four years of undergraduate study, as long as you meet the renewal criteria. They're worth far more than they first appear. A $4,000 renewable scholarship is a $16,000 scholarship over four years. That's why renewability belongs in every comparison, both when you prioritize applications and when you compare aid offers in Chapter 16.

    Renewability also comes with a risk: if you lose the award, your cost rises. Before counting a renewable scholarship as four years of money, ask two questions: What do I have to do to keep it? and What happens if I don't? Some awards can be regained the following year, and others are gone for good. College merit scholarships are often renewable. Outside scholarships are more often one-time awards, though some renew if you reapply each year.

    Worked Example: The Value of Renewing¶

    Casey compares two offers:

    • Regional public university: a $4,000-per-year merit scholarship, renewable for four years with a 3.0 college GPA.
    • A local business scholarship: $6,000, one time only.

    The local award looks bigger, but over four years the university's scholarship is worth $16,000, almost three times as much, if Casey keeps a 3.0. Casey also checks the university's renewal data. About 80% of recipients keep the scholarship through four years. Casey decides to count it as about $13,000 in expected value and plans first-year study habits around the 3.0 requirement.

    Scholarship Renewal Criteria¶

    Scholarship renewal criteria are the conditions you must meet each year to keep a renewable scholarship. Common criteria include a minimum college GPA (often 3.0 or higher), full-time enrollment (often at least 12 credits per semester, sometimes 30 credits per year), continued enrollment in a particular major or program, and, for need-based awards, filing the FAFSA or CSS Profile each year. Some awards also require community service, participation in a program, or a renewal application. Read the criteria in your award letter, save them in your filing system, and add any renewal deadlines to your calendar. A scholarship lost to a missed form is one of the most painful and avoidable losses in college.

    Scholarship Scams¶

    Scholarship scams are fraudulent offers that pretend to offer scholarships, grants, or help finding aid, but are really designed to take your money or personal information. They circle every scholarship season, targeting students and families who are anxious about cost. The Federal Trade Commission's simplest rule covers most of them: never pay to apply for a scholarship. Legitimate scholarships don't charge application fees, and legitimate help, from your counselor, your college's financial aid office, and the free FAFSA, doesn't cost anything either.

    Scam Warning Signs¶

    Scam warning signs are the patterns that reveal a fraudulent scholarship or aid offer. Watch for:

    • Any fee to apply, "process," "redeem," or "hold" a scholarship.
    • Guarantees, such as "guaranteed or your money back." The FTC says that's a scammer's line.
    • "You've been selected" for a scholarship you never applied for.
    • Requests for a bank account or credit card number to "confirm eligibility."
    • Pressure to act immediately, such as "respond within 24 hours" or paying at a financial aid "seminar."
    • A vague sponsor with no verifiable address, website history, or past winners.
    • Anyone charging to file the FAFSA, which is free at studentaid.gov.

    If something looks off, search the scholarship's name with the word "scam," and ask your counselor. Report scams to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. The sorter below lets you practice spotting them.

    Award Displacement¶

    Award displacement, also called scholarship displacement, is when a college reduces the financial aid it offered you because you won an outside scholarship. It happens because federal rules generally don't allow a student's need-based aid, including outside scholarships, to exceed demonstrated need. When an outside award pushes your total aid above your need, the college must reduce something. Many colleges reduce loans or work-study first, which helps you. Some reduce their own grants, so the outside scholarship saves you less than it seems. A few states, including Maryland, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, California, Washington, and Minnesota, now restrict displacement at some or all of their colleges. Before accepting an outside award, ask each college's financial aid office how it treats outside scholarships, and report every award as the college requires. Failing to report can lead to having to repay aid later.

    Worked Example: What a Scholarship Actually Saves¶

    Ana wins a $2,000 outside scholarship. Her aid packages at two colleges already meet her full need:

    • College 1 reduces her federal loan by $2,000. Her net price stays the same, but she borrows $2,000 less. The scholarship replaced debt.
    • College 2 reduces its own grant by $2,000. Her net price and her loans stay the same. The scholarship replaced the college's money, not hers.

    At College 1, the scholarship is worth the full $2,000 to Ana. At College 2, it's worth nothing to her this year. Ana asks both offices about their policies before deciding where to enroll. It's one more line to compare in Chapter 16.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Scholarships are gift aid from colleges and outside sources. Automatic merit scholarships come with admission, and competitive scholarships require applications.
    • Outside scholarships come from businesses, foundations, and organizations. Local scholarships have the smallest pools and often the best odds. Scholarship search engines are free and useful but crowded.
    • Check scholarship eligibility first. Estimate the expected value of applying (amount × chance ÷ hours) to compare options.
    • Scholarship prioritization: start local, favor reusable essays and renewable awards, and track deadlines. Scholarship essay reuse adapts material, not text.
    • Renewable scholarships multiply value over four years. Know the scholarship renewal criteria and what happens if you miss them.
    • Scholarship scams charge fees or seek personal information. Learn the scam warning signs, and never pay to apply.
    • Award displacement can reduce your college aid when you win outside money. Ask each college how it handles outside scholarships.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    Scholarship A is $5,000, with about 500 applicants for one award, and takes 6 hours. Scholarship B is $800, with about 8 applicants for two awards, and takes 2 hours. Which has the higher expected value per hour?

    A: \(5,000 × (1 ÷ 500) ÷ 6 ≈ **\)1.67/hr. B: \(800 × (2 ÷ 8) ÷ 2 = **\)100/hr. Scholarship B is far better per hour.

    Is a $3,000 renewable scholarship (four years, 3.0 GPA required) worth more than a $7,000 one-time scholarship?

    If renewed all four years, the renewable award is worth $12,000, more than $7,000. Factor in your chance of keeping the GPA requirement.

    You get an email saying you've won a $2,500 grant and need to pay a $50 fee to receive it. What should you do?

    Don't pay. A fee to receive a scholarship you didn't apply for is a classic scam. Report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov and tell your counselor.

    What is award displacement, and what question should you ask your college about it?

    It's when a college reduces its own aid because you won an outside scholarship. Ask whether the college reduces loans and work-study first or its own grants.

    Why do local scholarships often offer better odds than national ones?

    They're rarely listed on national search engines and are limited to a small area, so far fewer students apply.
