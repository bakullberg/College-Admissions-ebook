    Quiz: The SAT and ACT Question¶

    Test your understanding of testing policies, SAT and ACT scoring, score submission decisions, and test prep with these review questions.

    1. Which organization makes the SAT, and what is its total score range?¶

    1. ACT, Inc., scored from 320 to 1520
    2. The College Board, scored from 400 to 1600
    3. The College Board, scored from 1 to 36
    4. ACT, Inc., scored from 400 to 1600
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The SAT is made by the College Board and given digitally in the Bluebook app. Its two sections, Reading and Writing and Math, are each scored from 200 to 800, for a total of 400 to 1600. The 1-to-36 scale belongs to the ACT, which is made by ACT, Inc. The 320-to-1520 range belongs to the PSAT/NMSQT, the practice version of the SAT.

    2. In which grade can a high PSAT/NMSQT score qualify a student for National Merit Scholarship Program recognition?¶

    1. 10th or 11th grade
    2. 9th grade only
    3. 11th grade only
    4. 12th grade only
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The PSAT/NMSQT is usually taken at school in October of 10th or 11th grade, but the chapter says that in 11th grade only, high scorers can qualify for recognition and scholarships through the National Merit Scholarship Program, with cutoffs that vary by state. Taking it in 10th grade is still useful as a free diagnostic. The PSAT doesn't go to colleges, so a low score costs you nothing.

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    3. What does the chapter say about test accommodations on score reports sent to colleges?¶

    1. Accommodations appear only if the student used extended time
    2. Accommodations don't appear on score reports sent to colleges
    3. Accommodations are shown only to test-required colleges
    4. Accommodations are listed so colleges can adjust the score
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Test accommodations, such as extended time, extra breaks, a reader, or a separate room, are offered by both the College Board and ACT for students with documented disabilities or medical needs. The chapter states that they don't appear on score reports sent to colleges. Students with an IEP or 504 plan usually request them through the school's testing coordinator, and they should start early because approval can take several weeks.

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    4. Why does the chapter call it a myth that "test-optional" means scores no longer matter?¶

    1. A submitted strong score can still help, and scores can matter for scholarships and programs
    2. Test-optional colleges secretly penalize applicants who don't send scores
    3. Test-optional colleges require scores from all applicants after the first round
    4. Test-optional policies apply only to students who have never taken a test
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Test-optional means you get to choose whether a score is part of your application. A strong score you submit can still help, and a weak one you leave off won't hurt you. Scores can also matter for merit scholarships, specific programs, and colleges that have gone back to requiring them. The chapter says test-optional colleges review applications without scores fully, so there's no hidden penalty. Students who believe the myth may discover a scholarship cutoff too late to test.

    Concept Tested: Test-Optional Myth

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    5. A test-blind college won't consider SAT or ACT scores for admission. Why might it still ask for scores after a student enrolls?¶

    1. To decide whether to offer the student a spot off the waitlist
    2. To recalculate the student's admission decision
    3. To place the student in the right first math or writing class
    4. To report the student's score in its Common Data Set
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter explains that some test-blind colleges still use scores after enrollment, for example to place students in the right first math or writing class. That's why it suggests checking whether a test-blind college asks for scores once you've committed. Because the college doesn't look at scores for admission, they can't change the admission decision or a waitlist outcome. The University of California and Cal State systems are the best-known test-blind examples.

    Concept Tested: Test-Blind Policy

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    6. What is the main purpose of keeping an error log during test prep?¶

    1. To track which official practice tests you have already used
    2. To record each practice test's total score over time
    3. To prove to colleges how much you prepared for the test
    4. To find the pattern behind your mistakes and turn it into a study plan
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. An error log is a running record of every practice question you get wrong, noting the question type, why you missed it, and what you'll do differently. The chapter says it turns mistakes into a study plan, and that most students who review only their score miss the pattern behind it. After a few practice tests, you sort the log by type and reason. A log of total scores alone would hide that pattern, and colleges never see your log.

    Concept Tested: Error Log

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    7. A student's ACT section scores are English 30, Math 25, and Reading 28. What is the composite score?¶

    1. 28
    2. 83
    3. 30
    4. 27
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The ACT composite is the average of the core section scores, English, Math, and Reading, rounded to the nearest whole number. Here, (30 + 25 + 28) ÷ 3 = 83 ÷ 3 ≈ 27.67, which rounds to 28. The value 83 is the sum, which is how the SAT total works but not the ACT. Choosing 27 drops the decimal instead of rounding, and 30 is simply the highest section score.

    Concept Tested: Composite Score

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    8. A test-optional college reports a middle 50% SAT range of 1200–1360. A student scored 1300, and no scholarship or program rules apply. Using the chapter's rule, what should the student do?¶

    1. Withhold the score, because test-optional ranges are inflated
    2. Submit the score, because it is at or above the estimated midpoint of 1280
    3. Withhold the score, because it is below the 75th percentile
    4. Treat it as a judgment call, because it is inside the range
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter estimates the midpoint as halfway through the middle 50% range: (1200 + 1360) ÷ 2 = 1280. A score at or above the midpoint is a strength, so the rule is to submit. The judgment-call zone is only between the 25th percentile and the midpoint. Being below the 75th percentile isn't a reason to withhold. The inflated-range caution actually makes a score near the midpoint look stronger, not weaker.

    Concept Tested: Score Submission Decision

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    9. A student took the SAT twice. In March: Reading and Writing 620, Math 640. In October: Reading and Writing 660, Math 610. What is the superscore?¶

    1. 1250
    2. 1260
    3. 1270
    4. 1300
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Superscoring combines your highest section scores from different test dates into a new composite. The best Reading and Writing score is 660 from October, and the best Math score is 640 from March, so the superscore is 660 + 640 = 1300. The March total is 1260 and the October total is 1270, which are single-date scores. The value 1250 pairs the two lower section scores. Not every college superscores, so check each policy.

    Concept Tested: Superscoring

    See: The SAT and ACT Question

    10. Which student's situation meets all three of the chapter's conditions for a retest?¶

    1. A student below a scholarship cutoff, with a weak section to study, and a test date before deadlines
    2. A student who has already tested three times and has no new prep plan
    3. A student whose score is above the midpoint at every college but who wants a higher number
    4. A student with a weak Math section who plans to retest the week after their last deadline
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says to retest when your score is below the level that matters for an important college or scholarship, you have a specific way to improve, and there's time for another test date before deadlines. The fourth student meets all three. The first student's score already works everywhere. The second has no time for scores to count. The third has tested three times with nothing changed, and the chapter says two sittings are enough for most students.

    Concept Tested: Retesting Decision
