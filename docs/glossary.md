# Glossary of Terms

#### Academic Fit

How well a college's programs, teaching style, academic intensity, and available majors match a student's interests, preparation, and learning preferences.

**Example:** A student who wants hands-on engineering projects from freshman year looks for colleges with early design courses.

See also: College Fit, Intended Major, Liberal Arts College

#### Academic Record

The complete history of a student's high school coursework, grades, course levels, and related academic achievements, usually documented in the transcript.

The academic record is typically the single most important part of an application.

**Example:** An academic record showing steady improvement from C's in ninth grade to A's in eleventh grade tells a story of growth.

See also: High School Transcript, Course Rigor, Grade Point Average

#### Acceptance

An admission decision in which a college offers an applicant a place in its incoming class, usually along with instructions for replying, paying a deposit, and reviewing financial aid.

See also: Admission Decision, Accepting an Offer, Comparing Offers

#### Accepting an Offer

Formally confirming that a student will attend a college, typically by submitting a reply through the applicant portal and paying an enrollment deposit by the deadline.

**Example:** A student accepts her offer in the portal on April 20 and pays the deposit the same day.

See also: Enrollment Deposit, May 1 Decision Deadline, Final College Choice

#### ACT

A standardized college admissions exam that measures English, math, and reading, with science and writing sections that may be optional depending on the format.

See also: SAT, Composite Score, SAT-ACT Concordance

#### Action Verbs

Strong, specific verbs such as led, organized, built, or trained, used to begin activity descriptions and convey what a student actually did.

See also: Activity Description, Activities Resume

#### Activities List

The section of an application where students record their extracurricular activities, including roles, time commitments, years of involvement, and brief descriptions.

**Example:** The Common App activities list holds up to ten entries, each with a short description.

See also: Activity Description, Activity Ordering, Activities Section

#### Activities Resume

A one- or two-page document summarizing a student's activities, awards, and experience, often shared with recommenders, interviewers, or scholarship committees.

**Example:** A student brings a one-page activities resume to an alumni interview so the interviewer can see her experiences at a glance.

See also: Brag Sheet, Recommender Packet, Activities List

#### Activities Section

The part of the Common App where students list up to ten extracurricular activities, with the position held, organization, grades of participation, hours, and a short description.

See also: Activities List, Activity Description, Activity Ordering

#### Activity Description

The short written explanation in an activities list of what a student did in an activity and what resulted, usually under a tight character limit.

**Example:** "Trained 6 new cashiers; handled weekend closing shift and cash reconciliation" says more than "Worked at a grocery store."

See also: Action Verbs, Measurable Impact, Activities List

#### Activity Essay

A short supplemental essay that asks an applicant to describe one extracurricular activity, job, or responsibility in more depth than the activities list allows.

**Example:** A student expands on her role running the sound board for every school play over three years.

See also: Extracurricular Activities, Activity Description, Supplemental Essays

#### Activity Ordering

Arranging the entries in an application's activities list from most to least significant, so admissions readers see the student's most meaningful commitments first.

See also: Activities List, Depth Over Breadth

#### Adding Colleges

The step of searching for and selecting colleges within an application platform so their questions and requirements appear in the student's account.

See also: Common App Dashboard, Member Questions, Writing Supplements

#### Additional Information Section

An optional space on the Common App for explaining circumstances not covered elsewhere, such as an illness, a family situation, or a school schedule change.

**Example:** A student explains that a grade dip in tenth grade happened while caring for a sick parent.

See also: Writing Section, Family Responsibilities, Optional Essays

#### Additional Recommendation

An optional letter from someone outside the classroom, such as a coach, employer, or mentor, that adds a new perspective to an application.

**Example:** A student adds a letter from her manager at the restaurant where she has worked for three years.

See also: Choosing Recommenders, Letters of Recommendation

#### Admission Decision

A college's formal response to a completed application, which may be an acceptance, a denial, a deferral to a later round, or a place on the waitlist.

See also: Acceptance, Denial, Waitlist

#### Admission Rate

The percentage of applicants a college admits in a given year, calculated by dividing the number of admitted students by the number of applicants.

**Example:** A college that receives 20,000 applications and admits 4,000 students has a 20% admission rate.

See also: Selective Admissions, Common Data Set, Reach School

#### Admissions Information Session

A presentation, in person or online, in which a college's admissions staff explain the school's programs, admission process, and financial aid.

See also: Campus Visit, Demonstrated Interest, Admissions Office

#### Admissions Myths

Widely repeated but inaccurate beliefs about how colleges choose students, which can lead applicants to make poor decisions about where and how to apply.

See also: No Single Right School Myth, Test-Optional Myth, Well-Rounded Applicant Myth

#### Admissions Office

The college department responsible for recruiting students, reviewing applications, making admission decisions, and communicating with applicants, families, and high school counselors throughout the application cycle.

See also: Admissions Officer, Applicant Portal

#### Admissions Officer

A staff member of a college's admissions office who reads and evaluates applications, often for a specific geographic region, and represents the college at fairs and high school visits.

Many officers are assigned to a territory, so the same person who visits your high school may read your file.

**Example:** The admissions officer responsible for the Pacific Northwest reads every application from that region.

See also: Admissions Office, Admissions Officer Interview, Holistic Review

#### Admissions Officer Interview

A college interview conducted by a member of the admissions staff, usually on campus or by video, as opposed to one conducted by an alum.

See also: Interviews, Alumni Interview, Admissions Officer

#### Admissions Timeline

The calendar of milestones, from testing and research through deadlines, decisions, and enrollment, that structures a student's path to college across junior and senior year.

**Example:** A typical timeline places campus visits in the summer, early applications in the fall, and the final enrollment decision in spring.

See also: Senior Year Calendar, Application Deadline, Month-by-Month Planner

#### Admitted Student Day

An event a college hosts for accepted students and their families to tour campus, meet classmates and faculty, and learn more before deciding whether to enroll.

**Example:** At an admitted student day, a student attends a sample lecture and meets future classmates.

See also: Campus Revisit, Comparing Offers

#### Advanced Placement

A College Board program offering college-level courses in high school, each ending in an AP Exam that may earn college credit.

See also: AP Exam, AP Score, AP Credit Transfer

#### Aid Priority Deadline

The date by which a student should complete financial aid applications to receive full consideration for a college's or state's limited aid funds.

**Example:** A college with an aid priority deadline in February gives its full grant budget to students who file by that date.

See also: Priority Deadline, First-Come First-Served Aid, FAFSA

#### Aid Reconsideration Request

A formal request asking a college's financial aid office to review a student's aid offer and possibly increase it, usually supported by new information or documents.

**Example:** A student emails the aid office asking them to review her package after her family's medical bills rose sharply.

See also: Financial Aid Appeal, Change in Circumstances, Competing Offer Leverage

#### Aid Renewal

The process of reapplying for financial aid each year, usually by submitting a new FAFSA and meeting academic progress requirements.

See also: FAFSA, Renewable Scholarship, Four-Year Cost Projection

#### Alumni Interview

An interview conducted by a graduate of the college, often in the applicant's hometown or online, who reports impressions back to the admissions office.

**Example:** An alum who graduated twenty years ago interviews a student over video and submits a short report to admissions.

See also: Interviews, Admissions Officer Interview, Virtual Interview

#### AP Credit Transfer

The process of sending official AP scores to the college a student will attend so it can award course credit or advanced placement for qualifying results.

**Example:** A student sends her AP scores to her college and receives credit for introductory psychology.

See also: AP Score, College Credit in High School, Course Registration

#### AP Exam

The standardized test given each spring at the end of an Advanced Placement course, measuring whether a student has mastered college-level material in that subject.

See also: Advanced Placement, AP Score, AP Credit Transfer

#### AP Score

The result on an AP Exam, reported on a scale from 1 to 5, which colleges may use to award credit or placement.

**Example:** A college might grant credit for an AP Calculus AB score of 4 or 5.

See also: AP Exam, AP Credit Transfer, College Credit in High School

#### Applicant Portal

A college's personal online account for each applicant, where the student can check application status, see missing documents, and view the admission decision.

See also: Checking Portals Regularly, Application Status, Submission Confirmation

#### Application Checklist

A list of every component a specific college requires, such as essays, recommendations, scores, and forms, used to confirm nothing is missing.

See also: College-Specific Requirements, Deadline Tracker, Getting Organized

#### Application Deadline

The date and sometimes time by which a student must submit the application to a college for a specific round.

Deadlines may be in the college's local time zone, so submit early.

**Example:** A college with a January 1 deadline at 11:59 p.m. Eastern time may not accept a submission at 12:05 a.m.

See also: Supporting Document Deadline, Priority Deadline, Deadline Tracker

#### Application Fee

A charge a college may require to process an application, paid at submission and often waived for students who demonstrate financial need.

Many colleges charge no fee, and others waive it for students with financial need.

See also: Fee Waiver, Application Submission

#### Application Preview

A full view of a completed application, often as a PDF, that the student reviews for errors, cut-off text, and formatting problems before submitting.

**Example:** Reviewing the preview reveals that an activity description was cut off by the character limit.

See also: Application Submission, Editing and Proofreading

#### Application Review Process

The sequence of steps a college follows after receiving applications, including checking files for completeness, reading them, discussing candidates in committee, and making decisions.

**Example:** After an application is complete, a first reader summarizes it, a second reader reviews it, and a committee votes.

See also: Holistic Review, Admissions Officer, Waiting Period

#### Application Rounds

The distinct application periods a college offers, each with its own deadline and decision timing, such as early decision, early action, and regular decision.

**Example:** A college might offer an early action round with a fall deadline and a regular decision round with a winter deadline.

See also: Early Decision, Early Action, Regular Decision

#### Application Status

The current state of an application, such as incomplete, under review, or decision available, typically shown in the applicant portal.

See also: Applicant Portal, Checking Portals Regularly, Common App Dashboard

#### Application Stress

The pressure, worry, and fatigue that students and families experience during the college application process, often peaking around deadlines and decision release dates.

**Example:** A student feels overwhelmed in late October with three essays and two applications due the same week.

See also: Managing Uncertainty, Healthy Coping Strategies, Self-Care Routine

#### Application Submission

The act of formally sending a completed application to a college through a platform or the college's website, including paying any fee or using a waiver.

See also: Application Preview, Submission Confirmation, Application Fee

#### Asking for a Recommendation

Requesting that a teacher or counselor write a letter, ideally in person and with enough notice, followed by helpful materials.

**Example:** A student asks a teacher in person after class, then follows up with an email containing her brag sheet and deadlines.

See also: Recommendation Request Timing, Recommender Packet, Thank-You Notes

#### Assigning Recommenders

The application step of inviting teachers, counselors, and others to submit letters electronically and linking them to the colleges that will receive their recommendations.

See also: Letters of Recommendation, FERPA Waiver, Recommender Deadlines

#### Authentic Voice

Writing that sounds like the student's natural way of thinking and speaking, rather than overly formal language or someone else's style.

**Example:** An essay that says "I was super nervous" in the student's natural tone often reads better than one stuffed with thesaurus words.

See also: Show Don't Tell, Personal Essay, Feedback on Essays

#### Automatic Merit Scholarship

A scholarship a college awards to every admitted student who meets set criteria, such as a GPA or test score, without a separate application.

**Example:** A university awards $8,000 per year to every admitted student with at least a 3.8 GPA.

See also: Merit Aid, Competitive Scholarship, Priority Deadline

#### Award Displacement

The reduction of a college's own aid when a student receives an outside scholarship, potentially lowering the scholarship's real benefit.

Ask each college how it handles outside awards.

**Example:** A student wins a $3,000 outside scholarship, and her college reduces its own grant by $3,000.

See also: Outside Scholarship, Institutional Aid, Award Letter

#### Award Letter

A college's official notice listing the financial aid offered to an admitted student, such as grants, scholarships, work-study, and loans.

**Example:** An award letter lists a $20,000 grant, a $3,000 work-study job, and a $5,500 loan for the first year.

See also: Reading an Award Letter, Award Letter Comparison, Financial Aid

#### Award Letter Comparison

Evaluating financial aid offers from several colleges side by side using consistent categories such as net price and loan amounts.

**Example:** A spreadsheet with rows for grants, loans, and out-of-pocket cost makes it clear which offer is cheapest.

See also: Reading an Award Letter, Side-by-Side Comparison, Out-of-Pocket Cost

#### Balanced College List

A college list containing a mix of reach, target, and safety schools so that a student is likely to receive several good, affordable options.

**Example:** A balanced list of ten might include three reaches, four targets, and three safeties.

See also: College List, Reach School, Safety School

#### Balancing Rigor and Burnout

Choosing a challenging schedule while leaving enough time and energy to earn strong grades, sleep, and keep up activities and well-being.

**Example:** A student drops a sixth AP to keep enough time for her job and sleep, preserving strong grades in her other classes.

See also: Course Selection Strategy, Course Rigor, Self-Care Routine

#### Binding Commitment

A promise, made when applying early decision, to enroll at a college if admitted and to withdraw all other applications.

Breaking it without a valid reason, such as unaffordable aid, can lead colleges to cancel your other admissions.

See also: Early Decision, Early Decision Release, Double Depositing

#### Brag Sheet

A form or document in which a student lists accomplishments, activities, goals, and personal details to help a counselor or teacher write a recommendation.

**Example:** A student's brag sheet lists her favorite class project, her weekend job, and a challenge she overcame.

See also: Recommender Packet, Activities Resume, Counselor Recommendation

#### Brainstorming

The early idea-generating stage of essay writing, in which a student lists memories, values, and experiences without judging them yet.

**Example:** A student spends twenty minutes listing every job, hobby, family tradition, and turning point she can think of before choosing a topic.

See also: Essay Material List, Essay Topic Selection, Personal Story

#### Calendar Reminders

Alerts set in a digital or paper calendar that warn a student in advance of upcoming deadlines, tests, and tasks so nothing is missed.

**Example:** Setting a reminder one week and one day before each deadline gives time to finish and double-check.

See also: Deadline Tracker, Senior Year Calendar

#### Campus Culture

The shared values, traditions, social life, and overall atmosphere of a college's student community, which shape what daily life feels like on campus.

**Example:** One campus may center on big football weekends, while another revolves around arts events and student clubs.

See also: Social Fit, Campus Visit, Student Reviews

#### Campus Revisit

A second trip to a college, usually after admission, made to confirm whether the school is a good fit before the student commits to enroll.

**Example:** A student revisits two campuses in April and finds that one feels much more like home.

See also: Admitted Student Day, Campus Visit, Final College Choice

#### Campus Setting

The physical environment of a college, described as urban, suburban, rural, or small-town, which affects daily life, internships, and activities.

See also: School Size, Distance From Home, Social Fit

#### Campus Visit

An in-person trip to a college, typically including a guided tour and information session, that lets a student experience the campus firsthand.

**Example:** During a visit, a student might sit in on a class and eat lunch in the dining hall.

See also: Virtual Tour, Admissions Information Session, Campus Revisit

#### Capstone Application Roadmap

A personal, end-to-end plan a student builds that pulls together the college list, deadlines, testing, essays, recommendations, and financial aid into one action plan.

**Example:** A student's roadmap lists each college, its deadline, required essays, recommendation status, and aid forms in one document.

See also: Month-by-Month Planner, Deadline Tracker, Final College List

#### Celebrating Milestones

Recognizing and enjoying progress points in the application process, such as submitting an application or finishing the FAFSA, to stay motivated and reduce stress.

**Example:** A family goes out for pizza the night the last application is submitted.

See also: Self-Care Routine, Support Network

#### Change in Circumstances

A significant shift in a family's financial situation, such as job loss, illness, or divorce, that is not reflected on aid applications.

**Example:** A parent loses a job in January, after the FAFSA reported last year's higher income.

See also: Aid Reconsideration Request, Financial Aid Appeal

#### Checking Portals Regularly

The habit of logging in to each college's applicant portal on a schedule to catch missing documents, requests, and decision updates.

**Example:** A student checks every portal on Sunday evenings and discovers a missing recommendation in time to fix it.

See also: Applicant Portal, Application Status

#### Choosing Recommenders

Selecting teachers or others who know a student well and can describe specific strengths, rather than those who simply gave the highest grade.

**Example:** A student picks the chemistry teacher who watched her lead lab groups over the history teacher she barely spoke with, even though her history grade was higher.

See also: Teacher Recommendation, Asking for a Recommendation, Additional Recommendation

#### Class Rank

A student's academic standing compared with classmates in the same graduating class, usually based on GPA and expressed as a number or percentile.

Many high schools no longer report rank, and colleges then evaluate the transcript without it.

**Example:** A student ranked 15th in a class of 300 is in the top 5% of her class.

See also: Grade Point Average, Formula-Based Admissions

#### Coalition App

An online application platform, run by the Coalition for College, that many colleges accept as an alternative to the Common App.

See also: Common App, Institutional Application

#### College Admissions Process

The full sequence of steps a student completes to gain entry to a college, from researching schools and preparing materials through submitting applications, receiving decisions, and enrolling.

Seeing the process as a series of manageable stages makes senior year far less overwhelming.

**Example:** A senior who builds a college list in August, submits applications in the fall, and chooses a school in spring has moved through the whole process.

See also: Admissions Timeline, Application Submission, Final College Choice

#### College Credit in High School

Credit toward a college degree earned before high school graduation, through AP or IB exams, dual enrollment, or similar programs.

It can reduce college costs, but each college sets its own rules on what it accepts.

See also: Dual Enrollment, AP Score, AP Credit Transfer

#### College Essay

Any piece of writing a college asks applicants to submit, giving admissions readers insight into the student's personality, thinking, and voice.

See also: Personal Essay, Supplemental Essays, Common App Essay Prompts

#### College Fair

An event where representatives from many colleges gather in one place to share information and answer questions from prospective students.

**Example:** At a fall college fair in a school gym, a student talks with ten admissions representatives in one evening.

See also: College Research Tools, Admissions Officer, Demonstrated Interest

#### College Fit

The degree to which a college matches a student's academic goals, social preferences, and financial situation, which together predict whether the student is likely to thrive there.

Fit predicts whether you will thrive and graduate, which matters more than a school's name.

**Example:** A student who wants small classes, a strong art program, and a low net price evaluates each college against all three.

See also: Academic Fit, Social Fit, Financial Fit

#### College List

The set of colleges a student researches and plans to apply to, ideally spanning a range of selectivity and fit.

See also: Balanced College List, Long List, Final College List

#### College Research Tools

The websites, databases, publications, and events students use to gather information about colleges, such as search websites, the College Scorecard, and college fairs.

See also: College Search Website, College Scorecard, Common Data Set

#### College Scorecard

A free U.S. Department of Education website that reports data about colleges, including average cost, graduation rates, typical debt, and graduates' earnings.

**Example:** A student uses the College Scorecard to compare median debt at graduation for two state universities.

See also: College Research Tools, Graduation Rate, Total Debt at Graduation

#### College Search Website

An online tool that lets students filter and compare colleges by features such as location, size, majors, cost, and selectivity.

**Example:** BigFuture lets a student filter for mid-sized colleges in the Midwest that offer a nursing major.

See also: College Research Tools, Student Reviews, Long List

#### College-Specific Requirements

Application components that only a particular college or program asks for, such as a portfolio, audition, extra essay, or specific test, beyond the shared application.

**Example:** A college requires art applicants to submit a portfolio of 15 pieces in addition to the Common App.

See also: Application Checklist, Verifying Current Policies, Member Questions

#### Common App

An online application platform accepted by many U.S. colleges, letting students complete one main application and send it to multiple schools.

**Example:** A student completes one Common App with a single personal essay and sends it to eight different colleges.

See also: Coalition App, Institutional Application, Common App Account

#### Common App Account

A student's personal login on the Common App platform, where all application work, college selections, and recommender invitations are saved and submitted.

Accounts can usually roll over from year to year, so juniors can start early.

See also: Common App, Common App Dashboard, Login Credentials Record

#### Common App Dashboard

The Common App page that shows each added college, its deadlines, requirements, and the status of each part of the application.

See also: Common App Account, Adding Colleges, Application Status

#### Common App Essay Prompts

The set of essay questions the Common App provides each year, from which students choose one to answer in their personal essay.

The prompts are broad, and one lets you write on a topic of your choice.

**Example:** One prompt asks students to describe a challenge or setback and what they learned from it.

See also: Personal Essay, Essay Word Limit, Essay Topic Selection

#### Common Data Set

A standardized report many colleges publish each year with detailed figures on admissions, enrollment, test scores, financial aid, and the factors they weigh in decisions.

**Example:** Searching a college's name plus "Common Data Set" reveals whether it considers demonstrated interest.

See also: College Research Tools, Admission Rate, Demonstrated Interest

#### Community Essay

A supplemental essay asking an applicant to describe a group they belong to and their role within it, or how they would contribute to campus life.

**Example:** A student writes about running the weekly game night at a local library and what it taught her about including newcomers.

See also: Supplemental Essays, Personal Story

#### Community Service

Unpaid work a student does to benefit other people or the community, such as volunteering, tutoring, mentoring, or organizing local projects.

**Example:** A student tutors elementary students in reading at the public library every Saturday for two years.

See also: Extracurricular Activities, Sustained Commitment, Measurable Impact

#### Comparing Offers

Evaluating multiple college acceptances by weighing academic fit, social fit, net cost, financial aid, and personal priorities before making a final choice.

**Example:** A student compares two acceptances and chooses the one with a stronger engineering program and a lower net price.

See also: Side-by-Side Comparison, Award Letter Comparison, Final College Choice

#### Competing Offer Leverage

Using a stronger financial aid offer from one college as supporting evidence when asking another college to reconsider and possibly improve its award.

Not every college matches offers, but many will review them.

**Example:** A student shares a peer college's offer of $10,000 more in grants and asks her first choice to review its award.

See also: Aid Reconsideration Request, Award Letter Comparison

#### Competitive Scholarship

A scholarship awarded through a selective process, often requiring a separate application, essays, or interviews, with only some applicants chosen.

**Example:** A college invites 100 finalists to interview for 15 full-tuition scholarships.

See also: Automatic Merit Scholarship, Scholarship Eligibility, Expected Value of Applying

#### Composite Score

The single overall ACT score, on a scale from 1 to 36, calculated as the rounded average of a student's scores on the required test sections.

**Example:** ACT section scores of 30, 28, and 32 average to a composite score of 30.

See also: Section Scores, ACT, Superscoring

#### Core Academic Courses

The main subject areas colleges expect high school students to study: English, mathematics, laboratory science, social studies, and world language.

**Example:** Many colleges look for four years of English and at least three years of math among core academic courses.

See also: Course Rigor, Courses and Grades Section

#### Cost of Attendance

A college's estimate of the total yearly cost of being a student, including tuition, fees, housing, food, books, transportation, and personal expenses.

**Example:** A college lists tuition of $30,000, housing and food of $14,000, and $4,000 for books and personal costs, for a cost of attendance of $48,000.

See also: Sticker Price, Tuition, Indirect Costs

#### Counselor Meeting

A scheduled conversation between a student and school counselor to review the college list, timeline, requirements, and any questions about applying.

See also: School Counselor, Brag Sheet, Senior Year Calendar

#### Counselor Recommendation

A letter or report from the school counselor describing the student in the context of the high school and its curriculum.

**Example:** A counselor explains that a student took the most demanding schedule available while working to support her family.

See also: School Counselor, Letters of Recommendation, Brag Sheet

#### Course Registration

The process of choosing classes and enrolling in them for an upcoming college term, often completed online or during orientation with help from an academic advisor.

See also: Orientation, Placement Tests, AP Credit Transfer

#### Course Rigor

The level of academic challenge in a student's course schedule, judged relative to the most demanding options the student's own high school offers.

Colleges look at whether you took advantage of challenging options available to you, not options your school does not provide.

**Example:** A student who chose AP Calculus and AP Chemistry senior year shows more rigor than one who took no advanced math or science.

See also: Honors Courses, Advanced Placement, Balancing Rigor and Burnout

#### Course Selection Strategy

A deliberate approach to choosing high school classes that balances academic challenge, personal interest, intended major, and a manageable overall workload.

**Example:** A future engineering student prioritizes physics and calculus while choosing one fewer AP in an unrelated subject.

See also: Course Rigor, Balancing Rigor and Burnout, Senior Year Course Load

#### Courses and Grades Section

A part of the Common App, required by some colleges, where students enter every high school course and grade from their transcript.

See also: Self-Reported Academic Record, Transcript Copies, Education Section

#### CSS Profile

An online financial aid application, run by the College Board, that many private colleges use to award their own institutional aid.

It asks more detailed questions than the FAFSA, and a fee may apply unless waived.

**Example:** A student applying to several private colleges completes both the FAFSA and the CSS Profile.

See also: FAFSA, Institutional Aid, Need-Based Aid

#### Deadline Tracker

A spreadsheet or document listing every college's deadlines and requirements in one place so a student can see what is due and when.

**Example:** A tracker with one row per college and columns for deadline, essays, and test policy shows at a glance what is left.

See also: Shared Tracker, Application Deadline, Calendar Reminders

#### Decision Release Date

The specific date, and often time, when a college makes admission decisions for an application round available to applicants, typically through the applicant portal.

See also: Admission Decision, Applicant Portal, Waiting Period

#### Declining Offers

Formally telling colleges that an admitted student will not enroll, usually through the applicant portal, so the college can offer the spot to someone on its waitlist.

See also: Accepting an Offer, Waitlist

#### Dedicated Email Address

A separate, professional-sounding email account used only for college applications so messages from colleges are not lost among other mail.

**Example:** An address like firstname.lastname.college@gmail.com keeps admissions emails in one place.

See also: Getting Organized, Checking Portals Regularly

#### Deferral

A decision, usually given to early applicants, that postpones a final admission decision so the college can review the application again with the regular decision pool.

**Example:** A student deferred in December is reviewed again with regular decision applicants and hears back in spring.

See also: Admission Decision, Letter of Continued Interest, Early Action

#### Demonstrated Financial Need

The difference between a college's cost of attendance and the amount a family is expected to contribute, used to award need-based aid.

**Example:** If cost of attendance is $40,000 and the calculated family contribution is $15,000, demonstrated need is $25,000.

See also: Student Aid Index, Need-Based Aid, Meets Full Need

#### Demonstrated Interest

Evidence that an applicant has engaged with a specific college, such as visiting, attending information sessions, or opening emails, which some colleges consider when making decisions.

Not every college tracks it, so check each school's Common Data Set or website.

**Example:** Attending a virtual information session and registering for a campus tour both count as demonstrated interest at colleges that track it.

See also: Campus Visit, Admissions Information Session, Common Data Set

#### Denial

An admission decision in which a college does not offer the applicant a place in the incoming class, sometimes called a rejection.

A denial reflects the college's limited spots and priorities, not your worth.

**Example:** A student who is denied by her top choice focuses on the three colleges that admitted her.

See also: Admission Decision, Rejection as Fit, Healthy Coping Strategies

#### Depth Over Breadth

The principle that sustained, meaningful involvement in a few activities usually impresses admissions readers more than brief participation in many.

**Example:** Four years in a robotics team, rising to build captain, outweighs one-month memberships in eight clubs.

See also: Sustained Commitment, Well-Rounded Applicant Myth, Measurable Impact

#### Diagnostic Practice Test

A full-length practice exam taken at the start of test preparation to establish a baseline score and identify the skills that need the most work.

**Example:** A diagnostic test showing strong reading but weak algebra tells a student where to focus study time.

See also: Official Practice Tests, Error Log, Retesting Decision

#### Digital Document Folder

An online folder, such as in Google Drive or OneDrive, where a student stores application materials like transcripts, resumes, and essays.

See also: Filing System, Transcript Copies, Essay Drafts Folder

#### Distance From Home

How far a college is from a student's home, affecting travel cost, how often the student can return, and the level of independence involved.

**Example:** A student chooses a college within a three-hour drive so she can come home for holidays without buying plane tickets.

See also: Campus Setting, Personal Priorities, Indirect Costs

#### Double Depositing

Paying enrollment deposits at more than one college to hold multiple places, which is widely considered unethical and can lead to rescinded offers.

**Example:** A student who deposits at two colleges in May risks having both offers withdrawn if the schools find out.

See also: Enrollment Deposit, Rescinded Admission, Binding Commitment

#### Drafting

The stage of essay writing in which a student writes a complete first version, focusing on getting ideas and story onto the page rather than polishing sentences.

See also: Revision, Essay Drafts Folder, Brainstorming

#### Dual Enrollment

An arrangement in which a high school student takes college courses, often through a local community college, earning both high school and college credit.

**Example:** A junior takes English Composition at a community college and receives credit on both transcripts.

See also: College Credit in High School, Course Rigor, AP Credit Transfer

#### Early Action

An early application round in which a student submits by an earlier deadline and receives an earlier decision without any commitment to enroll if admitted.

**Example:** A student who applies early action to three colleges can hear back by winter and still compare offers until spring.

See also: Early Decision, Restrictive Early Action, Regular Decision

#### Early Decision

An early application round in which a student commits to enroll if admitted, applying to only one college this way and withdrawing other applications upon acceptance.

Early decision suits students with a clear first choice and confidence about affordability.

**Example:** A student applies early decision to her top school in November, is admitted in December, and withdraws all her other applications.

See also: Binding Commitment, Early Action, Early Decision Release

#### Early Decision Release

The process of being let out of an early decision commitment, usually only when the financial aid offer is truly unaffordable.

**Example:** A family shows that the aid offer leaves an unaffordable gap, and the college releases the student from the agreement.

See also: Early Decision, Binding Commitment, Net Price Calculator

#### Editing and Proofreading

The final stage of essay writing, in which the student corrects grammar, spelling, punctuation, and word choice and trims the essay to fit the word limit.

**Example:** Reading an essay aloud often catches missing words that the eye skips over.

#### Education Section

The part of the Common App where students list their current high school, counselor, graduation date, grade information, and any colleges or programs attended.

#### Enrollment Deposit

A payment an admitted student makes to a college to reserve a place in the incoming class, usually non-refundable and often credited toward the first tuition bill.

See also: Accepting an Offer, Double Depositing, May 1 Decision Deadline

#### Error Log

A record of practice-test mistakes, noting the question type and the reason for each error, used to target study efforts.

#### Essay Adaptation

Revising an existing essay to fit a new prompt or college, keeping strong material while tailoring details to the new audience.

**Example:** A student adapts a 650-word essay about her job into a 250-word supplement on responsibility.

#### Essay Drafts Folder

A dedicated place for storing every version of every essay, labeled clearly so a student can track revisions and reuse material.

#### Essay Material List

A collected inventory of stories, moments, traits, and experiences from brainstorming that a student can draw on for different essays.

#### Essay Structure

The organization of an essay's ideas, from opening through development to conclusion, that guides the reader through the story and its meaning.

**Example:** An essay might open in the middle of a scene, step back to explain context, and end with reflection on what changed.

See also: Opening Hook, Reflection and Insight, Drafting

#### Essay Topic Selection

Choosing the subject for an essay based on which idea best reveals the writer's character and offers the most room for reflection.

**Example:** A student chooses between writing about a sports injury or her family's weekend food truck and picks the food truck because it reveals more about her.

#### Essay Word Limit

The maximum number of words, or sometimes characters, that a college or application platform allows for a given essay or short-answer response.

**Example:** The Common App personal essay has a limit of 650 words.

See also: Personal Essay, Editing and Proofreading, Short-Answer Questions

#### Evaluative Interview

A college interview in which the interviewer's notes and impressions become part of the application file and can influence the admission decision.

**Example:** At a college with evaluative interviews, an interviewer's comments about a student's curiosity may be read during committee review.

See also: Informational Interview, Interviews

#### Expected Value of Applying

An estimate of how worthwhile a scholarship application is, weighing the award amount and likely chance of winning against the time it takes.

**Example:** A $1,000 local award with 20 applicants may be worth more effort than a $10,000 national award with 50,000 applicants.

#### Extracurricular Activities

Activities a student pursues outside regular class time, such as clubs, sports, paid jobs, the arts, community service, and family responsibilities, all of which count on applications.

#### FAFSA

The Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the U.S. government form used to apply for federal grants, loans, and work-study, and often state and college aid.

The people, such as the student, the student's parents, or a spouse, who must provide information and consent on the FAFSA for it to be complete.

#### FAFSA Opening Date

The date each year when the FAFSA becomes available for the next academic year, typically in the fall of a student's senior year.

#### Family Responsibilities

Duties a student carries at home, such as caring for siblings or relatives or helping with a family business, which count as meaningful activities on applications.

#### Family Section

#### Federal Aid

Financial aid funded by the U.S. government, including Pell Grants, Federal Direct Loans, and Federal Work-Study, which students apply for by completing the FAFSA.

#### Federal Direct Loan

A loan for students offered by the U.S. Department of Education, with fixed interest rates and borrower protections, available as subsidized or unsubsidized.

#### Feedback on Essays

Comments from trusted readers, such as teachers, counselors, or family members, about how clearly and effectively an essay communicates the writer's ideas.

#### FERPA Waiver

#### Final College Choice

#### Final College List

The settled set of colleges a student will actually apply to, ideally balanced across reach, target, and safety schools that all fit the student's needs.

#### Final Transcript

The completed high school transcript, sent to the chosen college after graduation, showing all senior-year grades and confirming that the student earned a diploma.

**Example:** A student's final transcript arrives at her college in July showing a strong spring semester and her graduation date.

#### Financial Aid

Money from the government, colleges, or private sources that helps students pay for college, including grants and scholarships that are not repaid and loans that are.

#### Financial Aid Appeal

A written request with supporting documentation that asks a college to reconsider a financial aid award, often due to new or unreported circumstances.

#### Financial Fit

How well a college's realistic cost after financial aid matches what a student and family can afford without taking on unmanageable debt.

**Example:** A college with a high sticker price can still be a financial fit if its net price calculator shows strong aid.

#### First-Come First-Served Aid

Financial aid that a program awards in the order completed applications are received, continuing until the available money runs out.

**Example:** Some state grant programs run out of funds early, so filing the FAFSA soon after it opens matters.

See also: Aid Priority Deadline, State Aid, FAFSA Opening Date

#### First-Generation Student

A student whose parents or guardians did not complete a four-year college degree, often making the student the first in the family to navigate college admissions.

Many colleges and scholarship programs offer extra support for first-generation students.

**Example:** A student whose parents attended community college but did not finish a bachelor's degree is often considered first-generation.

#### Five First Steps

A short starter set of actions this book recommends for beginning the application process, such as setting up a tracker and meeting a counselor.

See also: Getting Organized, Counselor Meeting, Deadline Tracker

#### Flagship University

The leading public university in a state's system, usually the oldest or most prominent campus and often the most selective.

**Example:** The University of Michigan in Ann Arbor is Michigan's flagship university.

See also: Public University, Research University, In-State Residency

#### Formula-Based Admissions

An admissions method in which a college decides primarily by applying set numerical criteria, such as a minimum GPA, class rank, or test score, to each application.

**Example:** A state university may guarantee admission to students in the top portion of their class who complete required courses.

#### Four-Year College

A college or university that awards bachelor's degrees, which full-time students typically complete in about four years, as distinct from two-year community colleges that award associate degrees.

#### Four-Year Cost Projection

An estimate of the total cost of a degree over four years, accounting for likely tuition increases and changes in aid.

**Example:** A student multiplies one year's net price by four and adds a few percent per year for price increases.

See also: Net Price, Renewable Scholarship, Total Debt at Graduation

#### Free Money vs. Borrowed Money

The distinction between aid that never has to be repaid, like grants and scholarships, and loans that must be repaid with interest.

**Example:** A $15,000 scholarship is free money, while a $5,500 loan in the same package is borrowed money that must be repaid.

#### Free Test Prep

No-cost resources for preparing for the SAT or ACT, such as official practice tests, online study programs, and school or library workshops.

**Example:** Khan Academy offers free, official SAT practice.

See also: Official Practice Tests, Error Log, Timed Practice

#### FSA ID

The username and password combination used to sign in to U.S. Department of Education websites and electronically sign the FAFSA.

**Example:** Both the student and a parent create FSA IDs at StudentAid.gov so they can each sign the FAFSA online.

#### Getting Organized

Setting up systems such as trackers, folders, and calendars to keep application materials, deadlines, and logins under control throughout senior year.

#### Getting Ready for Fall

The tasks a student completes between choosing a college and arriving on campus, such as housing, forms, orientation, and registration.

#### Grade Point Average

A numerical summary of a student's grades, calculated by converting each course grade to points and averaging them across courses.

**Example:** If a student earns two A's (4 points each) and two B's (3 points each), the GPA is 3.5.

#### Graduation Rate

The percentage of students who complete their degree at a college within a set time, commonly reported as six years for bachelor's programs.

**Example:** A college with a 90% six-year graduation rate sees nine of every ten students finish their degree within six years.

#### Grants

Financial aid awarded by the government, a state, or a college that does not have to be repaid, usually based on a student's financial need.

See also: Pell Grant, Scholarships, Loans vs. Grants

#### Health Forms and Immunizations

Medical history forms and vaccination records that colleges require incoming students to submit before they can register for classes or move into campus housing.

#### Healthy Coping Strategies

Positive ways of handling stress, such as exercise, talking with trusted people, taking breaks, and getting enough sleep, that support well-being without causing harm.

See also: Self-Care Routine, Support Network, Application Stress

#### High School Transcript

The official document issued by a high school that lists every course a student has taken, the grade earned in each, and usually credits and GPA.

**Example:** Your counselor sends your transcript showing grades from ninth grade through the end of junior year when you apply.

See also: Academic Record, Final Transcript, Sending Official Transcripts

#### Holistic Review

An evaluation method in which admissions readers consider an applicant's whole file, including grades, course rigor, essays, activities, recommendations, and personal context, rather than relying on numbers alone.

Holistic review is why essays and activities can make a real difference.

**Example:** Two students with identical GPAs may receive different decisions because one showed significant leadership and family responsibilities.

See also: Formula-Based Admissions, Selective Admissions, Academic Record

#### Honors and Awards

Formal recognitions a student has earned for academic or other achievements, listed in a dedicated section of many applications with the level of recognition.

#### Honors Courses

High school classes taught at a faster pace or greater depth than standard courses, often given extra weight in GPA calculations.

See also: Advanced Placement, Weighted GPA, Course Rigor

#### Hours per Week

The average weekly time a student spends on an activity, reported in the activities list to show the level of commitment.

**Example:** A student lists 20 hours per week during the season for varsity soccer.

#### Housing Application

A form an incoming student submits to request on-campus housing, typically listing preferences for residence halls, room types, and roommates.

**Example:** A student submits a housing application in May, ranking three residence halls and requesting a quiet floor.

#### IB Diploma

The full International Baccalaureate credential, earned by completing courses across subject groups plus an extended essay, a theory of knowledge course, and service requirements.

**Example:** A student completes six IB subjects, a 4,000-word extended essay, and service projects to earn the IB Diploma.

See also: International Baccalaureate, Course Rigor

#### In-State Residency

A student's legal status as a resident of a state, which qualifies the student for lower tuition and sometimes state aid at that state's public colleges.

#### Independent Counselor

A privately hired admissions advisor who works outside the high school to help students with college lists, essays, and applications for a fee.

An independent counselor is optional and does not replace the school counselor's official role.

#### Indirect Costs

Expenses of attending college that the school does not bill directly, such as books, supplies, transportation, and personal items, but that families must still budget for.

#### Informational Interview

An interview mainly intended to let the applicant learn about the college and ask questions, with little or no effect on the admission decision.

#### Institution Codes

Identification numbers that testing organizations and application services use to route scores and documents to the correct college or scholarship program.

#### Institutional Aid

Financial aid that a college awards from its own funds, including need-based grants and merit scholarships, as opposed to federal or state aid.

#### Institutional Application

An application form a college creates and hosts on its own website, used instead of or in addition to shared platforms.

**Example:** Some public universities accept only their own institutional application.

#### Institutional Priorities

The goals a college is trying to meet in shaping its incoming class, such as filling certain majors, enrolling students from particular regions, or recruiting athletes and musicians.

**Example:** A college building a new music program may admit more oboe players one year than it usually does.

#### Intended Major

The field of study a student expects to pursue in college, which may be indicated on applications and can affect admission at some schools or programs.

Some majors, such as nursing or engineering, may be more competitive than the college overall.

**Example:** A student lists computer science as an intended major and applies directly to the engineering school.

#### International Baccalaureate

An internationally recognized program offering rigorous high school courses with external exams, available as individual courses or as a full diploma program.

See also: IB Diploma, Advanced Placement, Course Rigor

#### Interview Follow-Up

A brief thank-you message an applicant sends soon after an interview, mentioning something specific from the conversation to show appreciation and interest.

**Example:** A student emails her interviewer the next day, thanking him and mentioning the robotics lab he described.

See also: Thank-You Notes, Interviews

#### Interview Preparation

Getting ready for a college interview by researching the school, reviewing one's own experiences and goals, and practicing answers to common questions.

**Example:** A student reviews the college's website, rereads her activities list, and practices answering "Why this college?"

See also: Practice Interview, Questions to Ask Interviewer, School-Specific Research

#### Interviews

Conversations between an applicant and a college representative, used to share information about the college and, at some schools, to evaluate the applicant.

See also: Alumni Interview, Evaluative Interview, Interview Preparation

#### Leadership Roles

Positions in which a student takes responsibility for guiding others or organizing projects, whether through an elected title or informal initiative.

Leadership includes starting a project or training coworkers, not just holding an officer title.

**Example:** A student who organizes a weekly study group for younger players on her team is showing leadership without a title.

See also: Extracurricular Activities, Measurable Impact

#### Legacy Status

An applicant's family connection to a college, usually through a parent or grandparent who graduated from it, which some colleges consider in admissions.

A growing number of colleges have stopped considering legacy status.

**Example:** A student whose father graduated from a college marks that connection in the family section of the application.

#### Letter of Continued Interest

A short letter or email from a deferred or waitlisted applicant that reaffirms interest in the college and shares any new accomplishments since applying.

**Example:** A waitlisted student writes a one-page letter mentioning her new science award and confirming the college remains her first choice.

#### Letters of Recommendation

Written evaluations from teachers, counselors, or others who know an applicant well, describing the student's character, abilities, and contributions from an outside perspective.

#### Liberal Arts College

A usually small, undergraduate-focused college that emphasizes broad study across the humanities, sciences, and social sciences, with small classes and close contact with professors.

**Example:** A student who wants seminar-style classes of 15 students might focus on liberal arts colleges.

#### Limiting Comparison

The deliberate practice of reducing how often a student measures their applications, scores, or results against classmates', in order to lower stress and protect confidence.

**Example:** A student stops asking friends about their test scores and focuses on her own list.

#### Living Learning Community

A residence hall group where students who share an academic interest or theme live together and take part in related activities.

**Example:** Future engineering majors may live on the same floor and share a study group.

See also: Housing Application, Social Fit

#### Loans vs. Grants

The comparison between borrowed aid that must be repaid and gift aid that does not, central to judging an aid package's real value.

**Example:** Two packages that both total $30,000 differ greatly if one is all grants and the other is half loans.

#### Local Scholarship

An outside scholarship offered by a community organization, business, or group in a student's area, often with fewer applicants than national awards.

#### Login Credentials Record

A secure record of the usernames and account details a student uses for application portals and financial aid sites, kept safe so accounts are not lost.

#### Long-Term Perspective

Viewing college admissions as one step in a longer life path, recognizing that success depends more on what a student does in college than where they attend.

#### Managing Uncertainty

Handling the stress of unknown admission outcomes by focusing on steps within one's control, keeping several good options, and maintaining a realistic perspective.

#### Measurable Impact

#### Meets Full Need

#### Member Questions

#### Merit Aid

Financial aid awarded for a student's academic achievements, talents, leadership, or other qualities, regardless of the family's financial situation or demonstrated need.

#### Mid-Year Report

A form and updated transcript that the school counselor sends to colleges, typically in winter, showing a student's first-semester senior-year grades.

#### Middle 50% Range

The span of GPAs or test scores between the 25th and 75th percentiles of a college's admitted or enrolled students, showing where the central half of the class falls.

**Example:** If a college's SAT middle 50% range is 1250-1400, a student with a 1320 sits inside it.

#### Month-by-Month Planner

A planning tool that breaks senior-year tasks into monthly goals so work is spread out instead of piling up at deadlines.

#### Move-In Day

The scheduled day when a new student arrives on campus, moves into assigned housing, and begins the transition to college life.

#### Narrowing the List

The process of removing colleges from a long list based on fit, cost, and research until a manageable set of applications remains.

#### Need-Aware Admissions

An admissions policy in which a college may consider an applicant's ability to pay in some or all admission decisions.

#### Need-Based Aid

Financial aid awarded according to a family's ability to pay, as determined from information on forms such as the FAFSA or CSS Profile.

#### Need-Blind Admissions

An admissions policy in which a college does not consider an applicant's ability to pay when deciding whether to admit.

**Example:** At a need-blind college, readers decide on a student's application without knowing whether she applied for aid.

#### Net Price

The amount a student actually pays for a year of college after subtracting grants and scholarships from the cost of attendance.

#### Net Price Calculator

A tool on each college's website that estimates a student's likely net price at that school based on the family's income, assets, and other financial information.

#### No Single Right School Myth

The mistaken belief that one perfect college exists for each student and that missing it means failure, when in fact many colleges can be a good fit.

Letting go of this myth reduces stress and leads to a more balanced list.

**Example:** A student who loved one college but was denied ends up thriving at her second choice, which offered a similar program.

#### Official Practice Tests

#### Official Score Report

#### Opening Hook

The first sentence or few lines of an essay, written to grab the reader's attention and draw them into the story.

#### Optional Essays

Supplemental essays a college invites but does not require, which can still strengthen an application when the student has something meaningful to add.

#### Orientation

A program for incoming students, held before or at the start of the first term, that introduces campus resources, academic policies, and student life, and often includes course registration.

#### Out-of-Pocket Cost

The amount a family must actually pay or borrow after all grants and scholarships are applied to the cost of attendance.

#### Out-of-State Applicant

A student applying to a public college in a state where the student is not a legal resident, typically facing higher tuition and sometimes a different admission standard.

#### Outside Scholarship

A scholarship awarded by an organization other than the college the student attends, such as a business, foundation, employer, or community group.

#### Overused Essay Topics

Subjects so common in application essays, such as winning the big game or a service trip, that they are hard to make distinctive.

#### Parent and Family Role

#### Reach School

A college where a student's admission is unlikely or uncertain because the student's credentials fall below the typical admitted range or the college admits very few applicants.

Carefully examining a financial aid award letter to separate grants from loans, identify the true out-of-pocket cost, and spot expenses the letter leaves out.

The choice of whether to take the SAT or ACT again, weighing likely score gains against the time, cost, and stress of another attempt.

Rewriting an essay to improve its content, structure, and clarity, often through several rounds of substantial changes rather than small word fixes.

#### Rolling Admission

An admissions approach in which a college reviews applications as they arrive and releases decisions on an ongoing basis until the class is full.

Applying early in a rolling cycle can improve your chances because spaces may fill.

**Example:** A student submits to a rolling-admission university in September and receives a decision four weeks later.

The combined charge a college sets for on-campus housing and a meal plan, usually listed as a major part of the cost of attendance for students who live on campus.

#### Safety School

A college where a student is very likely to be admitted and can afford to attend, and that the student would be genuinely happy to enroll in.

Fraudulent offers that pretend to provide scholarships, grants, or financial aid help but are designed to take a student's money or personal information.

A website that matches students with possible scholarships by comparing a profile of their interests, background, location, and achievements against a database of awards.

Money awarded to students for college that does not have to be repaid, based on merit, need, talents, or other criteria.

The high school staff member who guides students through course planning and college applications and who sends transcripts and a counselor recommendation.

The number of students enrolled at a college, which shapes class sizes, the variety of programs, and the feel of campus life.

Investigating a particular college's programs, courses, traditions, and opportunities so that supplemental essays and interviews include specific, accurate details about that school.

An admissions approach in which a college admits only a portion of qualified applicants, choosing among them based on academic and personal factors rather than accepting everyone who meets minimum requirements.

A chart or spreadsheet that lines up colleges using the same categories, such as cost, programs, and location, to make differences clear.

An essay built around a brief, ordinary experience that reveals something meaningful about the writer, rather than a large dramatic event.

#### Social Fit

How well a college's student body, campus life, traditions, and community match the kind of environment in which a student feels comfortable and engaged.

#### State Aid

See also: Weighted GPA, Grade Point Average, Course Rigor

#### Verification

A process in which a college asks a student to confirm the information reported on the FAFSA by submitting documents such as tax records.

**Example:** A student selected for verification uploads a parent's tax transcript to the financial aid office.

See also: FAFSA, Applicant Portal

#### Verifying Current Policies

The practice of checking each college's official website for its latest requirements, deadlines, and testing rules, because policies change from year to year.

**Example:** Before applying, a student confirms on a college's admissions page that it is still test-optional this year.

See also: College-Specific Requirements, Test-Optional Policy

#### Virtual Interview

A college interview held over a video platform instead of in person, following the same format and expectations as a face-to-face conversation with an alum or admissions officer.

**Example:** A student tests the camera and lighting, picks a quiet room, and joins five minutes early.

See also: Interviews, Interview Preparation

#### Virtual Tour

An online walkthrough of a college campus, using video, photos, or interactive maps, that lets students explore buildings and grounds without traveling.

See also: Campus Visit, College Research Tools, Demonstrated Interest

#### Waiting Period

The stretch of time between submitting applications and receiving admission decisions, during which the applicant has little control over outcomes and often feels anxious.

**Example:** Between a November submission and a March decision, a student might focus on senior grades and scholarships.

See also: Managing Uncertainty, Decision Release Date, Healthy Coping Strategies

#### Waitlist

A decision that places a qualified applicant on a list of students who may be admitted later if spaces open in the class.

**Example:** A student is offered a waitlist spot in March and is admitted in May when a space opens.

See also: Waitlist Strategy, Letter of Continued Interest, Admission Decision

#### Waitlist Strategy

A plan for responding to a waitlist offer, including accepting a spot, sending updates, and committing elsewhere in the meantime.

**Example:** A waitlisted student deposits at another college by the deadline while sending a continued-interest letter to the waitlist school.

See also: Waitlist, Letter of Continued Interest, Enrollment Deposit

#### Weighted GPA

A grade point average that awards extra points for grades earned in more demanding courses, such as honors, AP, or IB classes, so the scale can exceed the standard maximum.

**Example:** At a school that weights AP courses, an A in AP Biology might count as 5.0 instead of 4.0.

See also: Unweighted GPA, Honors Courses, Advanced Placement

#### Well-Rounded Applicant Myth

The mistaken belief that colleges want applicants who do a little of everything, when many value focused depth in a few areas that builds a well-rounded class overall.

**Example:** A student deeply committed to theater and stage design can be more compelling than one who joined ten unrelated clubs.

See also: Admissions Myths, Depth Over Breadth, Sustained Commitment

#### Why This Major Essay

A supplemental essay asking why an applicant wants to study a chosen field, connecting past experiences to future academic plans.

**Example:** A student connects her experience volunteering at a vet clinic to her plan to study animal science.

See also: Intended Major, School-Specific Research, Supplemental Essays

#### Why Us Essay

A supplemental essay asking why an applicant wants to attend a particular college, answered best with specific details linking the school to the student's goals.

**Example:** A student mentions a specific marine biology field station and a student-run research journal at the college.

See also: School-Specific Research, Supplemental Essays, Recycled Essay Trap

#### Work-Study

A financial aid program that provides part-time jobs to students with financial need, letting them earn money for college expenses.

**Example:** A work-study student earns a paycheck working at the campus library 10 hours a week.

See also: Federal Aid, Need-Based Aid, Award Letter

#### Writing Section

The part of the Common App that holds the personal essay along with other writing, such as optional statements about challenges or disciplinary history.

See also: Personal Essay, Additional Information Section, Common App Essay Prompts

#### Writing Supplements

The college-specific essays and short-answer questions that appear in an applicant's Common App account after a particular college is added to the list.

See also: Supplemental Essays, Member Questions, Supplement Planning
