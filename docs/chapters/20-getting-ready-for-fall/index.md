## From Decision to Day One

Committing to a college by May 1 can feel like the end of the process. It isn't. (Sorry.) It's the start of a different, more practical one. The months between your deposit and move-in day are full of forms and small deadlines, such as housing, orientation, health records, and course registration. They're easy to lose track of once the emotional weight of decisions has lifted and senior year is ending.

This chapter carries you from your deposit to your first day on campus. You'll plan the summer transition, apply for housing and choose a roommate, register for orientation, sort out placement tests and AP credit, register for classes, complete health forms, and avoid **summer melt**, the surprisingly common problem of admitted students who never arrive. Then the book closes by pulling everything together. You'll get a month-by-month planner for senior year, a reminder to verify every policy for your own year, and a complete application roadmap for one real school, the University of Iowa, from the first spreadsheet to move-in day.

## Getting Ready for Fall

**Getting ready for fall** means completing the steps between committing to a college and starting classes there: housing, orientation, health requirements, credit and placement, registration, finances, and the personal transition. Most of these steps open as soon as you pay your enrollment deposit (Chapter 19), and many have deadlines in May and June, earlier than students expect.

The best approach is the same one that worked for applications: **put every task and deadline in your tracker** the week you commit. Your college's admitted-student portal usually lists them in a "next steps" checklist. Iowa's, for instance, unlocks orientation reservations only after the acceptance fee is paid. Check that portal and the email address from Chapter 5 at least weekly through the summer. Colleges communicate almost everything by email, and a missed message about housing or registration can cost you your best options.

Three things are worth doing in the first week after you commit:

1. **Copy every summer deadline** from the college's portal into your tracker and calendar.
2. **Submit the housing application,** if the college has one, since many housing choices go in order of application.
3. **Register for orientation,** since popular dates fill early.

## Housing and Community

### The Housing Application

The **housing application** is the form you complete to live in a college's residence halls, usually opened after you accept admission and often requiring its own deposit or fee. It typically asks for your preferences for residence halls and room types, your roommate preferences or a lifestyle questionnaire for matching, and any accommodations you need for a disability or medical condition. Those should be flagged early, with documentation.

Submit it **as early as your college allows.** At many colleges, room selection or assignment goes in order of when applications were submitted. At Iowa, for example, students who apply for housing earlier get earlier room selection times, and students who finish their housing application by May 1 and schedule orientation by May 15 can choose their own rooms online in June. Students who apply later are assigned by staff. Read the housing contract before signing. It's often binding for the full academic year, and its cancellation terms and fees matter if your plans change.

### Roommate Selection

**Roommate selection** is how you're matched with the person or people you'll share a room with. You may be able to choose someone you know, search for a match through the college's housing portal, or be assigned using a lifestyle questionnaire. Answer the questionnaire honestly, especially about sleep schedule, tidiness, and noise, since it's designed to help you, not to impress anyone. Once you know your roommate, connect before move-in. Talk about who's bringing what, such as a mini-fridge or a rug, and about living habits like sleep times, guests, and study space. A friendly conversation in July prevents a lot of awkwardness in September.

### Living Learning Communities

A **living learning community** (LLC) is a residence hall floor or building where students who share an academic interest or identity live together, often with linked courses, faculty involvement, and special events. Examples include communities for engineering, the arts, first-generation students, honors, or students planning to teach. Research suggests LLCs can help first-year students feel at home faster and stay in college. They often require a short application through the housing portal, with deadlines in spring. At Iowa, for example, the LLC application opens in the housing portal after you submit the main housing application. If an LLC matches your interests, apply. It's one of the easiest ways to make a large campus feel small.

## Orientation and Academics

### Orientation

**Orientation** is the program a college runs to prepare new students for their first semester, usually held over one to three days in summer, sometimes with a second program right before classes. Orientation typically includes academic advising, **course registration**, placement testing, campus tours, student ID photos, and sessions on campus life. Families often have a parallel program.

Orientation isn't optional, and it isn't just social. At many colleges, it's where you register for your first-semester classes, so a late orientation date can mean registering after popular sections fill. **Register for the earliest date that works** as soon as registration opens. Some colleges also hold a welcome week just before classes. Iowa's, called **On Iowa!**, runs the week before fall classes, and Iowa's acceptance fee covers it.

#### Worked Example: Getting the Most From Orientation

Marcus attends Lakeshore's two-day orientation in June. He prepares by:

- Taking the online math placement test two weeks ahead.
- Confirming his AP and community college credit had arrived, by checking his student portal.
- Reading the computer science degree requirements and drafting a list of first-semester courses, with alternatives.

At his advising appointment, he learns that his community college Discrete Mathematics course transferred as a CS requirement, opening a seat in his schedule. He registers before lunch on day one and gets the intro CS section with the peer-mentoring lab he'd heard about at the admitted student day.

### Placement Tests

**Placement tests** are assessments a college uses to place new students in the right level of a subject, most often math, world languages, chemistry, and sometimes writing, so you don't start in a course that's too easy or too hard. They're usually taken online before orientation. They don't affect admission and aren't graded in the usual sense, but they do matter. Placing into a higher math course can save you a semester, and placing too high can mean struggling. Take them seriously, without outside help, and review the basics beforehand if you haven't taken that subject recently. AP scores and transfer credit may exempt you from some placement tests, so check first.

### AP Credit Transfer

**AP credit transfer** is the process of having your AP exam scores, IB results, or dual-enrollment courses recognized by your college as credit or placement. Chapter 7 explained that each college sets its own policy. Now it's time to act on it. Send your **official AP score report** from the College Board to your college. You get **one free score send per year** if you choose your college by **June 20**, and scores sent after that cost a fee. For dual enrollment, request an **official transcript** from the college that granted the credit (Chapter 7). Then check your college's credit policy pages and student portal to confirm how each score or course was applied, and ask an advisor at orientation before registering. Credit that hasn't posted by registration can mean signing up for a class you didn't need.

### Course Registration

**Course registration** is choosing and enrolling in your first-semester classes, usually during or right after orientation, with help from an academic advisor. Before registering, read your intended major's requirements and your college's general education requirements, note which credits from AP or dual enrollment apply, and draft a schedule with **backup choices,** since some sections will fill. A typical first semester is 12 to 16 credits, often four or five courses. Many advisors recommend a balanced first semester that mixes one or two major courses, general education requirements, and one class you simply want to take. Keep your scholarship and aid requirements in mind, since many require full-time enrollment (Chapter 15).

## Health and Finances

### Health Forms and Immunizations

**Health forms and immunizations** are the medical records colleges require before you can attend classes or move into housing, typically proof of required vaccinations, sometimes a physical exam or health history form, and proof of health insurance or enrollment in the college's student health plan. Requirements vary by state and college, and deadlines often fall in June or July, earlier than many students expect. Request your immunization records from your doctor or school nurse early, since some vaccines may require a second dose weeks later. Decide whether to keep your family's health insurance or enroll in the college's plan, since many colleges charge for their plan automatically unless you waive it by a deadline. If you have a disability or ongoing health condition, contact the college's disability services office in the summer to set up accommodations before classes start.

### Summer Melt

**Summer melt** is when a student who has been admitted, committed, and intends to enroll in college doesn't show up in the fall. Research by Benjamin Castleman and Lindsay Page found that between **10 and 40 percent** of college-intending students in the districts they studied didn't enroll anywhere that fall, with the highest rates among students from low- and moderate-income families and those planning to attend community college.

Summer melt usually isn't a change of heart. It's a pile-up of small obstacles: a missed email, an unexpected bill, a confusing form, a verification request, a missing transcript, or a summer job that makes it hard to get to orientation, without a counselor or teacher nearby to help, since school is out. The protection is simple and surprisingly effective. Castleman and Page found that low-cost reminders and access to help in the summer increased enrollment. Keep your tracker going all summer, check your college email weekly, respond to every request quickly, and **know who to call**: your college's admissions or first-year office, its financial aid office, or your high school counselor, many of whom check email over the summer. If something seems like it might stop you from enrolling, ask for help right away. It's almost always fixable if you ask early.

#### Worked Example: A Near-Melt

In July, Ana receives a bill from Northfield for $1,800 more than she expected. She panics. Her family can't pay it, and she starts to wonder whether she should enroll at all. Instead, she calls Northfield's financial aid office the next morning. The staff member finds the problem in ten minutes. Her state grant hadn't posted yet because a verification document was missing, and the bill showed the amount without the grant. Ana uploads the document that afternoon, and the corrected bill arrives a week later.

That's what summer melt looks like up close: a fixable problem that feels like a dead end. One phone call made the difference.

### Summer Transition Planning

**Summer transition planning** is organizing the summer between high school and college so that every required step gets done and you arrive ready, practically, financially, and personally. Beyond the tasks above, it includes a few things that are easy to forget:

- **Finances:** confirm your final aid package, accept or decline loans, complete any loan counseling and promissory notes required for first-time federal borrowers, set up a payment plan if you need one, and make sure your first bill is paid by its deadline.
- **Final transcript:** confirm with your counselor that it's been sent and received (Chapter 7).
- **Logistics:** check your room's dimensions and the college's list of prohibited items before you buy anything, and plan how you'll get to campus.
- **The personal side:** spend time with family and friends, and talk honestly about the transition. Excitement and nervousness usually arrive together, for students and for the people who raised them. That's normal.

### Move-In Day

**Move-in day** is the day you move into your residence hall and begin college, usually a few days to a week before classes start, often scheduled by building or time slot to manage crowds. Bring your college ID or confirmation, your room key instructions, and only what fits your room, following the college's packing list. Arrive at your assigned time. Expect long lines and a little chaos, and expect help. Many colleges have volunteers ready to carry boxes. Meet your roommate and neighbors, find your classrooms before the first day, and go to the welcome events. It's the fastest way to start feeling at home. Then let your family go, and know that the nervousness most people feel that night usually fades within a week or two.

#### Worked Example: Emily's Move-In Day

Emily's move-in slot at Hillcrest is 9:00 a.m. on a Thursday. Her parents drive her the four hours, and student volunteers unload the car in ten minutes. By noon her room is set up. She and her roommate, whom she'd texted with all summer, have agreed on a rug and a lamp. In the afternoon, she walks the route to her first-year seminar room and finds the literary review's office in the student union. At the floor meeting that evening, she volunteers to organize a Sunday study group.

When her parents call that night, she tells them she's tired and nervous and happy. All three are true, and all three are normal.

### Verifying Current Policies

**Verifying current policies** means checking each college's current rules, deadlines, and requirements for your own application year, rather than relying on last year's information, a friend's experience, or even a book. Admissions and aid policies change constantly. In recent years, colleges have moved between test-optional and test-required, the FAFSA has been redesigned, federal loan limits and the Pell Grant rules have changed, and colleges have added and dropped essays, interviews, and deadlines. Every fact in this book was checked carefully, and some will still be out of date by the time you read it. Before you rely on any date, number, or rule, confirm it on the **college's own website** or **studentaid.gov**, and when in doubt, email the admissions or financial aid office. Record what you confirm and when in your tracker.

## Key Takeaways

- **Getting ready for fall** starts the week you commit. Copy every deadline into your tracker, and check your college email weekly all summer.
- Submit the **housing application** early. Answer **roommate selection** questionnaires honestly, and consider a **living learning community**.
- Register early for **orientation**, where advising and **course registration** often happen. Take **placement tests** seriously.
- Complete **AP credit transfer**: use the free AP score send by June 20, and send dual-enrollment transcripts.
- Finish **health forms and immunizations** early, and decide about health insurance before the waiver deadline.
- Prevent **summer melt**. Respond quickly to every request, and know who to call. Most obstacles are fixable if you ask early.
- **Summer transition planning** covers finances, your final transcript, logistics, and the personal side. **Move-in day** is the beginning, not the finish line.
- Use the **month-by-month planner** to see the whole year. **Verify current policies** for your own year. Build a **capstone application roadmap** for each college on your list.
