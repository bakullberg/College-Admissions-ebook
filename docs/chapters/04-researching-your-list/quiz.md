# Quiz: Researching and Narrowing Your College List

Test your understanding of college research tools, outcome data, campus visits, and narrowing a long list to a final one with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Who publishes the College Scorecard, and what kind of information is it strongest on?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The College Board, strongest on test-score ranges and admission rates
2. Each state's higher education board, strongest on in-state tuition
3. Each college's institutional research office, strongest on class sizes
4. The U.S. Department of Education, strongest on cost and outcomes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The College Scorecard is a free website from the U.S. Department of Education. It reports standardized data for nearly every college that accepts federal aid, including average net price by family income, graduation rate, typical debt, and typical earnings. Its admissions data is thinner, so the chapter suggests pairing it with the Common Data Set, which each college publishes itself. The College Board runs the BigFuture search site, a third-party tool whose numbers often come from these sources.

#### 2. Which organization does the chapter name as the one that runs large national college fairs?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The U.S. Department of Education
2. The College Board
3. The National Association for College Admission Counseling (NACAC)
4. The Common Application
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says NACAC, the National Association for College Admission Counseling, runs large national college fairs, while many high schools and regions hold their own. A college fair brings representatives from many colleges to one place, so it's an efficient way to discover new schools and meet the admissions officer who may read your application. The Department of Education runs the College Scorecard, and the College Board runs BigFuture, a search site.

#### 3. How many colleges does the chapter say a long list usually contains?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Five to seven
2. Eight to twelve
3. Fifteen to twenty-five
4. Thirty to forty
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A long list is your first, broad list of colleges worth researching, usually fifteen to twenty-five schools. It should come from your priorities, stretch beyond familiar names, and span reaches, targets, and safeties. Eight to twelve is the size of the final college list, which you reach by narrowing the long list in deliberate passes. In the chapter's example, Marcus builds a long list of eighteen and narrows it to ten.

#### 4. What habit does the chapter recommend for combining data tools and experience tools?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Use experience tools for cost questions and data tools for social fit
2. Use data to decide which colleges to look at, and experience to decide which feel right
3. Use experience tools first, then check only your favorite colleges' data
4. Rely on data tools alone, since experience tools are designed to recruit you
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Data tools, such as the College Scorecard and Common Data Set, answer questions about admission chances, cost, and outcomes. Experience tools, such as visits and student reviews, answer questions about culture and social fit. The chapter says you need both: data alone can make an unhappy fit look perfect, and experience alone can make you fall for a campus you can't afford. The option pairing experience with cost and data with social fit reverses each tool's strength.

#### 5. Why does the chapter call the retention rate an early warning signal?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It measures how quickly the college fills its class under rolling admission
2. It shows whether first-years return, and it reacts faster than the graduation rate
3. It reports how many admitted students choose to enroll each year
4. It predicts which applicants will be placed on the waitlist
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The retention rate is the percentage of first-time, full-time first-year students who return for their second year. Students who leave after one year usually do so because of cost, academic difficulty, or poor social fit. Retention and graduation rates usually move together, but retention reacts faster, so a low retention rate can flag problems early. It measures students who return, not admitted students who enroll, and it has nothing to do with waitlists.

    **Concept Tested:** Retention Rate

#### 6. Why should a single dramatic student review be given less weight than other evidence?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Colleges write most of the reviews that appear on review sites
2. Reviews only describe students who transferred away from the college
3. Review sites remove most negative reviews before posting them
4. Reviews are unverified and self-selected, so very good or bad experiences are overrepresented
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Student reviews can reveal things marketing doesn't, but the chapter says they are unverified and self-selected. Students with very good or very bad experiences post far more often than everyone in between. That's why you should look for patterns across many reviews and give more weight to specific details than to general feelings. The chapter doesn't claim colleges write the reviews or that sites delete negative ones.

    **Concept Tested:** Student Reviews

#### 7. A student wants to know how many of a college's classes are small and how many are large. Which Common Data Set section should they check?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Section C, first-year admission
2. Section B, enrollment and persistence
3. Section I, faculty and class size
4. Section H, financial aid
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. In the Common Data Set, Section I covers faculty and class size, including how many classes are small and how many are large. Section B covers enrollment, graduation, and retention. Section C covers first-year admission, including middle 50% ranges and demonstrated interest. Section H covers need-based and merit aid. Knowing the sections lets you compare colleges question by question, since every college uses the same format.

    **Concept Tested:** Common Data Set

    **See:** [Researching and Narrowing Your College List](index.md)

#### 8. Jordan has one afternoon on a college campus after the official tour ends. Which step follows the chapter's advice for getting the most from a visit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Ask a student in the library what they like least about the college
2. Retake the tour with a different guide to compare answers
3. Spend the time in the admissions office collecting brochures
4. Wait until getting home a week later to write down impressions
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter advises going beyond the tour and talking to students who weren't assigned to talk to you. Asking a student what they like least often tells you more than the tour, because tour guides are trained, enthusiastic volunteers. A second tour or a stack of brochures gives you more of the college's own message. The chapter also says to take notes within an hour of leaving, since campuses blur together after a few visits.

    **Concept Tested:** Campus Visits

#### 9. While narrowing a long list, a student runs a net price calculator and finds that a famous university would cost far more than the family can pay. According to the three-pass process, what should happen?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Cut it in Pass 1, because it clearly fails a dealbreaker no matter how famous it is
2. Move it to the safety category, since cost does not affect admission
3. Keep it until Pass 2, then label it a reach to balance the list
4. Keep it until Pass 3, since the student might picture being happy there
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Pass 1 cuts on dealbreakers and fit. If the net price calculator shows a college is out of reach, the chapter says to cut it no matter how famous it is. Cutting on fit first means you never waste time classifying colleges that wouldn't work anyway. Pass 2 classifies the remaining schools and balances categories, and Pass 3 tests whether you could picture yourself happy there. A college you can't afford can never be a true safety.

    **Concept Tested:** Narrowing the List

#### 10. College P has an 80% retention rate, a 40% four-year graduation rate, and a 78% six-year rate. College Q has a 92% retention rate, a 65% four-year rate, and a 75% six-year rate. Which conclusion does the data best support?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. College Q's students are less supported, because fewer finish in six years
2. College P is the better value, because its six-year rate is higher
3. The two colleges are equivalent, because their six-year rates are close
4. At College P, many students take extra years, which adds cost, and more first-years leave
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. College P's six-year rate looks solid, but the large gap between its four-year and six-year rates means many students take five or six years, which adds tuition and living costs. Its lower retention rate is also a warning sign that more first-years leave. Looking only at six-year rates hides these differences, so treating the colleges as equal misses the point. College Q's high retention and four-year rate suggest students stay and finish on time.

    **Concept Tested:** Graduation Rate
