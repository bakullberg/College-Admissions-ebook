## Why Start With the Map

Before you build a list or write a word of an essay, it helps to know the ground you're crossing. Applying to college has its own vocabulary — *Early Action*, *flagship*, *admit rate*, *binding* — and admissions offices use those words as if everyone already knows them. Students with a parent or private counselor who has done this before pick the vocabulary up at the dinner table. Everyone else has to learn it from scratch, usually in a panic in October.

This chapter is that vocabulary, in the order you'll need it. You'll start with the process as a whole and the calendar it runs on. Then you'll look at the kinds of four-year colleges you can apply to and what "selective" really means. You'll meet the people who read your application. Finally, you'll learn the different application rounds, which ones lock you in, and which ones keep your options open. Every later chapter builds on these terms.

## The College Admissions Process

The **college admissions process** is the full sequence of steps a student goes through to get from "I want to go to college" to "I'm enrolled at a college this fall." It is not one application. It's a project with six stages, and each stage hands its results to the next one:

1. **Build your list.** Decide which colleges to apply to, based on fit, cost, and your chances of admission.
2. **Test.** Decide whether to take the SAT or ACT, and whether to send your scores to each school.
3. **Apply.** Complete each application: forms, essays, activities, and recommendations.
4. **Seek aid.** File the financial aid forms and apply for scholarships so each school can tell you what you'd actually pay.
5. **Decide.** Compare the offers you receive and choose one school.
6. **Enroll.** Pay a deposit, decline the other offers, and complete the steps that get you to campus in the fall.

The stages overlap. You'll usually be filing financial aid forms while you're still finishing applications, and testing often happens while you're still settling your list. But the order matters, because each stage depends on the one before it. You can't write a "Why this college?" essay for a school that isn't on your list yet. You can't compare financial aid offers for schools you never applied to.

The process is long. For most students, it runs from the summer before senior year to the fall after graduation, about fifteen months. That length is the reason so many students feel behind: there's rarely a single moment when someone tells you to start. The good news is that a long process is forgiving. Most of it is small tasks done steadily, not one heroic weekend.

#### Worked Example: Where Is Priya Right Now?

It's late September. Priya, a senior, has a list of ten colleges, took the SAT once in June, and has a rough draft of her personal essay. She hasn't opened a financial aid form yet.

- **Build your list:** mostly done. She has a list, but she still needs to check that it includes schools she's very likely to get into.

## The Admissions Timeline

The **admissions timeline** is the calendar the process runs on: the months when each stage usually happens and the fixed dates the whole system shares. Individual colleges set their own deadlines, but most of them cluster around the same few anchor points, so the senior year of nearly every applicant has the same rhythm.

Here are the anchor points worth knowing now. Later chapters explain each one in depth.

- **August 1:** The Common App, the shared application most colleges accept, opens for the new year. You'll set it up in [Chapter 5](../05-getting-organized/index.md).
- **Fall (traditionally October 1):** The FAFSA, the federal financial aid form, opens. Its exact opening date has shifted in recent years, so confirm the current one. [Chapter 14](../14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md) covers it.
- **November 1 and November 15:** The most common early application deadlines.
- **Mid-December:** Most early decisions arrive.
- **January 1 to February 1:** The most common regular deadlines.
- **Mid-March to early April:** Most regular decisions arrive.
- **May 1:** The national deadline for choosing one college and paying a deposit. [Chapter 19](../19-making-your-final-choice/index.md) covers it.
- **June through August:** Housing forms, orientation, and final transcripts.

Notice how lopsided the calendar is. Almost all of the work (lists, testing, essays, applications, aid forms) is packed into August through January. February is quiet. Then March and April bring a rush of decisions that require careful comparison, and May 1 forces a choice. Planning around that shape is most of what it means to "start early."

#### Worked Example: Planning Backward From a Deadline

Suppose your earliest application is due November 1, and one of your recommending teachers asks for at least four weeks' notice. Work backward:

- **November 1:** Application due. Aim to submit by October 25 to leave room for technical problems.
- **October 25:** Final proofread of the essays and the whole application.
- **Around October 1:** The teacher needs the request by now, four weeks before the deadline. Ask in September to be safe.
- **Mid-September:** A full essay draft should exist so there's time for two rounds of revision.

The deadline on the college's website is November 1, but your real deadline for asking a teacher is September. Planning backward like this is how the timeline turns into a to-do list. [Chapter 5](../05-getting-organized/index.md) shows how to put it in a tracker.

## Application Deadlines

An **application deadline** is the date and time by which a college must receive your application for it to be considered in a given round. That definition has two details worth slowing down on.

First, *receive*, not *start*. If the Common App's servers are slow at 11:30 p.m. on deadline night, which happens nearly every year, that is your problem, not the college's. Submitting a few days early is the only reliable protection.

Second, a "deadline" is usually several deadlines. Most colleges separate the application form itself from the materials that support it, and some set additional dates for financial aid or scholarships. A single college can easily have three or four dates that matter to you:

- **Application deadline:** the form and essays must be submitted.
- **Supporting document deadline:** transcripts, recommendations, and test scores, often a week or two later.
- **Financial aid priority deadline:** the date to file aid forms for the best chance at the full range of aid.
- **Scholarship deadline:** sometimes earlier than the application deadline itself.

Deadlines also come with a time and a time zone. Many are 11:59 p.m. in your local time, but some colleges use their own time zone or a different hour entirely. Read the fine print on each school's admissions page, and record the exact time, not just the date.

#### Worked Example: One College, Four Dates

The University of Iowa, which this book uses as its running example, shows how one college's "deadline" splits apart. Iowa's own published dates change slightly from year to year, so treat these as an illustration and check the current ones.

| What's due | Early Action round | Regular round |
|------------|-------------------|---------------|
| Application submitted | November 2 | February 1 |
| Supporting documents (transcript, scores) | November 9 | February 8 |
| Financial aid priority (FAFSA filed) | December 1 | December 1 |
| Merit scholarship consideration | Complete application by early February | Complete application by early February |

Two things stand out. First, the financial aid priority date of December 1 falls *before* the regular application deadline. A student applying in the regular round who waits until January to think about aid has already missed the best aid window. Second, applications completed after February 1 lose merit scholarship consideration. A missed deadline here costs real money, not just a spot.

!!! warning "The deadline you see is rarely the only one"
    For every college on your list, record the application deadline, the supporting document deadline, the financial aid deadline, and any scholarship deadline, along with the exact time and time zone. [Chapter 5](../05-getting-organized/index.md) shows how to keep them in one tracker.

## Four-Year Colleges

A **four-year college** is a school that awards the **bachelor's degree**, the undergraduate degree designed to take about four years of full-time study. This book is about applying to four-year colleges, as opposed to community colleges, which mainly award two-year associate degrees and generally admit anyone with a high school diploma.

"College" and "university" are used loosely in everyday speech, and the names on campus signs aren't always consistent. Dartmouth College is a research university with graduate schools; some places called "university" are small and focus on undergraduates. So don't judge a school by its name. Ask what kind of institution it actually is.

There are more than two thousand four-year colleges in the United States. They are sorted along two main lines that you'll use to describe any school on your list:

- **How it's funded and governed:** public or private.
- **What it focuses on:** research and graduate study, or teaching undergraduates.

The next sections define each of these categories one at a time. After that, a summary table and an interactive sorter let you put them together.

### Public Universities

A **public university** is a college funded in part by a state government and governed by a state board. Because state taxpayers help pay for it, a public university usually charges much lower tuition to students who live in that state. Examples include the University of Iowa, Iowa State University, the University of Michigan, and the University of California campuses.

Public universities tend to be large. Many enroll more than 20,000 undergraduates, which brings a wide range of majors, courses, and activities. Size also shapes how they admit students. With tens of thousands of applications and a duty to serve their state, many public universities lean more heavily on grades and test scores than smaller private colleges do. Some use a formula in which an application that clears a set academic threshold is admitted. You'll see how that works in [Chapter 2](../02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md).

Public universities also have a responsibility to the state's own students, so they treat applicants differently based on where they live. That's where residency comes in, covered later in this chapter.

#### Worked Example: Reading a Public University's Profile

Here is how you'd read the key facts about the University of Iowa:

- **Public:** funded partly by the state of Iowa, so Iowa residents pay a lower tuition rate.
- **Large:** over 20,000 undergraduates, with many majors to choose from.
- **Admission style:** largely formula-driven. Iowa computes a Regent Admission Index from grades and courses, and the minimum required score for its main college is lower for residents (245) than for nonresidents (255).
- **Rounds offered:** Early Action and Regular, both non-binding, with no Early Decision.

That short profile already tells an applicant a lot. Iowa residents get a cost advantage and an admissions advantage. The application is less essay-driven than at a small private college. And applying early carries no commitment.

### Private Colleges

A **private college** is a college that is not run by a state government. It is funded mainly by tuition, donations, and its **endowment**, the pool of invested money a college has built up over time. Most private colleges are nonprofits. Harvard, Stanford, Grinnell College, and Drake University are all private.

Private colleges usually charge the same tuition to every student no matter where they live, and their listed price is often much higher than a public university's. But many have large financial aid budgets of their own, so the price a student actually pays can be far lower than the listed price. Sometimes it's lower than a public university's. [Chapter 14](../14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md) explains how to find the real price.

### Research Universities

A **research university** is a university where professors are expected to produce research as well as teach, and where there are graduate programs (master's and doctoral degrees) alongside the undergraduate college. Research universities can be public, like the University of Iowa, or private, like Stanford. For an undergraduate, that means access to labs, research projects, and a huge course catalog. It can also mean large introductory lectures and some classes taught by graduate students.

### Liberal Arts Colleges

A **liberal arts college** is a college, usually small and private, that focuses on undergraduate education across the arts, humanities, social sciences, and sciences rather than on graduate programs or job-specific training. Classes are typically small and taught by professors, and students are expected to explore broadly before going deep in a major. Grinnell College in Iowa, Williams College in Massachusetts, and Pomona College in California are well-known examples. Most liberal arts colleges enroll somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand students.

### Flagship Universities

A **flagship university** is the best-known and usually oldest public research university in a state, often the one whose name is the state's own. The University of Iowa, the University of Michigan, and the University of Texas at Austin are flagships. A flagship usually gets the most state attention and offers the widest range of programs, and in many states it is the hardest public university to get into. Every flagship is a public research university, but not every public research university is a flagship. Iowa State is a major public research university, but the University of Iowa is the state's flagship.

## In-State Residency and Out-of-State Applicants

Because public universities are funded by state taxpayers, they distinguish between applicants who live in the state and those who don't.

**In-state residency** is the legal status of a student whose home is officially in a public university's state, which qualifies that student for the lower in-state tuition rate. Each state sets its own rules. For most high school seniors, residency follows where your parents or guardians live and pay taxes. Most states require at least a year of living there before you enroll. Moving to a state just to attend college usually doesn't make you a resident right away.

An **out-of-state applicant** is a student applying to a public university in a state where they are not a legal resident. Out-of-state students pay a higher tuition rate, often two to three times the in-state rate. At some public universities they also face a tougher admissions bar, because the school reserves most of its seats for residents. The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, for example, limits out-of-state students to about 18 percent of each first-year class by state policy.

The tuition gap is the reason residency matters so much. According to the College Board's *Trends in College Pricing*, average published tuition and fees for 2024–25 were roughly:

| Student type | Average published tuition and fees (2024–25) |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------|
| In-state student at a public four-year college | about $11,600 |
| Out-of-state student at a public four-year college | about $30,800 |
| Any student at a private nonprofit four-year college | about $43,400 |

These are sticker prices, before any financial aid. A few regional programs, such as the Western Undergraduate Exchange in the western states, let students attend participating public colleges in nearby states at a reduced rate. Check whether your state belongs to one.

## Admission Rate

An **admission rate**, also called an acceptance rate or admit rate, is the percentage of applicants a college admits in a given year. It's the single number people use most often to describe how hard a college is to get into.

You calculate it by dividing the number of students admitted by the number who applied, then multiplying by 100 to get a percentage:

> **Admission rate = (students admitted ÷ students who applied) × 100%**

Colleges report this number every year, and you can find it on their websites and on federal and third-party college search tools, covered in [Chapter 4](../04-researching-your-list/index.md).

#### Worked Example: Calculating and Comparing Admission Rates

Two colleges report last year's numbers:

- **College A** received 40,000 applications and admitted 6,000 students.
- **College B** received 9,000 applications and admitted 6,300 students.

- **College A:** 6,000 ÷ 40,000 × 100% = **15%**
- **College B:** 6,300 ÷ 9,000 × 100% = **70%**

College A admits about 3 of every 20 applicants. College B admits about 7 of every 10. College B admitted *more* students, yet College A is far harder to get into, because so many more people applied.

The admission rate is useful, but it hides as much as it shows. Keep three cautions in mind:

- **It describes the whole applicant pool, not you.** A 15 percent rate doesn't mean you have a 15 percent chance. Your odds depend on how your academic record compares with the students the college actually admits, which [Chapter 2](../02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md) covers.
- **Who applies changes the number.** A college that encourages lots of applications, even from students unlikely to be admitted, will see its rate fall without becoming any more demanding.
- **Rates differ by round and by program.** Early rounds often have higher admit rates, partly because of who tends to apply early. Engineering or nursing programs inside a university can be much harder to get into than the university's overall rate suggests.

## Selective Admissions

**Selective admissions** describes a college that admits only some of its qualified applicants, choosing among them rather than accepting everyone who meets a minimum standard. Selectivity is a spectrum, not a yes-or-no label, and the admission rate is the most common way to place a school on it.

There's no official definition of each level, but these rough bands, which this book uses, match how counselors and college guides talk about schools:

| Band | Approximate admission rate | What it means for applicants |
|------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Most selective | Under 10% | Nearly every applicant is academically strong; essays, activities, and recommendations decide most cases. A reach for everyone. |
| Highly selective | 10–30% | Strong grades in hard courses are expected; the rest of the application matters a great deal. |
| Selective | 30–60% | Solid academic records are usually admitted; the rest of the application can tip borderline cases. |
| Less selective | 60–90% | Most students who meet the academic requirements are admitted. |
| Open or nearly open | Over 90% | Almost everyone who applies with a high school diploma is admitted. |

The most important fact about this table is how few colleges sit at the top. Most four-year colleges admit more than half of the students who apply. The handful of famous schools that admit fewer than one in ten get most of the news coverage. That gives a distorted picture of college admissions as a whole.

#### Worked Example: Same Student, Different Odds

Marcus has a strong record: mostly A's in a demanding course load. He's considering three schools:

- **School X** admits 6% of applicants. Most of the students it turns down have records like his, so even with a strong application his chances are low. This is a reach for him, as it would be for any applicant.
- **School Y** admits 45%. Marcus's grades are above those of most students it admits, so his chances are good, though not guaranteed.
- **School Z** admits 80%. His record is well above its typical admit, so he is very likely to be admitted.

Marcus's application is the same in all three cases. What changes is how selective the school is and where his record falls among its admitted students. [Chapter 3](../03-finding-your-fit/index.md) turns this reasoning into reach, target, and safety categories for your own list.

!!! tip "Selectivity isn't quality"
    A low admission rate tells you a school is hard to get into. It doesn't tell you the school is a good fit for you, that it teaches your intended major well, or that you can afford it. Many excellent colleges admit most of their applicants.

## Inside the Admissions Office

The **admissions office** is the department of a college that recruits students, reads applications, makes admission decisions, and manages the process of turning admitted students into enrolled ones. When you email a question, attend an information session, or submit an application, you're dealing with the admissions office. At most colleges it works closely with, but separately from, the financial aid office, which decides what you'll pay.

An admissions office has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It wants as many good applications as possible, so it markets the college to students: visits to high schools, college fairs, emails, campus tours. Then it has to turn most of those applicants away, or at least choose among them, to build a class that fits the college's goals. Understanding both jobs explains a lot of what you'll experience. The glossy brochures are the first job. The careful reading of your file is the second.

Admissions offices also run on the calendar you saw earlier. Fall is travel season, when staff visit high schools and fairs. Winter is reading season, when the office reads applications almost full time. Spring is decision and recruitment season, when admitted students are invited to campus. Summer is planning for the next cycle.

The people in a typical admissions office fill a few distinct roles:

| Role | What they do | When you'll meet them |
|------|--------------|-----------------------|
| Dean or director of admissions | Sets admissions policy and class goals; signs off on final decisions | Rarely; perhaps at a large information session |
| Admissions officer (counselor) | Recruits in a region, reads applications, presents files in committee | At school visits, college fairs, emails, interviews |
| Application reader | Reads and rates files during reading season; may be seasonal staff | Never directly |
| Operations staff | Processes documents, matches transcripts and letters to files, sends missing-items notices | Through portal messages and emails about missing items |

#### Worked Example: Who Do You Email?

You notice your application portal says your transcript is missing, and your school counselor says they sent it two weeks ago. Who do you contact?

- **Not the dean.** This is a processing problem, not a policy question.
- **Start with the portal message or the general admissions email.** Operations staff match incoming documents to files, and a transcript is often received but not yet matched.
- **Include the details they need:** your full name, birth date, high school, and the date the counselor sent it.
- **If it's still unresolved in a week, email your regional admissions officer.** That's the person assigned to your area, usually listed on the college's website under "Find your counselor."

Knowing who does what saves days, and in deadline season a few days matter.

## Admissions Officers

An **admissions officer**, often called an admissions counselor, is a staff member of an admissions office who recruits students and evaluates applications. At most colleges each officer is assigned a **territory**: a group of states, regions, or high schools. They visit those schools in the fall, get to know the counselors there, and are usually the first to read applications from that territory in the winter.

That territory system matters to you for three reasons:

- **The officer reading your file usually knows your high school.** They've seen its course list, know how hard its grading is, and may have read applications from your classmates in earlier years. They read your record against what your school offers, not against an ideal applicant.
- **They're a real person you can contact.** If you have a question that's specific to you, your regional officer is the right person to ask.
- **They often argue for applicants.** At colleges that use a committee, the first reader summarizes the file and may advocate for the student when the committee meets.

Admissions officers read a lot. During reading season, an officer at a selective college may read dozens of files a day and spend somewhere around ten to fifteen minutes on each. That doesn't mean your application is skimmed carelessly. It means it has to be clear. A reader should be able to tell who you are and what you care about without hunting for it.

## Application Rounds

An **application round** is a set of applications that a college reviews together, with a shared deadline, a shared decision date, and a shared set of rules about what you're committing to. Most colleges offer more than one round. Applying in one round instead of another can change your deadline, when you hear back, and, most importantly, whether you're free to say no.

Before comparing the rounds, you need one more term.

### Binding Commitment

A **binding commitment** is a promise that, if a college admits you, you will enroll there and withdraw every other application. When you apply in a binding round, you, a parent or guardian, and your school counselor typically all sign an agreement saying so. The agreement isn't a legal contract that a court would enforce, but colleges and high school counselors treat it very seriously. Breaking it can lead to the college withdrawing your admission, and colleges sometimes share lists of binding admits with one another. The one widely accepted exception is money: if the financial aid offer genuinely makes the college unaffordable for your family, you can ask to be released. [Chapter 18](../18-handling-decisions/index.md) covers that process.

With that term in hand, here are the five rounds you'll encounter.

### Early Decision

**Early Decision (ED)** is an early application round that is *binding*: if you're admitted, you must attend. You may apply to only one college Early Decision. Deadlines are usually November 1 or November 15, and decisions arrive by mid-December. Some colleges also offer **Early Decision II**, with the same binding rules but a January deadline, for students who decide on their first choice later.

Early Decision can raise your chances at many colleges, because it tells the college you'll definitely enroll. The cost is that you lose the ability to compare financial aid offers. Use ED only for a school that is clearly your first choice *and* whose cost your family has checked in advance.

### Early Action

**Early Action (EA)** is an early application round that is *not* binding: you apply early and hear back early, but you're free to decline the offer and wait until May 1 to decide. You can usually apply Early Action to as many colleges as you like. Deadlines are typically in November, and decisions come anywhere from December to February. The University of Iowa's November 2 deadline is an Early Action deadline. Applying early there gets you an early answer and no obligation.

### Restrictive Early Action

**Restrictive Early Action (REA)**, also called Single-Choice Early Action, is a non-binding early round that *limits where else you can apply early*. If you're admitted, you're still free to say no. But in exchange for applying, you agree not to apply early to most other private colleges. Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Stanford are among the schools that use a version of it. The exact restrictions differ by college; some allow early applications to public universities, for example. Read each school's REA policy word by word before you apply.

### Regular Decision

**Regular Decision (RD)** is the standard, non-binding application round. Deadlines usually fall between January 1 and February 1, and decisions arrive between mid-March and early April. If admitted, you have until May 1 to decide. Regular Decision gives you the most time to strengthen your application, including your fall semester grades, and it lets you compare every admission and financial aid offer side by side before committing.

Most students submit most of their applications in the Regular Decision round, and that's a perfectly sound plan. Applying early is an option, not an obligation, and a rushed early application can be weaker than a careful regular one.

#### Worked Example: Is Regular Decision the Right Call?

Sam's first-choice school offers Early Decision with a November 1 deadline. Sam's grades have climbed steadily, with junior year their strongest, and they expect a strong fall semester in two AP classes. Their family needs to compare aid offers because cost will decide the final choice.

- **Early Decision** would lock Sam in before they can compare costs. That's a poor fit for a family that needs to compare aid.
- **Early Action**, if the school offered it, would be fine. But this school doesn't.
- **Regular Decision** lets the school see Sam's fall grades, which help their case, and keeps every financial option open until May 1.

For Sam, Regular Decision is the stronger choice even though it gives up the early-applicant advantage. The early round isn't automatically the smart round. It depends on how certain you are and how much the cost matters.

### Rolling Admission

**Rolling admission** is a system in which a college reviews applications as they arrive and sends decisions continuously, usually within a few weeks, until the class is full. There's often no single deadline, though many rolling schools set a **priority date** for scholarships or housing. Many large public universities use rolling admission.

The catch is that the class can fill up. Applying in September or October to a rolling school often brings an answer before Thanksgiving, which can take a great deal of pressure off the rest of senior year. Applying in March may mean fewer spots, less scholarship money, and less choice of housing. Rolling admission is non-binding.

### Comparing the Rounds

You've now seen all five rounds. This table puts them side by side.

| Round | Binding? | Apply to how many? | Typical deadline | Typical decision | Can compare aid offers? |
|-------|----------|--------------------|------------------|------------------|-------------------------|
| Early Decision | Yes | One | Nov 1 or 15 (ED II: January) | Mid-December (ED II: February) | No |
| Early Action | No | Any number | November | December to February | Yes |
| Restrictive Early Action | No | One early application (with exceptions) | November 1 | Mid-December | Yes |
| Regular Decision | No | Any number | January 1 to February 1 | Mid-March to early April | Yes |
| Rolling | No | Any number | Open until full; priority dates vary | Within a few weeks | Yes |

## Key Takeaways

- The **college admissions process** has six stages: build your list, test, apply, seek aid, decide, and enroll. It runs about fifteen months, from the summer before senior year to the fall after graduation.
- The **admissions timeline** is lopsided. Most of the work happens from August to January, decisions come in March and April, and **May 1** is the national commitment deadline.
- An **application deadline** is usually several deadlines. Track the application, supporting documents, financial aid, and scholarship dates for each college, with exact times.
- A **four-year college** awards the bachelor's degree. Colleges sort into **public** or **private** by funding, and into **research universities** or **liberal arts colleges** by focus. A **flagship** is a state's leading public research university.
- Public universities charge **in-state residents** much less than **out-of-state applicants**, and some also admit residents more readily.
- The **admission rate** is admits divided by applicants. It measures how **selective** a school is, not your personal chances and not the school's quality.
- The **admissions office** recruits and reads. Your regional **admissions officer** usually knows your high school and reads your file in that context.
- **Early Decision** is a **binding commitment**. **Early Action**, **Restrictive Early Action**, **Regular Decision**, and **rolling admission** are not, though REA limits where else you can apply early.

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "A college received 25,000 applications and admitted 5,000 students. What is its admission rate, and which selectivity band does it fall in?"
    5,000 ÷ 25,000 × 100% = **20%**. That places it in the **highly selective** band (10–30%).

??? question "Your top choice offers Early Decision and Regular Decision. Your family needs to compare financial aid offers before you commit. Which round fits, and why?"
    **Regular Decision.** Early Decision is binding, so you'd have to enroll if admitted, before you could compare other aid offers. Regular Decision keeps you free to compare every offer until May 1.

??? question "Is every public research university a flagship? Is every flagship a public research university?"
    No to the first: Iowa State is a public research university but not Iowa's flagship. Yes to the second: a flagship is by definition a state's leading public research university.

??? question "You applied Restrictive Early Action to a private university and were admitted in December. Are you required to attend?"
    **No.** Restrictive Early Action is non-binding. It limits where else you can apply *early*, but once admitted you can still decline and decide by May 1.

??? question "Why might an out-of-state applicant find a public flagship harder to get into than an in-state applicant with the same record?"
    Public universities are funded partly by their state and reserve most seats for residents. Some, like UNC–Chapel Hill, cap the share of out-of-state students, so out-of-state applicants compete for fewer spots.
