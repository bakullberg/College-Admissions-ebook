## Systems Before Essays

Applications rarely fall apart because of one hard step. They fall apart because of dropped threads: a deadline you knew about but forgot to write down, a teacher who never got your resume, a password you can't remember in December, an email from a college that landed in a folder you never check. Each mistake is small. With ten colleges and five months, there are hundreds of chances to make one.

This chapter sets up the systems that prevent that. You'll begin with what getting organized means and why it pays off. Then you'll build the core tools: a deadline tracker you share with your family, a checklist for each college, and a filing system for your documents and logins. Next come the accounts that every application runs through, starting with a dedicated email address and the Common App. You'll also learn who can help you, from your school counselor to your family, and how senior year's calendar fits together. The chapter ends with five first steps you can take this week.

The good news is that none of this is hard. Most of it takes an afternoon or two. That small investment pays off every week for the rest of the year.

## Getting Organized

**Getting organized** means setting up, before your applications begin, the tools and habits that keep every task, date, document, and login in a known place. It's not busywork that comes before the real work. It's what lets the real work, like writing strong essays and comparing aid offers, happen without a last-minute scramble.

Organization rests on three principles:

1. **One source of truth.** Every deadline lives in one tracker, every document in one folder, every password in one secure place. When information is scattered across texts, sticky notes, and memory, something always gets lost.
2. **Visible to the people helping you.** A parent, guardian, or counselor can only catch a missed date if they can see the plan. Sharing your tracker turns your helpers into a safety net.
3. **Early buffers.** Set your own deadlines a few days before the real ones. Portals crash on deadline night, recommenders run late, and files refuse to upload. A buffer turns those problems into minor annoyances instead of emergencies.

The payoff is easy to underestimate. Think about how many separate items the process involves. Ten colleges, each with an application, a supplement or two, a supporting document deadline, and an aid deadline, already add up to more than forty dates. Add in testing, recommendations, scholarship applications, and the FAFSA, and the total climbs past a hundred. No one can track that from memory.

#### Worked Example: The Cost of a Dropped Thread

Two students apply to the same set of colleges.

**Student 1** keeps deadlines in their head and in a few phone notes. In late October, they realize their top choice's Early Action deadline is November 1, not November 15 as they'd remembered. They rush their supplemental essay and submit it at 11:40 p.m. with two typos. They also discover that one teacher never received the resume they meant to send, so that letter arrives a week late.

**Student 2** spent one Saturday in August building a tracker and shared it with a parent. Every deadline has a personal due date five days earlier, and phone reminders go off a week before each one. Their supplemental essays are done by October 25. When a teacher's letter hasn't appeared in the portal by November 3, the tracker flags it, and a polite email fixes it the same day.

Both students are equally capable. The difference in their applications comes entirely from one Saturday of setup.

## The Deadline Tracker

A **deadline tracker** is a single document, usually a spreadsheet, that lists every college on your list along with every date and requirement attached to it. It's the center of your organizational system. Chapter 1 explained why one college can have several deadlines. The tracker is where you record all of them.

For each college, a good tracker includes:

- **Application round and deadline,** with the exact time and time zone.
- **Supporting document deadline** for transcripts, recommendations, and test scores.
- **Financial aid and scholarship deadlines,** including any priority deadlines.
- **Your personal due date,** a few days before the real deadline.
- **Application platform,** whether the Common App or the college's own application.
- **Supplemental essays,** with their prompts and word limits.
- **Recommendations required,** and from whom.
- **Testing policy,** and whether you plan to submit scores.
- **Status:** not started, in progress, submitted, or complete.

| Hillcrest College | RD | Jan 15, 11:59 p.m. ET | Jan 9 | Feb 1 | Feb 15 (CSS Profile) | Common App | "Why Hillcrest" (300 words) | 2 teachers + counselor | Not started |

Notice that Priya's flagship row has an aid priority date (December 1) that comes *after* her application but *before* she'd hear back. Without the tracker, she might assume aid comes after admission. Also notice that Hillcrest requires the CSS Profile, an extra aid form [Chapter 14](../14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md) explains, so it gets its own deadline.

### The Shared Tracker

A **shared tracker** is your deadline tracker made visible to the people helping you, usually a parent or guardian and sometimes your counselor, through a shared online spreadsheet or document. Sharing it doesn't hand over control. You still own the process. What it does is give someone else a chance to notice a problem you're too close to see, like a missing aid deadline, two essays due the same week, or a status that hasn't changed in a month. Decide together how you'll use it. For example, your parent might check it every Sunday and add questions as comments rather than sending reminders all week. One caution: never put passwords in a shared tracker. They belong in your private login record, covered later in this chapter.

### Supporting Document Deadlines

A **supporting document deadline** is the date by which a college must receive the materials that accompany your application: your transcript, recommendation letters, test scores, and any school report. Chapter 1 introduced it. Here's why it gets its own column in your tracker. Most of these documents are sent by *other people*, such as your counselor, your teachers, and testing agencies, so your job is to request them early and then confirm they arrived. Record each document's status separately, and check your applicant portal a week or so after the deadline to make sure everything was matched to your file.

### Priority Deadlines

A **priority deadline** is an earlier date that gives you the best chance at something limited, such as financial aid, merit scholarships, honors programs, or housing, even though the college still accepts applications or forms after it. "Priority" is easy to misread as "optional." In practice, students who miss a priority deadline are still considered, but often for whatever money or spots are left. Iowa's December 1 FAFSA priority date, for example, comes before its regular application deadline. Treat every priority deadline as a real deadline.

### Calendar Reminders

**Calendar reminders** are alerts you set on your phone or computer calendar for each date in your tracker, so deadlines come to you instead of depending on you to check. A tracker only helps when you look at it. Reminders make sure you do. A useful pattern is to set two reminders for each deadline: one a week before your personal due date, to start finishing, and one the day before, to submit. Add reminders for checking your portals and email, too.

## The Application Checklist

An **application checklist** is a list of every component one specific college requires, which you check off as you complete each one. Your tracker gives the overview across all your colleges. The checklist gives the detail for one. You might keep one checklist per college as a tab in your spreadsheet, a note, or a printed page in that college's folder.

A typical checklist includes the application form, the personal essay, each supplemental essay, the activities list, each recommendation, the transcript request, test scores if submitting, the application fee or fee waiver, and any extras such as a portfolio, interview, or specific form for a program. It ends with two items students often forget: **"Submitted (confirmation saved)"** and **"Portal checked: all documents received."**

## College-Specific Requirements

**College-specific requirements** are the parts of an application that differ from one college to another: extra essays, extra recommendations, testing rules, portfolios, interviews, program-specific forms, and the specific deadlines each college sets. Even when ten colleges share one application platform, they rarely ask for exactly the same things. Missing one college's unique requirement is one of the most common ways an otherwise complete application ends up incomplete.

Requirements vary in several ways:

- **Essays.** Some colleges ask for no supplemental essays. Others ask for two, three, or more, with different word limits.
- **Recommendations.** Some require none, some require one teacher, and some require two teachers plus a counselor. A few accept an optional additional letter.
- **Testing.** Policies range from test-required to test-optional to test-blind, and can differ by program or for merit scholarships.
- **Program requirements.** Art, music, theater, and architecture programs often require portfolios or auditions. Nursing and engineering may ask for extra essays or have different deadlines.
- **Course requirements.** Some colleges, or some majors, require specific high school courses, such as four years of math or a year of chemistry.
- **Interviews.** Some colleges offer them, some recommend them, and a few require them for certain programs.

Where do you find them? Always on the college's **own admissions website**, usually on a page called "first-year requirements" or "how to apply." The Common App also lists each member college's requirements, but treat the college's own site as the final word, since it's updated most directly. Policies change from year to year, so check them in the fall of the year you apply, not from a friend's experience last year.

## The Filing System

A **filing system** is an organized set of folders, digital, paper, or both, where every document in your application process has a known place. You'll create and receive dozens of documents this year: transcripts, score reports, essay drafts, recommendation requests, financial forms, confirmation emails, and award letters. Without a system, finding the right one in a hurry becomes a scavenger hunt.

A simple structure works best. Create one main folder with these subfolders:

- **Transcripts:** unofficial copies for your own reference, and records of official transcript requests.
- **Test scores:** score reports from the College Board or ACT.
- **Essays:** your personal essay drafts, plus a subfolder for each college's supplements.
- **Activities:** your activities resume and notes for the activities list.
- **Recommendations:** the resume and notes you gave each recommender, and confirmations that letters were submitted.
- **Financial aid:** tax documents and other paperwork for the FAFSA and CSS Profile, kept securely.
- **Colleges:** one subfolder per college for its checklist, confirmation emails, portal screenshots, and decision letters.

Name files clearly so you can find them later, for example "Hillcrest_WhyHillcrest_v3.docx" rather than "essay final FINAL.docx."

#### Worked Example: Finding a Document in Thirty Seconds

In early December, Marcus gets an email from Lakeshore University's portal: "We have not received your mid-year report or your counselor recommendation." He's sure his counselor sent them. In his "Colleges › Lakeshore" folder, he finds the screenshot he took on November 10 showing the counselor recommendation marked "received." He replies to the admissions office with the screenshot attached. An hour later, they confirm a matching error and fix it. (The mid-year report isn't due until February. The automated email was simply early.)

Without the filing system, Marcus would have spent his evening worrying and emailing his counselor. With it, he had proof in thirty seconds.

### The Digital Document Folder

A **digital document folder** is the online version of your filing system, kept in a cloud storage service such as Google Drive, OneDrive, or iCloud so you can reach it from any device. Digital storage has three advantages over paper: you can search it, you can't lose it in a move, and you can upload directly from it to application portals. Back it up, keep it under your own account rather than a school account you'll lose after graduation, and share only specific subfolders with the people who need them, never the financial aid folder by default.

### Transcript Copies

**Transcript copies** are unofficial copies of your high school transcript that you keep for your own use. Colleges need *official* transcripts, which your school sends directly. But you'll need an unofficial copy too, because many applications ask you to enter your own courses and grades, and you'll want to check them against the record. Ask your counselor or school office for an unofficial copy at the end of junior year, and again after fall grades come out in senior year. Save each one in your Transcripts folder, and check it for errors. Mistakes on transcripts happen, and it's much easier to fix one in September than in January.

### The Login Credentials Record

A **login credentials record** is a secure, private record of every account you create during the process, including the site, your username, and your password. By spring you may have twenty or more accounts: the Common App, several college portals, the College Board, ACT, the FAFSA, the CSS Profile, and scholarship sites. You'll need to get back into many of them months after creating them. The safest option is a **password manager**, an app that stores passwords securely behind one master password. If you don't use one, keep a single document protected with a strong password, not a note on your phone and never your shared tracker. Use a different password for each account.

## The Dedicated Email Address

A **dedicated email address** is a personal email account you create just for the college process and use for every application, portal, testing account, and college you contact. It sounds like a small thing, but it matters more than nearly anything else you'll set up, because almost every important message in the process arrives by email: login links, missing-document notices, interview invitations, financial aid requests, and decisions.

A dedicated address solves three problems:

- **Nothing gets buried.** College messages don't compete with social media notifications, store promotions, and group chats in your main inbox. When something arrives, you'll see it.
- **You keep access.** Many students apply using their high school email, then lose access to it after graduation, often just when colleges send housing and orientation information. A personal account you control stays with you.
- **You look like an adult.** Admissions officers see your email address. *firstname.lastname@* reads better than a nickname you picked in middle school.

Set it up with a few habits in mind. Use a free provider and a simple, professional address that includes your name. Use the **same address everywhere**, since colleges match records partly by email. Check it at least every other day during application season, and daily from March through May. Set up filters or labels so messages from each college, testing agencies, and financial aid sort themselves. Check your spam folder weekly, since important college emails sometimes land there. Finally, consider sharing access or forwarding with a parent if your family agrees. Some families find that helpful. Others prefer that the student own it fully.

#### Worked Example: The Missed Email

Ana's older cousin, Luis, applied to a university using his high school email account. In February, the university's financial aid office emailed to say his aid file was missing a tax form, with a two-week deadline. His school's email filter flagged the message as outside mail and sent it to a quarantine folder he didn't know existed. He found it a day after the deadline. The aid office still processed his file, but his grant offer came weeks later than his friends' offers, and some of the university's limited grant money had already been awarded.

Ana learned from Luis's experience. She created a dedicated address before opening any accounts, set up a "Colleges" label, and checks it every day. When a college asks her for something, she'll see it within hours.

## Applications and Accounts

Every college application is completed through one of two kinds of system: a college's own application, or a shared platform used by many colleges. Most students use both, because their lists include colleges of both kinds.

### The Institutional Application

An **institutional application** is a college's own application form, completed on the college's website rather than through a shared platform. Many public universities use one, and some private colleges do too. Some colleges accept *only* their institutional application. The University of California system, MIT, and Georgetown, for example, don't accept the Common App. Others, like the University of Iowa, accept both, so you choose.

The term covers more than the form itself. In a sense, every application is institutional at heart. Even when you apply through a shared platform, each college sets its own deadlines, its own supplements, and its own requirements, and it makes its own decision. Shared platforms only bundle the parts many colleges have in common.

When a college offers both options, choosing between them is a real decision:

| Consideration | Institutional application | Shared platform (Common App) |
|---------------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Effort if it's your only college of this kind | Often shorter and simpler | More sections to complete |
| Effort if you're already using the platform | A separate set of forms to fill out | Little extra work beyond any supplements |
| Personal essay | Sometimes not required | Your Common App essay goes to every college you send it to |
| Fees and waivers | Set by the college | Set by the college; fee waivers handled through the platform |

Iowa shows the trade-off. Its own application requires no personal essay for general admission, while the Common App sends along the essay you wrote for other colleges. If Iowa is one of several colleges on your list and you already have an essay, the Common App is little extra work. If it's your only application, the institutional application is simpler.

#### Worked Example: Two Platforms, One List

Marcus's final list of ten includes eight colleges that accept the Common App, the in-state public university, which accepts both, and the nearby regional university, which uses only its own application. He decides:

- **Eight colleges → Common App.** One set of core sections, one personal essay.
- **In-state public university → Common App too.** He's already completing it, so adding the college takes minutes.
- **Regional university → its own application.** It's short. He blocks out one evening for it in his tracker.

He records the platform for each college in his tracker. That way he won't open the Common App in December, wonder why the regional university isn't there, and panic.

### The Applicant Portal

An **applicant portal** is a personal online account a college creates for you after you apply, where you check your application status, see which documents have arrived, upload missing items, and eventually view your decision and financial aid. After you submit, each college typically emails you instructions to set up your portal login within a few days to a couple of weeks. That's one more reason the dedicated email matters.

Portals are where problems surface. A transcript that arrived but wasn't matched to your file, or a recommendation listed as missing, appears there long before anyone emails you about it, if anyone does. Make checking portals part of your routine. Look about a week after each submission and again after each supporting document deadline. Record every new portal login in your credentials record the day you create it.

### The Common App

The **Common App** (short for the Common Application) is a shared online application platform accepted by more than 1,000 colleges, including most private colleges and many public universities. You complete one set of core sections, covering your profile, family, education, testing, activities, and a personal essay, and send it to every member college on your list. Each college may then add its own questions and supplemental essays.

The main advantage is efficiency. Without it, you'd type your address, your parents' information, your activities, and your courses separately for every college. With it, you do that once. The Common App also lets your counselor and teachers upload their documents once for all your colleges, and it handles application fee waivers in one place.

A few facts about how it works:

- **It runs on a yearly cycle.** The new application year opens on **August 1**, when that year's essay prompts and college-specific questions go live.
- **You can add up to 20 colleges.** That's far more than most students need.
- **Each college sets its own rules.** Deadlines, supplements, recommendation requirements, and testing policies all come from the college, not the Common App.
- **You'll see a separate list of requirements for each college.** It shows that college's deadlines, supplements, and documents, the same information your requirements matrix collects.

The Common App isn't the only shared platform. Chapter 12 also covers the Coalition App, which some colleges accept, and gives a section-by-section walkthrough of the Common App itself. For now, the important step is the account.

### The Common App Account

Your **Common App account** is the personal login you create on the Common App website to complete and submit your applications. You can create it any time, including as a junior. Most of what you enter **rolls over** when the new application year begins on August 1, so starting early is safe. Creating it early has real benefits. The process starts to feel concrete, you can explore each college's requirements, and you can finish the long factual sections over the summer, leaving fall for essays.

When you create the account, get four details right from the start:

1. **Use your dedicated email address.** Every Common App and college message will go there.
2. **Use your legal name,** exactly as it appears on your school records, test registrations, and eventually financial aid forms. Mismatched names are a common cause of documents not being matched to your file. You can list a preferred name separately.
3. **Choose "first-year student"** as your applicant type, unless you've already attended college after high school graduation.
4. **Record the login** in your credentials record right away.

After that, add the colleges on your list and invite your counselor through the Education section. The Common App connects you with your counselor, and from there, to your teachers. Chapter 12 explains how recommenders are invited.

#### Worked Example: A Summer Head Start

Priya creates her Common App account in June after junior year. Over July, she fills in the Profile, Family, and Education sections, using her unofficial transcript to enter her courses. She drafts her activities list from her activities resume. On August 1, the new year opens and her information carries over. She adds her eight Common App colleges, reads each one's requirements page, and copies every supplemental essay prompt into her tracker.

By mid-August, the only parts of her application left are essays and recommendations. While some of her classmates spend September typing addresses and course names, she spends it writing.

## The People Who Help

You own your applications, but you shouldn't do them alone. Several people can help, and knowing what each one does keeps you from asking the wrong person or asking too late.

### The School Counselor

Your **school counselor** is the staff member at your high school who guides students through academic planning and college applications, and who represents your school to colleges. In the admissions process, the counselor does several things only they can do. They send your official transcript and school profile. They write the counselor recommendation and complete the school report, including the course-rigor rating from Chapter 2. They send your mid-year and final grades. They're also often the person who knows how students from your school have done at specific colleges in past years.

Counselors are often stretched thin. At many public high schools, one counselor works with several hundred students, and senior fall is their busiest time. That means two things for you. First, **help them help you.** Give them your list, your deadlines, and any information they'll need well ahead of time. Second, **don't wait for them to come to you.** Request a meeting yourself, early.

#### Worked Example: Helping a Busy Counselor

Ana's high school has one counselor for about 400 students. Ana is first-generation, and her family doesn't know the process, so her counselor matters a lot to her. In May of junior year, she sends a short email asking for a meeting before summer. She attaches her draft college list and a one-page summary of her activities. At the meeting, the counselor tells her about a fee waiver she qualifies for, a fly-in program at one of her colleges, and a local community organization that helps first-generation students with essays.

In September, Ana shares her tracker with her counselor and sends a short reminder two weeks before her first deadline. Her counselor later mentions that Ana was one of the easiest students to write for, because she'd made it simple to know her.

### The Counselor Meeting

A **counselor meeting** is a scheduled conversation with your school counselor to plan your applications. Ideally, you have one in spring of junior year and another early in senior fall. Come prepared. Bring your draft college list, your unofficial transcript, a list of questions, and your activities resume. Good questions include: *Is my list balanced? How have students from our school done at these colleges? What do you need from me, and by when? How does our school handle transcript and recommendation requests?* Take notes, and add any new dates to your tracker that day.

### Independent Counselors

An **independent counselor**, also called an independent educational consultant, is a private admissions advisor a family hires and pays, separate from the school. Some are former admissions officers or school counselors. They can offer more individual time than a busy school counselor, but they typically charge anywhere from a few hundred dollars for a single consultation to several thousand for full support. **You don't need one to apply successfully.** This book, your school counselor, and free resources cover the process. Many community organizations and nonprofit programs offer free counseling, especially for first-generation and low-income students. If your family does hire one, check credentials and references, and be wary of anyone who promises admission to specific colleges. An independent counselor never replaces your school counselor, who still sends your official documents.

### The Parent and Family Role

The **parent and family role** is the part that parents, guardians, and other family members play in the process: supporting, informing, and helping with specific tasks, while the student leads. Families matter most in three areas. The first is **money**. Parents usually complete parts of the financial aid forms and are central to the conversation about what the family can afford. The second is **logistics**, such as visits, deadlines, and paperwork. The third is **encouragement** through a long, uncertain year.

The hardest part is balance. Too little family involvement can leave a student alone with a complicated process, especially if no one has done it before. Too much can take the process away from the student. Essays written by parents tend to sound like parents, and admissions readers can tell. A useful division looks like this:

| Task | Student leads | Family supports |
|------|---------------|-----------------|
| Building the college list | Researches, decides, explains choices | Shares cost limits, asks questions, joins visits |
| Applications and essays | Writes everything, meets deadlines | Reads drafts if asked, offers general feedback |
| Deadlines | Owns the tracker | Checks the shared tracker, flags problems |
| Financial aid | Learns the process, completes student sections | Provides financial information, completes parent sections |
| Final decision | Makes the choice | Helps compare costs and offers |

Families who've never done this, or who are new to the U.S. education system, have just as important a role. Their job isn't to know the process. It's to support the student in learning it, to show up for the money conversation, and to help with the forms only they can complete. Many colleges and community organizations offer financial aid workshops for families, often in several languages.

#### Worked Example: Dividing the Work

Ana and her parents sit down in August to divide the work. Ana's mother doesn't know the application process, but she manages the family's paperwork. Her father works evenings and can't attend events, but wants to help.

They agree on three things. Ana owns her tracker and applications and shares the tracker with her mother. Her mother gathers the tax documents for the FAFSA by October and attends a free financial aid workshop at Ana's school. Her father reads Ana's essays on Sunday mornings when she asks him to, and tells her whether they sound like her. Each person's role fits what they can actually do, and none of them is pretending to be an expert.

## The Senior Year Calendar

### The Summer Before Senior Year

The **summer before senior year** is the stretch between the end of junior year and the start of senior year, the last long block of free time before deadlines begin. Use it for the tasks that take time but not urgency. Finalize your college list, visit nearby campuses, create your Common App account and fill in its factual sections, build your activities resume, draft your personal essay, and decide whether to take or retake a standardized test in the fall. Students who use the summer well start senior year with essays in progress. Students who don't start it with everything at once. You don't need to work every day. A few hours a week adds up.

### The Senior Year Calendar

The **senior year calendar** is the month-by-month sequence of admissions tasks from the start of senior year through May 1, built from the shared admissions timeline in Chapter 1 and your own application rounds. Its overall shape is the same for nearly everyone:

- **August–September:** Finish your list, open the Common App, ask for recommendations, write essays.
- **October:** Test if you're testing, file the FAFSA once it opens, finish early applications.
- **November:** Submit early applications, then turn to regular-round essays.
- **December:** Early decisions arrive. Finish regular applications.
- **January–February:** Submit the remaining applications, send mid-year grades, apply for scholarships.
- **March–April:** Decisions and aid offers arrive. Compare and decide.
- **May 1:** Commit to one college.

Your personal calendar shifts with your rounds. An Early Decision applicant's fall is busier, and a Regular Decision applicant's winter is. Chapter 1's Senior Year Admissions Timeline shows both, and the Month-by-Month Planner in [Chapter 20](../20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md) turns this outline into a detailed checklist.

## Five First Steps

The **five first steps** are five concrete tasks that start the process and set up its foundations. You can do all of them in a week, even before your list is final:

1. **Set up a shared tracker.** Create the spreadsheet, add the colleges you're considering, and share it with a parent or guardian.
2. **Create your Common App account,** using your dedicated email address and legal name. Create the email first if you haven't already.
3. **Ask two teachers for recommendations.** Teachers get many requests in the fall, and asking early gets you a better letter, not just a faster one. [Chapter 11](../11-letters-of-recommendation/index.md) covers whom to ask and how.
4. **Draft an activities resume.** List your jobs, clubs, sports, volunteering, and family responsibilities. You'll use it for the Common App, for your recommenders, and for essay ideas. [Chapter 8](../08-activities-and-resume/index.md) covers it in detail.
5. **Start a running list of essay material.** Keep a note on your phone for moments, stories, and details as they occur to you. The best essay material often shows up when you're not trying to write.

## Key Takeaways

- **Getting organized** rests on one source of truth, visibility to your helpers, and early buffers. One afternoon of setup prevents most dropped threads.
- A **deadline tracker** lists every college's dates and requirements. A **shared tracker** lets a parent or counselor catch problems. **Calendar reminders** bring deadlines to you. Track **supporting document deadlines** and **priority deadlines** as real deadlines.
- Keep an **application checklist** for each college, and capture **college-specific requirements** from the college's own website.
- A **filing system** in a **digital document folder** gives every document a home. Keep **transcript copies** and a private **login credentials record**, never in the shared tracker.
- A **dedicated email address** that you control and check often is the single most important account you'll create.
- Colleges use an **institutional application**, the **Common App**, or both. After you apply, each college's **applicant portal** shows what's missing.
- Create your **Common App account** early, with your dedicated email and legal name. Your information rolls over on August 1.
- Your **school counselor** sends key documents and knows your school's history. Prepare for each **counselor meeting**. An **independent counselor** is optional. Your **family's role** is to support while you lead.
- Use the **summer before senior year** for slow tasks, and follow the **senior year calendar**. Start with the **five first steps** this week.

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "Why should you avoid using your high school email address for college applications?"
    You'll likely **lose access after graduation**, often just as colleges send housing and orientation information. School filters may also block or quarantine college emails. A dedicated personal address avoids both problems.

??? question "A college lists a 'priority deadline' of December 1 for merit scholarships and a regular deadline of January 15. You apply January 10. What's the risk?"
    You'll still be considered for admission, but you may be considered for merit scholarships only from whatever money is left, or not at all. Priority deadlines should be treated as real deadlines.

??? question "Your Common App shows eight colleges, but your final list has ten. What's the most likely explanation, and where should you check?"
    The other two probably use their own **institutional applications**. Check your tracker's platform column and each college's admissions website.

??? question "Where should you store your applicant portal passwords, and where should you never store them?"
    In a **password manager** or a single secure, private document, your **login credentials record**. Never in your **shared tracker**, which other people can see.

??? question "Name two things only your school counselor can do in the application process."
    Any two of: send your **official transcript** and **school profile**; complete the **school report** and counselor recommendation, including the course-rigor rating; send **mid-year and final grades**.
