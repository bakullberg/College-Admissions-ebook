## Reading Past the Formatting

When admission letters arrive, financial aid offers come with them or soon after. These **award letters** are the moment the estimates from earlier chapters turn into real numbers. They're also frustratingly inconsistent. Colleges aren't required to use a standard format, so two colleges can describe the same real cost in ways that look completely different. Some list loans as "aid." Some leave out the cost of attendance. Some show a "remaining balance" that hides what you'll actually owe.

This chapter is about reading past the formatting to the numbers that matter. You'll start with the forms of aid you'll see on a letter, especially work-study and the different kinds of loans, because telling free money from borrowed money is the core skill. Then you'll learn to read an award letter line by line, calculate your out-of-pocket cost, project the cost over four years, estimate your debt at graduation, and compare offers side by side. The chapter ends with how to ask a college to reconsider its offer.

Loan rules changed significantly for loans made on or after July 1, 2026. The figures in this chapter are for the 2026–27 aid year. Confirm current rates and limits at [studentaid.gov](https://studentaid.gov).

## Self-Help Aid on the Letter

Chapter 14 sorted aid into **gift aid** (grants and scholarships, which you don't repay) and **self-help aid** (work-study and loans, which you earn or repay). Award letters mix all of these together. Before you can read one, you need to know exactly what each kind of self-help aid means.

### Work-Study

**Work-study** is a need-based financial aid program, usually Federal Work-Study, that gives you the chance to earn money through a part-time job, typically on campus. It appears on your award letter as a dollar amount, such as "$2,500," but that number is a **ceiling, not a payment.** You earn it only by finding a work-study job and working the hours, and you're paid by paycheck as you work, usually every two weeks. It isn't subtracted from your bill.

That has three consequences for reading a letter. First, **work-study doesn't lower your cost.** It's income you earn during the year, which can help with books, transportation, and personal expenses. Second, **it isn't guaranteed.** You still have to get hired, and popular jobs fill quickly. Third, **you can earn it only by working,** typically 8 to 12 hours a week. That's time you need to budget alongside classes.

Work-study has real advantages too. Campus jobs are usually flexible around class schedules, and many, such as library desks, labs, and tutoring centers, allow some studying during quiet hours. Work-study earnings also don't count against you on next year's FAFSA the way other earnings can.

#### Worked Example: What $2,500 in Work-Study Means

Emily's Hillcrest offer includes $2,500 in Federal Work-Study. She works out what it would take:

- Hillcrest's work-study jobs pay about $13 an hour.
- $2,500 ÷ $13 ≈ **192 hours** over the year.
- Over about 30 weeks of classes, that's roughly **6 to 7 hours a week.**

That's manageable alongside a full course load. But the $2,500 won't arrive as a discount on her August bill. It will arrive in paychecks between September and April. Emily plans to use it for books and personal expenses, not tuition, and she budgets her August payment without it.

## Federal Loans

### Federal Direct Loans

**Federal Direct Loans** are student loans from the U.S. Department of Education, available to almost every student who files the FAFSA. They're the most common loans on an award letter and, for most students, the safest way to borrow. They have fixed interest rates, borrower protections, and repayment options that private loans usually lack. There are three main kinds, covered in the next sections: subsidized loans, unsubsidized loans, and Parent PLUS loans.

Direct Loans for students have **annual limits** that rise as you progress through college. For dependent undergraduates, they are:

| Year | Total annual limit | Of which subsidized, at most |
|------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| First year | $5,500 | $3,500 |
| Second year | $6,500 | $4,500 |
| Third year and beyond | $7,500 | $5,500 |

The total over a dependent student's undergraduate years is capped at $31,000. A student who borrows the maximum each year for four years borrows $27,000. These limits are deliberately modest, which is a protection. They keep most undergraduates' federal debt within a range that a typical starting salary can repay.

For loans first disbursed between July 1, 2026 and June 30, 2027, the **interest rate** on undergraduate Direct Loans is **6.52%**, fixed for the life of the loan. There's also an **origination fee** of about 1% that's subtracted from each disbursement, so a $5,500 loan delivers a little less than $5,500 to your bill. Interest rates are reset each July for new loans. Repayment usually begins six months after you graduate or drop below half-time enrollment. For loans made on or after July 1, 2026, borrowers can choose between a standard fixed-payment plan and an income-based plan called the Repayment Assistance Plan.

#### Worked Example: What Borrowing the Maximum Means

Marcus considers borrowing the maximum federal Direct Loan every year: $5,500 + $6,500 + $7,500 + $7,500 = **$27,000.** At 6.52% interest over a standard ten-year repayment, his monthly payment after graduation would be about:

\[
\text{Payment} = \frac{P \cdot r}{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}} = \frac{27{,}000 \times 0.00543}{1 - (1.00543)^{-120}} \approx \$307 \text{ per month}
\]

Here \(P\) is the amount borrowed, \(r\) is the monthly interest rate (6.52% ÷ 12), and \(n\) is the number of monthly payments (120 over ten years). Over ten years, he'd repay about $36,800, roughly $9,800 of it interest. A software developer's starting salary could manage $307 a month, so the loan is a reasonable tool for Marcus. But it's not free, and seeing the monthly payment makes that clear.

### Subsidized Loans

A **subsidized loan**, formally a Direct Subsidized Loan, is a federal student loan for undergraduates with demonstrated financial need, on which the **government pays the interest** while you're enrolled at least half-time, during the six-month grace period after you leave school, and during certain deferment periods. That makes it the least expensive loan available. You owe only what you borrowed when repayment starts. Subsidized loans are awarded only based on need, up to the subsidized limits in the table above. If your award letter offers one, it's usually the first loan to accept, if you need to borrow at all.

### Unsubsidized Loans

An **unsubsidized loan**, formally a Direct Unsubsidized Loan, is a federal student loan available to undergraduates regardless of financial need, on which **interest builds from the day the money is disbursed**, including while you're in school. You can pay that interest while in school or let it accumulate. If you let it accumulate, it's added to your balance when repayment begins, so you'll pay interest on interest. Unsubsidized loans fill the gap between your subsidized amount and your annual limit. A first-year student eligible for $3,500 subsidized can borrow $2,000 unsubsidized. Students without need can borrow the full annual limit as unsubsidized.

#### Worked Example: Subsidized Versus Unsubsidized

Ana and Casey each borrow $3,500 in their first year at 6.52% interest.

- **Ana's loan is subsidized.** The government pays the interest while she's in school. Four and a half years later, when repayment starts, she owes **$3,500.**
- **Casey's loan is unsubsidized.** Interest builds at about $228 a year. If Casey pays nothing during school, about $1,030 of interest has built up by the time repayment starts, so Casey owes about **$4,530.**

The two loans were identical on the award letter, but they cost very different amounts. That's why subsidized loans come first.

### Parent PLUS Loans

A **Parent PLUS loan** is a federal loan that **parents** borrow, in their own names, to help pay for a dependent student's education. Parents, not students, are legally responsible for repaying it. For 2026–27, the interest rate is **9.07%**, with an origination fee of about 4%, which is much higher than student Direct Loans. Parents must pass a basic credit check. For new borrowers starting July 1, 2026, Parent PLUS loans are capped at **$20,000 per year** and **$65,000 total** per student, and they must be repaid under a standard plan, without access to the income-based plan student borrowers can use.

Many award letters list a Parent PLUS loan to "cover the remaining balance." That's the most misleading line on many letters. A Parent PLUS loan isn't aid. It's family debt, often at a high rate. If a college's offer works only with a large Parent PLUS loan, the college isn't affordable for your family in the way the letter suggests. Treat it as the last option, and talk honestly as a family before accepting one.

### Private Loans

A **private loan** is a student loan from a bank, credit union, or other private lender, rather than the federal government. Private loans usually require a creditworthy cosigner, typically a parent. Their interest rates depend on credit and can be fixed or variable. They lack most federal protections, such as income-based repayment, deferment options, and forgiveness programs. Some award letters list "alternative" or private loan options, but colleges rarely require them. The standard advice is clear: **use all federal student loans first, and borrow privately only as a last resort,** after exhausting grants, scholarships, savings, work, and federal options. If you do borrow privately, compare several lenders, and read the terms carefully.

## Free Money and Borrowed Money

### Free Money Versus Borrowed Money

**Free money versus borrowed money** is the distinction between aid you never repay, meaning grants and scholarships, and aid you must repay, meaning loans. It's the single most important idea for reading an award letter. Only free money lowers what college costs you. Borrowed money only changes *when* you pay, and usually adds interest.

Many award letters blur the distinction. They might list grants, work-study, and loans together under "Your financial aid," add them into a single total, and show a small "remaining balance." A letter that offers "$46,000 in financial aid" may contain $30,000 of free money and $16,000 of loans. The headline total tells you almost nothing about the real cost.

The fix is a habit. **Every time you read an award letter, sort each line into one of three columns:** free money (grants and scholarships), earned money (work-study), and borrowed money (all loans, including Parent PLUS). Then ignore the letter's totals and use your own.

#### Worked Example: Lakeshore's Letter, Sorted

Marcus's award letter from Lakeshore University says:

> *Cost of attendance: $52,000. Lakeshore Grant $30,000; Federal Work-Study $2,500; Direct Subsidized Loan $3,500; Direct Unsubsidized Loan $2,000; Parent PLUS Loan $8,000. **Total financial aid: $46,000. Remaining balance: $6,000.***

Marcus sorts it:

| Free money | Earned money | Borrowed money |
|------------|--------------|----------------|
| Lakeshore Grant $30,000 | Work-Study $2,500 | Subsidized $3,500 |
| | | Unsubsidized $2,000 |
| | | Parent PLUS $8,000 |
| **$30,000** | **$2,500** | **$13,500** |

The letter's "remaining balance" of $6,000 assumes his family borrows $8,000 in Parent PLUS loans and he borrows $5,500 himself. His real net price is $52,000 − $30,000 = **$22,000.** That's very different from $6,000.

### Loans Versus Grants

**Loans versus grants** is the practical comparison you make on every award letter: how much of each offer is grants (free money) and how much is loans (borrowed money), and how the different kinds of each compare. Two offers with the same total aid can have very different values depending on the mix, as Chapter 14's worked example showed. When comparing, rank aid from best to worst:

1. **Grants and scholarships,** especially renewable ones.
2. **Work-study,** which you earn and don't repay.
3. **Subsidized federal loans,** where no interest builds while you're in school.
4. **Unsubsidized federal loans,** where interest builds from the start.
5. **Parent PLUS loans,** with a higher rate, family debt, and fewer repayment options.
6. **Private loans,** often with the highest costs and fewest protections.

You're never required to accept every part of an offer. You can accept the grants and decline or reduce the loans. You can also accept a loan now and repay part of it early if you end up not needing it. The ranking tells you which parts to accept first.

## Reading and Comparing Award Letters

### The Award Letter

An **award letter**, also called a financial aid offer or aid package, is the document a college sends you, usually with or soon after your admission, listing the financial aid it's offering for your first year. It typically shows the cost of attendance, each grant, scholarship, work-study award, and loan, and instructions for accepting or declining each one, along with a deadline.

Award letters are **offers, not bills.** Nothing on them is final until you accept, and you can accept some parts and decline others. They're also **estimates** in some ways. The cost of attendance includes the college's estimate of indirect costs (Chapter 3), and need-based aid assumes your family's finances match your forms. If you're selected for verification (Chapter 14), the offer may change once it's complete.

Colleges aren't required to use a standard format. Many use some version of a federal template, once called the **Shopping Sheet** and now the **College Financing Plan,** that separates grants from loans and shows a net cost. But many don't. Some omit the cost of attendance entirely, show only direct costs billed by the college, or label loans as "awards." That inconsistency is why the skills in the next sections matter. You can't compare letters until you've translated each one into the same terms.

#### Worked Example: A Letter Missing the Cost

Emily's offer from College C lists only her aid: a $12,000 merit scholarship, a $6,000 grant, $5,500 in federal loans, and work-study. It never states the cost of attendance. Without it, she can't calculate her net price.

She finds College C's published cost of attendance on its financial aid website: $64,000 for students living on campus. Now she can do the math. Her net price is $64,000 − $18,000 = **$46,000,** far above what her family can pay. The letter's warm language about her "generous award" had hidden that. A letter that leaves out the cost of attendance isn't necessarily misleading on purpose, but it always requires you to look up the missing number.

### Reading an Award Letter

**Reading an award letter** means translating each college's offer into the same set of numbers so you can understand and compare it. Work through every letter in the same order:

1. **Find the full cost of attendance** for one year, including indirect costs. If it's missing or shows only billed costs, look it up on the college's website.
2. **List every grant and scholarship.** Note which are need-based and which are merit, and whether each is renewable and under what conditions.
3. **List work-study** separately.
4. **List every loan** by type: subsidized, unsubsidized, Parent PLUS, or private.
5. **Calculate net price:** cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships.
6. **Calculate out-of-pocket cost:** what your family pays from savings and income, explained in the next section.
7. **Note conditions and deadlines:** the date to accept, renewal requirements, and anything you need to do, such as verification.

### Out-of-Pocket Cost

**Out-of-pocket cost** is the amount your family actually pays from savings and income for one year, after grants, scholarships, work-study, and any student loans you accept:

\[
\text{Out-of-pocket cost} = \text{Net price} - \text{Work-study earnings} - \text{Student loans accepted}
\]

Out-of-pocket cost and net price answer different questions. **Net price is the true cost** of a year at the college, since every dollar of it is paid eventually, now or later with interest. **Out-of-pocket cost is the cash your family needs now.** You need both. A college whose out-of-pocket cost fits your budget only because of large loans may be affordable this year but expensive later.

Notice that Parent PLUS loans aren't subtracted in the formula. Parent PLUS is a way for your family to pay the out-of-pocket cost, not a reduction in it. If your family would need a Parent PLUS loan to cover the out-of-pocket cost, that's important information about affordability.

#### Worked Example: Marcus's Three Offers

Marcus's family can contribute about $15,000 a year. He compares three offers, accepting work-study and his federal student loans at each:

| | Lakeshore | Brookfield College (private, strong aid) | Regional public |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost of attendance | $52,000 | $79,000 | $24,000 |
| Grants and scholarships | $30,000 | $60,000 | $11,000 |
| **Net price** | **$22,000** | **$19,000** | **$13,000** |
| Work-study | $2,500 | $2,500 | — |
| Student federal loans | $5,500 | $5,500 | $5,500 |
| **Out-of-pocket cost** | **$14,000** | **$11,000** | **$7,500** |

All three fit his family's $15,000, without any Parent PLUS loan. Lakeshore's letter had listed an $8,000 Parent PLUS loan, which he can decline. The regional university is least expensive by both measures. Brookfield, the college with the highest sticker price, costs less than Lakeshore by both measures too.

### Four-Year Cost Projection

A **four-year cost projection** estimates what a college will cost over all four years, not just the first. Award letters cover only one year, but you'll pay for four, or more if you take longer to graduate. Costs usually rise each year, often 3% to 5%, and aid may not rise with them.

To project, start with the first-year net price, then adjust for three things:

- **Rising costs.** Assume the cost of attendance rises about 3% a year unless the college publishes a guarantee.
- **How your aid behaves.** Need-based grants are recalculated each year and often rise with costs if your family's finances stay the same. Merit scholarships are usually fixed dollar amounts that don't rise. Ask each college how its aid changes in later years.
- **Renewal risk.** Include renewal conditions for each scholarship (Chapter 15). If you lose one, your cost jumps.

#### Worked Example: Projecting Four Years

Marcus projects four years at each college, assuming costs rise 3% a year and his grants stay flat, a cautious assumption:

| Year | Lakeshore | Brookfield | Regional public |
|------|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| 1 | $22,000 | $19,000 | $13,000 |
| 2 | $23,560 | $21,370 | $13,720 |
| 3 | $25,167 | $23,811 | $14,462 |
| 4 | $26,822 | $26,325 | $15,225 |
| **Four-year net price** | **$97,549** | **$90,506** | **$56,407** |

The first-year gap of $3,000 between Lakeshore and Brookfield grows to about $7,000 over four years. Brookfield meets full need, so its grant would likely rise with its costs, making its real four-year cost lower than projected. Marcus emails both financial aid offices to ask how their grants change in later years. The regional university stays far less expensive, but Marcus also weighs its CS program against the others, which isn't something a cost table can measure.

### Total Debt at Graduation

**Total debt at graduation** is the total amount you, and your parents if they borrow, will owe when you finish college, including interest that builds on unsubsidized loans while you're in school. It's one of the most important numbers for comparing colleges, because it follows you for years afterward. Add up every loan you'd accept over four years, plus any Parent PLUS loans your family would take. A useful guideline many advisors use is to keep total student debt **below your expected first-year salary.** That keeps monthly payments manageable on a typical ten-year plan. Compare debt across colleges alongside each college's graduation rate (Chapter 4), since debt without a degree is the hardest kind to repay.

#### Worked Example: Debt at Three Colleges

If Marcus borrows the federal maximum each year, his student debt is **$27,000** at any of his three colleges. But his family's plans differ. At Lakeshore, covering the four-year out-of-pocket cost would require his parents to borrow about $2,000 in Parent PLUS loans in years three and four, when rising costs push the out-of-pocket amount just above their budget. At Brookfield and the regional university, they wouldn't need to borrow.

At the regional university, Marcus could also borrow less. His out-of-pocket cost there is low enough that he could decline the unsubsidized loan each year, borrow only subsidized loans, and graduate owing about $19,000. A computer science graduate's typical starting salary is well above any of these totals, but the regional university leaves him and his family the least debt by a wide margin.

### Award Letter Comparison

**Award letter comparison** is putting every college's offer side by side in the same terms, including net price, out-of-pocket cost, four-year projection, and total debt, so you can see which offers are truly affordable and how they rank. The comparison tool below lets you enter up to four offers and see them together.

Once the numbers are side by side, the decision isn't just "pick the cheapest." It's a judgment that weighs cost against fit (Chapter 3): academic program, social fit, graduation rate, and outcomes. A college that costs $8,000 more over four years may be worth it for a significantly stronger program or a much higher graduation rate. One that costs $40,000 more usually isn't. ### Aid Renewal

**Aid renewal** is the process of keeping your financial aid in future years. Most aid isn't automatic. To renew, you typically must **file the FAFSA every year** (and the CSS Profile, if your college requires it) by the college's deadline, **make satisfactory academic progress**, which means a minimum GPA and completing enough credits, and meet any **scholarship renewal criteria** (Chapter 15). Need-based aid is recalculated each year from your family's new financial information, so it can rise or fall. Before you commit, ask each college two questions: *Does my grant usually stay the same if my family's finances don't change?* and *What GPA and credit requirements apply to each part of my aid?* A college that "front-loads" aid, generous in year one and less so afterward, can cost much more over four years than its first letter suggests.

## Asking for More

### Change in Circumstances

A **change in circumstances** is a significant change in your family's finances since you filed the FAFSA or CSS Profile, such as job loss, reduced hours or income, a death or divorce, unusual medical expenses, or a natural disaster. Because the FAFSA uses tax information from two years earlier, it can miss recent changes entirely. Colleges' financial aid officers have legal authority, called **professional judgment**, to adjust your aid based on documented changes. If your family's situation has changed, contact each college's financial aid office, explain the change, and provide documentation, such as a layoff letter, recent pay stubs, or medical bills. Many families never ask, and leave money on the table.

### Aid Reconsideration Requests

An **aid reconsideration request**, often called a financial aid appeal, is a formal request asking a college's financial aid office to review and improve your offer. It's usually based on either a **change in circumstances** or a **better offer from a comparable college.** It's normal, and colleges expect a reasonable number each year. The worst likely outcome is "no change." A good request goes to the **financial aid office,** not admissions. It explains your situation clearly and specifically, includes documentation such as a competing award letter or proof of changed income, and is polite and factual, never an ultimatum. Make your request soon after receiving your offer, well before the May 1 deadline. [Chapter 18](../18-handling-decisions/index.md) covers appeals as part of responding to decisions.

#### Worked Example: Marcus Asks Lakeshore

Lakeshore is Marcus's favorite, but Brookfield's offer is $3,000 a year cheaper. In early April, he emails Lakeshore's financial aid office:

*"Dear Financial Aid Office, thank you for my offer of admission and aid. Lakeshore is my first choice, especially its CS peer-mentoring program. I've also received an offer from Brookfield College, which is $3,000 less per year after grants (attached). My family can contribute about $15,000 a year, and I'd like to avoid Parent PLUS loans. Would you be willing to review my grant? I'm happy to send any additional information. Thank you, Marcus Johnson, Applicant ID 4471023."*

Two weeks later, Lakeshore increases his grant by $2,000 a year. It doesn't match Brookfield exactly, but it makes Lakeshore affordable without parent loans, and Marcus now has a real choice between his top two colleges.

## Key Takeaways

- **Work-study** is a chance to earn money through a job, not a discount on your bill.
- **Federal Direct Loans** have annual limits ($5,500, $6,500, then $7,500) and a 2026–27 undergraduate rate of 6.52%. **Subsidized loans** don't build interest while you're in school, but **unsubsidized loans** do. **Parent PLUS loans** are family debt at 9.07%. **Private loans** come last.
- **Free money versus borrowed money**: only grants and scholarships lower your cost. Rank **loans versus grants** from grants down to private loans.
- An **award letter** is an offer, not a bill, and formats vary. **Reading an award letter** means finding the full cost, sorting every line, and calculating your own numbers.
- **Net price** is the true cost. **Out-of-pocket cost** is the cash your family pays now.
- A **four-year cost projection** accounts for rising costs and fixed merit awards. **Total debt at graduation** should stay below your expected first-year salary.
- **Award letter comparison** puts offers side by side. **Aid renewal** requires refiling and meeting conditions each year.
- A **change in circumstances** can justify more aid. Make a polite, documented **aid reconsideration request** to the financial aid office.

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "An award letter shows: cost of attendance $48,000; grant $20,000; scholarship $5,000; work-study $2,000; subsidized loan $3,500; unsubsidized loan $2,000; Parent PLUS $10,000. What's the net price, and what's the out-of-pocket cost if you accept work-study and your student loans?"
    Net price = $48,000 − ($20,000 + $5,000) = **$23,000**. Out-of-pocket = $23,000 − $2,000 − $5,500 = **$15,500**. The Parent PLUS loan is one way your family might pay that $15,500, not a reduction in it.

??? question "Why should you accept a subsidized loan before an unsubsidized one?"
    The government pays the interest on a **subsidized loan** while you're in school, so you owe only what you borrowed. Interest on an **unsubsidized loan** builds from the start.

??? question "A college's first-year offer includes a $10,000 merit scholarship and a $15,000 need-based grant. Costs rise 4% a year. Which part of your aid is more likely to keep pace?"
    The **need-based grant** is recalculated each year and often rises with costs if your finances stay similar. The **merit scholarship** is usually a fixed amount, so its value shrinks relative to rising costs.

??? question "An award letter lists a 'remaining balance' of $3,000. Why might that be misleading?"
    The letter may count **loans**, including Parent PLUS, and **work-study** as if they reduced the cost. Recalculate the **net price** using only grants and scholarships.

??? question "Your parent was laid off in January, after you filed the FAFSA. What should you do?"
    Contact each college's **financial aid office**, explain the **change in circumstances**, and provide documentation such as a layoff letter and recent pay stubs. Financial aid officers can use **professional judgment** to adjust your aid.
