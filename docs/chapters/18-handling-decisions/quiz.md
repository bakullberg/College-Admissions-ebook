# Quiz: Handling Decisions

Test your understanding of responding to acceptances, denials, deferrals, and waitlists, appealing aid, and keeping perspective with these review questions.

---

#### 1. When does waitlist activity usually happen?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. In January, before regular decisions are released
2. Only on May 1, the national response date
3. In May and June, though it can continue into the summer
4. In December, alongside early decisions
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A waitlist means a college found you admissible but can't offer a spot yet, so it holds your file in case space opens after admitted students decide by May 1. The chapter says waitlist activity usually happens in May and June, but it can continue into the summer. December is when early decisions arrive. Because waitlist offers can come late, the chapter insists you deposit elsewhere by May 1 no matter what.

#### 2. In the chapter's explanation of waitlists, what does a college's "yield" mean?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The total amount of aid a college awards each year
2. The share of applicants who are placed on the waitlist
3. The share of admitted students who enroll
4. The number of applications a college receives each year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Yield is the share of admitted students who enroll. The chapter says colleges use waitlists partly to protect their yield, so they're more likely to admit students who will actually come. That's why a letter of continued interest helps, especially when it honestly says you'd enroll if admitted. The number of applications relates to the admission rate, not yield.

    **Concept Tested:** Yield

#### 3. Why can depositing at more than one college lead to a rescinded admission?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It counts as a missed deadline at the second college
2. Colleges only allow deposits from Early Decision applicants
3. It automatically cancels the student's financial aid at both colleges
4. It holds a seat the student won't use, and the Common App asks students to affirm they'll deposit at only one college
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says colleges take a dim view of depositing at more than one college, since it holds a seat you won't use. The Common App asks you to affirm that you'll deposit at only one college, and colleges can withdraw offers when they discover a double deposit. The one accepted exception is being admitted from a waitlist after depositing elsewhere, as long as you notify the first college right away.

    **Concept Tested:** Rescinded Admission

#### 4. According to the chapter, when does competing offer leverage work best?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. When the student exaggerates the other offer to create pressure
2. When the colleges are very different in type and selectivity
3. When the request goes to the admissions office instead of financial aid
4. When the colleges are comparable, the student prefers the college asked, and the request focuses on grants
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Competing offer leverage means using a better offer from one college to ask another to improve its offer. The chapter says it works best when the colleges are comparable in selectivity and type, and when you genuinely prefer the college you're asking. Include the actual award letter, focus on the difference in grants rather than loans, and never invent or exaggerate an offer, since colleges often verify. Appeals go to the financial aid office, not admissions.

    **Concept Tested:** Competing Offer Leverage

#### 5. Jordan deposited at College X by May 1 while staying on College Y's waitlist. In June, College Y admits Jordan, and Jordan wants to go. What should Jordan do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Keep both deposits until August to decide which feels better
2. Accept College Y and notify College X right away, expecting to lose the deposit
3. Accept College Y without telling College X, since the deposit holds the seat
4. Decline College Y, since a deposit can never be withdrawn
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter's waitlist strategy says to commit to another college by May 1 no matter what. If you're later admitted from the waitlist and choose to accept, you withdraw from your other college and usually lose that deposit. The chapter calls this the one accepted exception to the single-deposit rule, as long as you notify the first college right away. Holding two deposits or staying silent keeps a seat another student could use.

    **Concept Tested:** Waitlist Strategy

#### 6. Sam was deferred from an Early Action college. Fall grades just posted with two new A's, and Sam won a regional science award. What should Sam's update to the college include?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Nothing, since deferred applications are never reconsidered
2. A request that the college explain why Sam was deferred
3. A short note with the new grades, the award, and whether the college remains a top choice
4. A new personal essay on a different topic and three extra recommendation letters
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A deferral postpones the decision to the regular round, so the college wants another look, often with mid-year grades. The chapter says to send a short update with significant new information, such as new grades, awards, or achievements, and to say whether the college remains your first choice. Marcus's 250-word update to Lakeshore is the model. A flood of new materials isn't what the chapter advises, and a deferral is a "maybe," not a no.

    **Concept Tested:** Deferral

    **See:** [Handling Decisions](index.md)

#### 7. Morgan was admitted in March. Since filing the FAFSA, Morgan's parent has had work hours cut, and the aid offer now looks unaffordable. What should Morgan do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Email the admissions office asking it to waive the enrollment deposit
2. Send a polite, documented appeal to the financial aid office weeks before May 1
3. Decline the offer, since aid offers can't be changed once sent
4. Wait until after May 1 to see whether the college offers more aid on its own
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A financial aid appeal asks the financial aid office, not admissions, to review an offer after admission. The chapter says appeals usually rest on a change in family circumstances or a better comparable offer. Morgan's reduced income is a change in circumstances, so the appeal should explain it clearly, attach documentation, and stay polite and factual. Send it weeks before May 1. Asking costs nothing, and colleges rarely make an offer worse because you asked.

#### 8. Three students were admitted Early Decision in December. Which one has the kind of situation the chapter says can justify an Early Decision release?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A student who received a larger merit award from a different college
2. A student whose best friend was admitted to a college in another state
3. A student who visited again and decided the campus felt too small
4. A student whose aid offer is far above the net price calculator's estimate, and the family can't pay even after a review
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. An Early Decision release frees an admitted student from the binding commitment. The main accepted reason, and at many colleges the only one, is that the aid offer isn't enough to make attendance possible compared with what the net price calculator estimated. Elena's case shows the steps: contact financial aid, ask for a review, and request release if the gap remains. The chapter says release requires a genuine affordability problem, not a better offer elsewhere or a change of heart.

#### 9. Avery was deferred from an Early Action college, and Blake was waitlisted at a Regular Decision college. Which statement correctly distinguishes their situations?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Avery's file moves to the regular pool for review, while Blake is admissible but waiting for space after May 1
2. Blake's college will decide in the regular round, while Avery's will decide in June
3. Both decisions are final, so neither student should contact the college
4. Avery must enroll if later admitted, while Blake is free to choose
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A deferral postpones a decision on an early application so the college can reconsider it with the Regular Decision pool, often using mid-year grades. A waitlist means the college found the student admissible but has no spot yet, and waits to see how many admitted students enroll by May 1. Neither decision is final, so both students can send updates. A deferral from Early Action carries no binding commitment, and the option about which college decides when has the timelines reversed.

    **Concept Tested:** Admission Decisions

    **See:** [Handling Decisions](index.md)

#### 10. Emily is waitlisted at her first choice and plans to send a letter of continued interest. Which draft best meets the chapter's standards?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A short letter with new grades and a recent award, a specific reason the college fits, and an honest statement that she'd enroll if admitted
2. A short letter telling each of her three waitlist colleges that it is her first choice
3. A three-page letter restating her whole application, plus a new essay and two extra recommendations
4. A letter asking the college to explain why she wasn't admitted in the first round
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A good letter of continued interest is brief, usually well under a page. It's specific about why the college fits, includes genuine updates such as new grades or awards, and, only if true, says clearly that you'd enroll if admitted. The long letter ignores the warning against flooding a college with materials. Telling three colleges each is your first choice breaks the chapter's honesty rule. Asking why you were waitlisted doesn't strengthen your case.

    **Concept Tested:** Letter of Continued Interest
