## Every Answer Has a Next Step

After months of work and weeks of waiting, decisions arrive, sometimes all at once, sometimes one at a time from December to April. Each one comes with feelings, and each one also comes with things to do. An acceptance needs comparing. A deferral needs an update. A waitlist needs a decision about whether to stay on it. A denial needs time, then a turn toward the colleges that said yes.

This chapter covers every kind of decision you can receive and what to do next. It starts with admission decisions in general and acceptances, including the rare case of a rescinded offer. Then it covers asking for more aid, including using a competing offer. Next come the decisions that aren't a yes: denials, deferrals, and waitlists, with a strategy and a sample letter for staying on a waitlist. It covers how Early Decision applicants can be released from their commitment. And it ends with two ideas that help with the disappointing ones: rejection as a question of fit, and the long view.

## Admission Decisions

An **admission decision** is a college's answer to your application. There are four main kinds, and each calls for a different response:

| Decision | What it means | What to do |
|----------|---------------|------------|
| Acceptance | You're admitted | Record it, review aid, compare offers, decide by May 1 |
| Denial | You're not admitted this year | Take time, then focus on the colleges that said yes |
| Deferral | An early application moved to the regular round | Send an update, keep grades up, wait for the later decision |
| Waitlist | You're admissible, but no spot is available yet | Decide whether to stay on, send a letter, and commit elsewhere by May 1 |

Some colleges have variations, such as admission to a different program than you chose, admission for spring instead of fall, or admission conditional on a summer program. Read every decision letter fully, including any links in the portal, since next steps and deadlines are often there. Record each decision and its deadline in your tracker the same day.

#### Worked Example: Priya's Decisions

By April 1, Priya has heard from all ten colleges:

- **Accepted (6):** College C (Early Action, December), Hillcrest, her state flagship (with honors college), and three others.
- **Denied (2):** College B and one other reach.
- **Waitlisted (1):** College A, her top reach.
- **Accepted to a different program (1):** One university admitted her to its general studies division instead of its English program, with an option to apply to transfer into English after one year.

Her list did exactly what Chapter 3 designed it to do. Even with two denials and a waitlist from her reaches, she has six real options, including two affordable colleges she'd be glad to attend. She records each decision, its aid offer, and its deadline in her tracker, then takes a weekend off before comparing anything.

## Acceptances

### Acceptance

An **acceptance**, or offer of admission, means a college has admitted you and invited you to enroll. Congratulations, and **don't rush.** An acceptance isn't a decision to enroll. It's an invitation to consider one. Unless you applied Early Decision, you have until the national response date, **May 1** at most colleges, to choose among your offers.

After an acceptance:

1. **Celebrate.** It's real, and you earned it.
2. **Record it** in your tracker, with the enrollment deposit deadline and amount.
3. **Find the financial aid offer.** If it hasn't arrived, check the portal and confirm that your aid forms are complete.
4. **Note any conditions,** such as finishing senior year in good standing, completing a summer program, or submitting a final transcript.
5. **Wait to decide** until you've heard from every college and compared offers, as [Chapter 19](../19-making-your-final-choice/index.md) describes.

Many colleges invite admitted students to visit, attend admitted student days, and talk with current students. Use those opportunities to test your fit before May 1. Once you've decided, decline every other offer promptly, so those spots can go to students on waitlists.

### Rescinded Admission

A **rescinded admission** is an offer of admission that a college withdraws after it's been made. It's uncommon, but it happens every year, usually for one of three reasons. The first is a **serious drop in senior grades** shown on the final transcript (Chapter 7). The second is **disciplinary or legal problems**, such as an academic integrity violation, a suspension, or a serious offense. The third is **dishonesty in the application**, such as invented activities, false information, or self-reported grades that don't match the transcript (Chapter 12).

Colleges also take a dim view of **depositing at more than one college**, since that holds a seat you won't use. The Common App asks you to affirm that you'll deposit at only one college, and colleges can withdraw offers when they discover a double deposit. The one accepted exception is a waitlist: if a college admits you from its waitlist after you've deposited elsewhere, you may accept, but you must notify the first college right away. Public behavior matters too. Colleges have rescinded offers over offensive social media posts. The protection is simple: finish your senior year well, keep your conduct consistent with the person in your application, deposit at only one college, and if something goes wrong, such as a failing grade or a disciplinary incident, **tell the college first,** honestly, before it learns another way. Colleges are far more forgiving of a student who explains than of one who conceals.

## Asking for More Aid

### Financial Aid Appeals

A **financial aid appeal** is a request to a college's financial aid office to review and improve your aid offer after you've been admitted. Chapter 16 described how to make an aid reconsideration request. The appeal is the same process, timed for decision season. Appeals usually rest on one of two grounds: a **change in your family's circumstances**, or a **better offer from a comparable college.** Send it to the financial aid office, not admissions, as soon as you've reviewed your offers, ideally weeks before May 1. Explain clearly what you're asking for and why, attach documentation, and keep the tone polite and factual. Asking costs nothing, and colleges rarely make an offer worse because you asked.

### Competing Offer Leverage

**Competing offer leverage** is using a better financial aid offer from one college to ask another college to improve its offer. It works best when the colleges are **comparable,** meaning similar in selectivity and type, and when the college you're asking is one you'd genuinely prefer. Many private colleges explicitly review competing offers. Some publish lists of peer colleges whose offers they'll consider, while others don't negotiate at all. Public universities are less likely to change offers, especially for need-based aid governed by formulas. Include the actual competing award letter, focus on the difference in grants, not loans, and say honestly that the college is your preference. Never invent or exaggerate an offer. Colleges often verify.

#### Worked Example: Ana's Appeal

Ana's two favorite colleges both admitted her. Riverside College offered $41,000 in grants, for a net price of $9,000. Her first choice, Northfield University, which has a similar cost and selectivity, offered $35,000 in grants, for a net price of $15,000. Ana's family can manage about $6,000 a year beyond her loans and work-study.

She emails Northfield's financial aid office, attaching Riverside's letter:

*"Thank you for admitting me and for my aid offer. Northfield is my first choice, especially its education program's early classroom placements. My family's contribution is limited, and Riverside College has offered $6,000 more in grants (attached). Would you consider reviewing my grant? I'm also happy to provide any additional information about our finances, including my father's reduced work hours since January."*

Northfield increases her grant by $4,000. Combined with the change in her father's hours, which she documents, her net price drops to $11,000. That's still more than Riverside, but close enough that Ana can choose Northfield for its program without stretching her family past what it can do.

## Decisions That Aren't a Yes

### Denial

A **denial**, or rejection, means a college has decided not to admit you this year. It's disappointing, and it's fair to feel that disappointment. Give yourself time to feel it before you do anything else. Talk to someone, take a day, and don't make any big decisions immediately.

Then remember what a denial is. It reflects how your file compared with a particular applicant pool, at a particular college, in a particular year, shaped by institutional priorities you couldn't see or control (Chapter 2). It's **not a verdict on your ability or your worth.** At highly selective colleges, most denied applicants are fully qualified.

Denials are almost always **final.** Most colleges don't accept appeals except for documented errors or significant new information, and those rarely succeed. Your energy is better spent on the colleges that said yes. If you're denied everywhere, which is rare with a balanced list, talk to your counselor right away. Many colleges have openings after May 1, and community college transfer pathways are strong options.

### Deferral

A **deferral** means a college has postponed its decision on your early application, Early Decision or Early Action, and will reconsider you with the Regular Decision pool. A deferral isn't a denial. The college saw enough to want another look, often with your mid-year grades. If you're deferred from an Early Decision college, you're **released from the binding commitment.** If you're admitted later in the regular round, you're free to compare that offer with all your others, though it's worth confirming in the college's deferral letter. To strengthen your chances, keep your grades strong, confirm that your mid-year report will be sent, send a **short update** with any significant new information, such as new grades, awards, or achievements, and say whether the college remains your first choice. Then continue with your other applications as if the deferral were a "maybe," because it is.

#### Worked Example: Marcus Is Deferred

In mid-December, Lakeshore University defers Marcus's Early Action application to the Regular round. He's disappointed, but he follows the plan:

- He checks Lakeshore's portal, which asks deferred students for **mid-year grades** and allows an **optional update letter.**
- In late January, after fall grades post, including an A in his community college Discrete Mathematics course, he writes a 250-word update. He reports his grades, mentions that his restaurant ordering app now takes about 50 orders a week, and says Lakeshore remains a top choice.
- He keeps working on his other Regular Decision applications without slowing down.

In late March, Lakeshore admits him. The deferral wasn't a no. It was a request for more information, and Marcus gave it.

### Waitlist

A **waitlist** means a college found you admissible but can't offer you a spot yet. It's holding your file in case space opens after admitted students decide by May 1. Waitlist activity usually happens in May and June, but it can continue into the summer. How many students are admitted from a waitlist varies enormously by college and year. Some admit dozens or hundreds, and others admit none. At highly selective colleges, the share of waitlisted students eventually admitted is often small. Treat a waitlist as a real possibility worth pursuing, but **never as a plan.**

### Waitlist Strategy

A **waitlist strategy** is your plan for responding to a waitlist offer. It has four steps:

1. **Decide whether to stay on.** If you'd no longer choose the college, decline your spot. It helps someone else.
2. **Confirm your spot** by the method and deadline the college specifies, usually a short online form. Students who don't respond are removed.
3. **Send a letter of continued interest,** covered next, if the college accepts one. Some colleges ask you not to send extra materials, so follow their instructions.
4. **Commit to another college by May 1 and pay the deposit,** no matter what. Waitlist offers can come late, and you must never be left without a confirmed place. If you're later admitted from the waitlist and choose to accept, you'll withdraw from your other college and usually lose that deposit.

### Letter of Continued Interest

A **letter of continued interest** is a short letter to a college that has waitlisted or deferred you, confirming that you still want to attend and sharing meaningful updates since you applied. It matters because colleges use waitlists partly to protect their **yield**, the share of admitted students who enroll. They're more likely to admit students who will actually come. A good letter is brief, usually well under a page. It's specific about why the college fits you, includes genuine updates such as new grades, awards, or accomplishments, and, only if it's true, says clearly that you'd enroll if admitted. Send it to your regional admissions officer, or as the college directs, soon after confirming your spot. Don't send a flood of materials or multiple letters, and never say a college is your first choice if it isn't.

#### Worked Example: Priya's Letter to College A

Priya is waitlisted at College A, her top choice. After confirming her spot, she sends this letter to her regional officer:

> *Dear Ms. Alvarez,*
>
> *Thank you for keeping me on College A's waitlist. I'm writing to say that College A remains my first choice, and if I'm admitted, I will enroll.*
>
> *Since I applied, I've earned A's in all four of my AP courses this fall, and one of my poems was selected for a statewide student anthology. I also launched our school literary magazine's first online issue, which has now had more than 1,200 readers.*
>
> *My alumni interview with Ms. Patel confirmed what drew me to College A: its first-year writing seminars, where she described arguing about a single paragraph until midnight. That's exactly the kind of conversation I want to spend four years having.*
>
> *Thank you for your continued consideration.*
>
> *Priya Raman*

The letter is about 125 words, and it's specific, honest, and complete. In the meantime, Priya will deposit at one of her acceptances by May 1, so she has a confirmed place whatever College A decides.

## Early Decision Release

An **Early Decision release** is a college's formal agreement to free an admitted Early Decision applicant from the binding commitment (Chapter 1). The main accepted reason, and at many colleges the only one, is that **the financial aid offer isn't enough** to make attendance possible for your family, compared with what the college's net price calculator estimated. If that happens, contact the financial aid office immediately. Explain the gap and ask for a review. If the college can't close it, ask to be released from the agreement. Release isn't automatic, and it requires a genuine affordability problem, not a better offer elsewhere or a change of heart. Because Early Decision decisions come in December, a released student usually still has time to apply in the Regular round elsewhere.

#### Worked Example: Elena's Release

Elena applied Early Decision to a private college whose net price calculator estimated $14,000 a year. In December, she's admitted, but the aid offer comes to a net price of $27,000, because the college counted a family farm the calculator hadn't asked about. Her family can't pay it.

Elena's counselor helps her email the financial aid office the same week, explaining the gap, attaching farm income documents, and asking for a review. The college revises the offer to $22,000, still far above what her family can manage. Elena asks to be released from the Early Decision agreement, and the college agrees. She submits her Regular Decision applications by their January deadlines. That's one more reason the Chapter 1 advice holds: apply Early Decision only after running the net price calculator carefully.

## Keeping Perspective

### Rejection as Fit

**Rejection as fit** is the idea that a denial says more about the match between you and a college in a particular year than about your worth or potential. A college is building a class to meet its own needs: majors, regions, talents, finances, and priorities you couldn't see (Chapter 2). It's comparing you with thousands of other strong applicants, many of whom are equally qualified. In that setting, a "no" is a statement about fit and circumstance. It isn't a judgment of you as a person. Reframing rejection this way doesn't make it painless, but it makes it accurate. It also points you toward the right question: not *Why wasn't I good enough?* but *Which of the colleges that said yes is the best fit for me?*

### The Long-Term Perspective

The **long-term perspective** is the reminder that where you go to college matters less to your future than what you do once you're there. Research on college outcomes consistently finds that students' own effort, engagement, and choices, such as the courses they take, the relationships they build with professors, the internships they pursue, and whether they graduate, shape their futures more than the name on their diploma. Most students end up genuinely happy at a college that wasn't their original first choice. A year from now, the decision that feels enormous this spring will be one part of a much bigger story. The goal, as the preface put it, was never to get into "the best" college. It was to find a place where you'll do good work and become more yourself. That's possible at many of the colleges on your list.

## Key Takeaways

- Every **admission decision**, whether acceptance, denial, deferral, or waitlist, has its own next steps and deadlines. Record each one the day it arrives.
- An **acceptance** is an invitation to consider. Compare offers and decide by May 1. A **rescinded admission** follows a grade collapse, misconduct, dishonesty, or double-depositing. If something goes wrong, tell the college first.
- A **financial aid appeal** is normal and goes to the financial aid office. **Competing offer leverage** works best between comparable colleges, with the real letter attached.
- A **denial** is almost always final, and it reflects fit, not worth. A **deferral** is a "maybe," so send an update and keep your grades strong.
- A **waitlist** is a possibility, not a plan. Follow a **waitlist strategy**: confirm your spot, send a **letter of continued interest**, and deposit elsewhere by May 1.
- An **Early Decision release** is available when the aid offer genuinely isn't affordable.
- See **rejection as fit**, and keep a **long-term perspective**. What you do in college matters more than where.

??? question "You're waitlisted at your first choice and accepted at two other colleges. What should you do by May 1?"
    Confirm your waitlist spot, send a **letter of continued interest** if allowed, and **deposit at one of the colleges that accepted you** by May 1. Never rely on the waitlist alone.

??? question "You were deferred from an Early Decision college. Are you still bound to attend if admitted later?"
    **No.** At most colleges, a deferral releases you from the Early Decision agreement, and a regular-round admission is not binding. Confirm in the college's deferral letter.

??? question "Your Early Decision college admits you, but its aid offer is $12,000 more per year than its net price calculator estimated, and your family can't pay it. What can you do?"
    Contact the **financial aid office** right away, explain the gap, and ask for a review. If the gap remains, ask to be **released** from the Early Decision agreement.

??? question "Name two things that can lead a college to rescind an acceptance."
    Any two of: a serious drop in senior grades; disciplinary or legal problems; dishonesty in the application; depositing at more than one college; seriously offensive public conduct.

??? question "A friend says a denial means they 'weren't good enough.' How would you reframe it?"
    A denial reflects **fit and circumstance**, meaning how one file compared with a specific pool and a college's needs that year. At selective colleges, most denied applicants are fully qualified.
