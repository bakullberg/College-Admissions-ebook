# Quiz: Your Senior Year Course Load

Test your understanding of advanced course options, how senior grades reach colleges, and balancing rigor against burnout with these review questions.

---

#### 1. On what scale are AP exam scores reported?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 1 to 7
2. 1 to 36
3. 1 to 5
4. 0 to 100
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. AP scores run from 1 to 5, with 5 meaning "extremely well qualified" and 1 meaning "no recommendation." Scores are released in early July, and each college decides which scores earn credit. The 1-to-7 scale is used for IB course assessments, and 1 to 36 is the ACT scale. Many colleges give credit for a 4 or 5, and some for a 3.

#### 2. How is the IB Diploma scored, and what is the minimum needed to earn it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Out of 45 points, with at least 24 needed along with other conditions
2. Out of 36 points, with at least 18 needed
3. Out of 5 points per subject, with at least 3 in each
4. Out of 100 points, with at least 60 needed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The IB Diploma is scored out of 45 points: up to 7 for each of six subjects plus up to 3 for the core requirements. Students need at least 24 points, along with other conditions, to earn it. The core includes Theory of Knowledge, the Extended Essay, and Creativity, Activity, Service. Because results arrive in July after senior year, colleges use predicted grades from teachers when they make admission decisions.

    **Concept Tested:** IB Diploma

#### 3. Who sends the mid-year report to colleges, and roughly when?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The school counselor, usually in January or February
2. The student, usually in December with early applications
3. The College Board, usually in July with AP scores
4. Each teacher, usually at the end of the school year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The mid-year report is a form your school counselor sends to colleges, usually in January or February, with your first-semester senior grades. On the Common App, the counselor submits it directly, and most selective colleges require it. It arrives in time to affect decisions for regular-round and deferred applicants. The final transcript, sent after graduation, is a different document that confirms you finished the year as promised.

    **Concept Tested:** Mid-Year Report

#### 4. Which statement about college credit earned in high school matches the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The most selective colleges grant the most credit for AP scores
2. Credit transfers the same way to every college that accepts AP or IB
3. The college you enroll at decides what credit it accepts, and placement isn't the same as credit
4. Credit should be the main reason for choosing your senior courses
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter lists three facts about college credit in high school. The college you enroll at decides what it accepts, so the same record can earn very different amounts. Credit and placement aren't the same, since some colleges let you skip an intro course without giving credit hours. And the most selective colleges tend to grant the least credit, not the most. The chapter calls credit a bonus to check after admission, not a reason to choose courses.

    **Concept Tested:** College Credit in High School

#### 5. Why does the chapter warn students to take dual-enrollment grades seriously?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A low dual-enrollment grade cancels the student's high school diploma credit
2. Dual-enrollment grades are hidden from colleges unless they are A's
3. Dual-enrollment grades replace the student's high school GPA
4. The grade goes on a real college transcript permanently and may be counted later
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Dual enrollment means taking actual college courses while in high school, and the grade goes on a real college transcript permanently. A C stays on that college record and may be counted when you later apply to college or to graduate or professional school. Dual-enrollment grades don't replace your high school GPA and aren't hidden. The chapter also notes that many colleges want an official transcript from the college that granted the credit.

    **Concept Tested:** Dual Enrollment

#### 6. According to the chapter, what is the realistic goal for dealing with senioritis?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Stop worrying about grades once you've been admitted in the spring
2. Work just as hard as in junior year until graduation
3. Drop a core course to free up time once applications are in
4. Don't check out completely: keep grades roughly steady and go easier on extras
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says the goal isn't to work as hard as you did junior year, but to not check out completely. Keep attending, keep turning in work, and keep your grades roughly where they were, while going easier on extras rather than the courses you committed to. Grades still matter after admission because every offer is conditional on finishing well. Dropping a core subject or easing off sharply can actively hurt an application.

    **Concept Tested:** Senioritis

#### 7. Using the common weighting system (+0.5 for honors, +1.0 for AP), what is the weighted GPA for a B in AP History, an A in Honors English, an A in regular Spanish, and a B in regular Physics, each worth 1.0 credit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 4.13
2. About 3.88
3. About 3.50
4. About 3.63
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. With the weights added, the B in AP History counts as 3 + 1.0 = 4.0, the A in Honors English counts as 4 + 0.5 = 4.5, the regular A counts as 4.0, and the regular B counts as 3.0. The total is 15.5 points over 4 credits, or about 3.88. The unweighted GPA for the same grades would be 14 ÷ 4 = 3.50. The other options misapply the honors or AP weight.

    **Concept Tested:** Weighted GPA

#### 8. Jordan plans to study engineering and took two APs as a junior. Which senior schedule best follows the chapter's course selection strategy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Only electives in engineering-related topics, dropping English and world language
2. No math or science, to leave time for applications
3. All five core subjects, with AP Calculus and AP Physics, plus one elective Jordan enjoys
4. Six APs in every subject the school offers, including two in history
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter's strategy is to keep all five core subjects, go deepest in your strengths and intended major, match or raise your junior-year level, choose two to four rigorous courses that fit, and leave room for one course you genuinely want. A future engineer should take the hardest math and physics available. Six APs ignores the two-to-four guideline, and dropping math, science, or core subjects is one of the few choices that can actively hurt an application.

    **Concept Tested:** Course Selection Strategy

    **See:** [Your Senior Year Course Load](index.md)

#### 9. Taylor has committed to a college and took a dual-enrollment course at a community college senior year. What does Taylor need to do about final transcripts?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Nothing, because the college already received the mid-year report
2. Tell the counselor which college Taylor committed to, and also send the community college's final transcript
3. Send the high school's final transcript to every college that admitted Taylor
4. Wait for the college to request the transcripts in the fall semester
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The final transcript goes from your high school, after graduation, to the one college where you'll enroll. Your job is to tell your counselor which college you've committed to and check your portal to confirm it arrived. If you took dual-enrollment courses, you also need to send the final college transcript from the college that granted the credit. The mid-year report doesn't replace it, and nearly every college requires the final transcript before your first semester.

    **Concept Tested:** Final Transcript

    **See:** [Your Senior Year Course Load](index.md)

#### 10. A student took four APs as a junior and plans a senior schedule with no APs and no math, despite planning to major in economics. How would a reader most likely interpret this schedule?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. As a sign of strong focus on the student's intended major
2. As a break in the student's trend that also doesn't fit their story
3. As neutral, since senior courses aren't graded until after decisions
4. As a sensible way to avoid burnout during application season
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Readers look at a senior schedule for whether it continues the trend and whether it fits your story. Dropping from four APs to none breaks the trend, and an economics student dropping math sends a confusing message. Senior courses aren't neutral, because colleges see them as "in progress" when they read your application. Avoiding burnout means choosing two to four rigorous courses that fit, not easing off sharply, which the chapter says can actively hurt an application.

    **Concept Tested:** Senior Year Course Load
