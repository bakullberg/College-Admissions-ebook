## A Question With a Different Answer for Each College

Testing used to be simple. Nearly every four-year college required the SAT or ACT, so nearly every applicant took one and sent the score everywhere. That's no longer true. Today, some colleges require scores, many let you decide whether to send them, and a few won't look at them at all. The same score can help you at one college on your list and hurt you at another.

That makes testing less a single decision than a series of small ones. Should you take a test at all? Which one? How do you read the score you got? Is it worth taking again? And for each college on your list, should you send it? This chapter gives you a clear way to answer each question.

You'll start with what standardized tests are and the three testing policies colleges use. Then you'll learn how the SAT and ACT are built and scored, and how to compare scores across the two. The heart of the chapter is a decision rule: compare your score with each college's range, then decide college by college whether to submit. The chapter ends with the practical side, including score reports, registration, test dates, and how to prepare well without paying for expensive tutoring.

Testing policies and test formats change often. The details here reflect the tests as they are given in the mid-2020s. Before you register or decide, check the College Board and ACT websites and each college's admissions page for current rules.

## Standardized Testing

**Standardized testing** means giving every test-taker the same kind of test, under the same conditions, scored the same way, so results can be compared fairly across students from different schools. In college admissions, the term refers mainly to two tests: the **SAT** and the **ACT**.

Colleges use standardized tests because grades aren't standardized. As Chapter 2 showed, an A at one high school may not mean the same thing as an A at another, and GPA scales differ. A test score, by contrast, means the same thing whether you took the test in Iowa or Florida. That gives admissions readers one common measure to set beside a transcript they're still learning to interpret.

Tests have real limits, though. A score reflects one morning, not four years. Scores tend to rise with family income, partly because wealthier families can pay for tutoring and repeat attempts. And for many students, grades in challenging courses predict college success at least as well as a test does. Those concerns are why so many colleges stopped requiring scores around 2020, and why the debate continues. Some colleges have since brought requirements back, arguing that scores help them find talented students at less-known high schools.

For you, the practical takeaways are simple:

- **Scores are one factor among several.** At almost every college, your academic record outweighs your test score.
- **Scores can still matter a lot.** At test-required colleges, for merit scholarships, and in formulas like Iowa's Regent Admission Index, a score can change an outcome.
- **The decision is yours more often than it used to be.** That makes it worth understanding, not guessing.

#### Worked Example: What a Score Adds to a File

A reader at a selective college is looking at two applicants from high schools the reader doesn't know well. Both have 3.8 GPAs with strong rigor.

- **Applicant 1** submitted a 1450 SAT. The reader now has an outside measure that confirms the transcript. The 3.8 at an unfamiliar school is backed by a score near the top of the college's range.
- **Applicant 2** submitted no score. The reader relies on the transcript, the school profile, the counselor's rigor rating, and teacher letters. These can show academic strength just as clearly. They just do it differently.

Neither applicant is penalized. But the example shows what a strong score does: it adds a piece of evidence that's easy to compare. That's also why a weak score can hurt. It adds evidence that points the other way.

## Testing Policies

Every college sets a **testing policy**, its rule about whether applicants must submit test scores and how they'll be used. There are three main types, and the colleges on a single list often use all three.

### Test-Required Policy

A **test-required policy** means a college requires every applicant to submit an SAT or ACT score. An application without one is incomplete and won't be reviewed. Before 2020, this was the norm. Many colleges then went test-optional, and a number of them have since gone back to requiring scores. Highly selective private colleges such as MIT, Dartmouth, and Harvard require them, as do the public university systems of a few states, including Florida and Georgia. A few colleges, such as Purdue, call themselves **test-expected**: they ask every applicant for a score and accept an application without one only in exceptional cases. For planning purposes, treat test-expected as test-required.

At a test-required college, the submission decision is made for you. You must send a score, so the question becomes how to make that score as strong as you reasonably can, and whether to retest. Some test-required colleges also accept other exams in place of the SAT or ACT, such as AP or IB scores. Read the policy closely, because it may be more flexible than the headline suggests.

### Test-Optional Policy

A **test-optional policy** means applicants choose whether to submit test scores. If you submit, the college considers your score. If you don't, the college reviews your application fully without it, and says it won't hold the missing score against you. Most four-year colleges today are test-optional. The University of Iowa is one.

Test-optional policies have fine print worth reading:

- **Scholarships may still use scores.** At Iowa, scores at or above about a 26 ACT or 1230 SAT open access to larger merit scholarships, even though admission doesn't require a test.
- **Some programs may require scores** even when the college as a whole doesn't, for example honors colleges, nursing, or engineering.
- **Some applicants may need scores,** such as homeschooled students or applicants from schools without traditional grades.
- **Formulas may change how you're reviewed.** Iowa's Regent Admission Index needs a test score to calculate. Applicants who apply without one can still be admitted, but they're reviewed individually instead of by formula.

So "test-optional" really means "optional for admission, under the rules on this college's page." Check each college's own wording.

### Test-Blind Policy

A **test-blind policy** means a college won't consider SAT or ACT scores for admission at all, even if you send them. It's rarer than test-optional. The University of California and California State University systems are the best-known examples. At a test-blind college, the decision is simple: don't bother sending scores for admission. Some test-blind colleges still use scores after enrollment, for example to place students in the right first math or writing class, so check whether they ask for scores once you've committed.

The table below summarizes the three policies covered in the sections above.

| Policy | Must you submit? | Will the college look at a score you send? | Your decision |
|--------|------------------|---------------------------------------------|---------------|
| Test-required | Yes | Yes | Make your score as strong as you reasonably can |
| Test-optional | No | Yes | Compare your score with the college's range, then decide |
| Test-blind | No | No (for admission) | Don't send scores for admission |

#### Worked Example: One List, Three Policies

Priya checks the testing policy for every college on her ten-school list and records it in her tracker:

- **College A**, her top reach, is **test-required**. She must send a score no matter what.
- **Seven colleges**, including College B, College C, Hillcrest, and her state flagship, are **test-optional**.
- **One college** in California is **test-blind**. She won't send anything there.
- **One test-optional college** offers merit scholarships with a published score cutoff. She marks it, because a score could be worth money there even if it doesn't affect admission.

Before checking, Priya had assumed she'd either send her score everywhere or nowhere. Now she sees that she has one required decision, seven real choices, one non-decision, and one scholarship question. The rest of this chapter shows how she makes each choice.

### The Test-Optional Myth

The **test-optional myth** is the belief that "test-optional" means test scores no longer matter. In reality, test-optional means you get to *choose* whether a score is part of your application. A strong score that you submit can still help. A weak one you leave off won't hurt you. Scores can also still matter for merit scholarships, specific programs, and colleges that have gone back to requiring them. Students who believe the myth often skip testing entirely and then discover a scholarship cutoff or a test-required college too late to test. A better approach for most students is to take the test at least once, then decide school by school what to do with the score.

## The SAT and the ACT

The SAT and the ACT are the two standardized tests used for U.S. college admissions. Every college that accepts one accepts the other, and none prefers one over the other. So the choice between them is about which test suits you better.

### The SAT

The **SAT** is a standardized college admissions test made by the College Board. Since 2024 it has been given digitally, in the College Board's Bluebook app on a laptop or tablet. It has two sections:

- **Reading and Writing:** short passages, each followed by one question, testing reading comprehension, vocabulary in context, grammar, and editing.
- **Math:** algebra, advanced math, problem solving and data analysis, and geometry and trigonometry. A graphing calculator is built into the app and allowed for the whole section.

Each section is scored from 200 to 800, for a total of 400 to 1600. The test takes a little over two hours. It's **adaptive by section**. Each section has two parts, called modules, and how you do on the first module determines whether the second is harder or easier. That's why you can't compare raw question counts between students. The scoring accounts for difficulty.

### The ACT

The **ACT** is a standardized college admissions test made by ACT, Inc. It's given on paper or online, depending on the test center. Its core sections are:

- **English:** grammar, punctuation, and editing of passages.
- **Math:** algebra, geometry, and some trigonometry, with a calculator allowed throughout.
- **Reading:** comprehension of longer passages.
- **Science (optional):** interpreting data, graphs, and experiments. Science reasoning, not memorized facts.

Each section is scored from 1 to 36. Since 2025, Science has been optional, and the **composite score** is based on English, Math, and Reading. An optional writing test is also available, though few colleges ask for it. The core ACT takes about two hours, or longer with Science.

### Choosing Between Them

Because colleges treat the two tests equally, choose the one that fits you. The tests reward slightly different strengths:

| Feature | SAT | ACT |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Format | Digital, adaptive by section | Paper or online, not adaptive |
| Sections | Reading and Writing, Math | English, Math, Reading, optional Science |
| Score range | 400–1600 (two sections, 200–800 each) | 1–36 composite (each section 1–36) |
| Pace | More time per question | Faster; more questions in less time |
| Math share | About half the score | About one-third of the composite |
| Reading style | Short passages, one question each | Longer passages, several questions each |

The best way to choose is to take a free official practice test of each under timed conditions and compare the results using the concordance tables later in this chapter. If your scores are about the same, pick the test whose format you found more comfortable.

#### Worked Example: Choosing a Test

Marcus takes one official practice SAT and one official practice ACT on two Saturdays in the spring of junior year:

- **SAT practice:** 1310 (Reading and Writing 610, Math 700).
- **ACT practice:** 26 composite. He ran out of time on Reading and left six questions blank.

Using the concordance table, his 1310 SAT is about equal to a 28 ACT, so his SAT result is about two points stronger. The reason is pace. The ACT's faster timing cost him on Reading, while the SAT's math-heavy scoring rewarded his strength. Marcus chooses the SAT and focuses his prep there.

### The PSAT/NMSQT

The **PSAT/NMSQT** (Preliminary SAT/National Merit Scholarship Qualifying Test) is a practice version of the SAT, usually taken at school in October of 10th or 11th grade. It uses the same digital format and question types as the SAT, scored from 320 to 1520. It doesn't go to colleges, so a low score costs you nothing. Two things make it worth taking seriously. First, it's a **free diagnostic**. Your score report shows your strengths and weaknesses and links to free practice. Second, in **11th grade only**, high scorers can qualify for recognition and scholarships through the National Merit Scholarship Program. The cutoffs vary by state.

## Reading Your Score

### Section Scores

**Section scores** are the scores you earn on each separate part of a test: Reading and Writing and Math on the SAT, and English, Math, Reading, and the optional Science section on the ACT. They show where your strengths are, which a single total can hide.

Section scores matter for three reasons. First, **they guide your prep.** A 1200 SAT made up of 700 Reading and Writing and 500 Math calls for a very different plan than one made up of 600 and 600. Second, **some colleges and programs look at them directly.** An engineering program may care more about your Math score than your total. Third, **they're the building blocks of superscoring,** which combines your best section scores across test dates, as you'll see later.

### Composite Score

A **composite score** is the single overall score that combines your section scores. On the SAT it's called the **total score**, and it's the sum of your two section scores:

\[
\text{SAT total} = \text{Reading and Writing} + \text{Math}
\]

On the ACT, the composite is the **average** of the core section scores, rounded to the nearest whole number:

\[
\text{ACT composite} = \frac{\text{English} + \text{Math} + \text{Reading}}{3}
\]

Colleges report their score ranges using composite scores, so the composite is the number you'll compare against each college. When people say "I got a 1350" or "I got a 29," they mean the composite.

#### Worked Example: Calculating Composites

Priya's June SAT section scores were Reading and Writing 680 and Math 600:

\[
\text{SAT total} = 680 + 600 = 1280
\]

Casey's ACT section scores were English 25, Math 22, and Reading 26:

\[
\text{ACT composite} = \frac{25 + 22 + 26}{3} = \frac{73}{3} \approx 24.3 \rightarrow 24
\]

Both composites hide a pattern. Priya's Math score is 80 points below her Reading and Writing score, and Casey's Math is three or four points below the other sections. For both students, math is where extra prep would most likely raise the composite.

### SAT-ACT Concordance

**SAT-ACT concordance** is the official way of matching an SAT score to the ACT score that represents about the same level of performance. The College Board and ACT publish joint concordance tables, built from students who took both tests. Concordance matters because colleges often report only one test's range, or because you took the other test. To compare your score with a college's range, you sometimes need to convert it.

Here's a selection of values from the official tables. Each ACT composite corresponds to a short range of SAT totals.

| ACT composite | Equivalent SAT total range |
|---------------|----------------------------|
| 36 | 1570–1600 |
| 34 | 1490–1520 |
| 32 | 1420–1440 |
| 30 | 1360–1380 |
| 28 | 1300–1320 |
| 27 | 1260–1290 |
| 26 | 1230–1250 |
| 25 | 1200–1220 |
| 24 | 1160–1190 |
| 22 | 1100–1120 |
| 20 | 1030–1050 |
| 18 | 960–980 |

Two cautions. Concordance is approximate, since the tests measure slightly different things. And because the ranges are short, a one-point difference in ACT terms is often within the normal variation from one test day to another. Use concordance to place your score, not to split hairs.

## Deciding Where to Send Scores

### Score Benchmark Comparison

A **score benchmark comparison** is the step of placing your composite score against a college's middle 50% range, which you learned to read in Chapter 2, to see whether the score is likely to help or hurt your application there. It's the core of every test-optional decision.

To make one, you need two things for each college: your composite score, converted if necessary, and the college's 25th and 75th percentile scores, usually from its Common Data Set (Section C9) or admissions website. Colleges don't usually publish the 50th percentile, the median, so estimate it as the **midpoint** of the range. A college with a middle 50% SAT range of 1220–1390 has an estimated midpoint of about 1305.

Your score then falls into one of three zones:

- **At or above the midpoint:** your score is a strength at this college.
- **Below the 25th percentile:** your score is weaker than at least three-quarters of the students in the range, so it's more likely to hurt than help.
- **Between the 25th percentile and the midpoint:** a gray zone where other factors decide.

Remember Chapter 2's caution: at test-optional colleges, published ranges include only students who chose to submit, and those tend to be the higher scorers. That pushes the range up. A score slightly below a test-optional college's midpoint may be closer to typical among *all* admitted students than it looks.

### The Score Submission Decision

The **score submission decision** is your choice, made separately for each test-optional college, about whether to send your test score. The benchmark comparison gives you a starting rule:

- **At or above the midpoint → submit.** Your score strengthens your application.
- **Below the 25th percentile → don't submit.** Your application will be read fully without it.
- **In between → it's a judgment call.** Lean toward submitting if the score is close to the midpoint, or if the rest of your record is ordinary and the score adds strength. Lean toward withholding if your GPA and rigor are strong and the score sits near the 25th percentile, so it would undercut an otherwise strong academic record.

Three other factors can override the rule:

- **Merit scholarships.** If a college awards scholarships by score cutoff and your score clears it, submit, even if the score is only average for admission.
- **Program requirements.** If your intended program requires scores, you must submit.
- **Formula-based admission.** Where a formula like Iowa's RAI uses the score, run the formula both ways and submit if the score raises your result.

Make the decision **college by college**, not once for your whole list. Students often submit everywhere or nowhere, which almost guarantees sending a weak score somewhere or withholding a strong one somewhere else.

### The Retesting Decision

The **retesting decision** is the choice of whether to take the SAT or ACT again to try to raise your score. Retest when three things are true: your score is **below the level that matters** for at least one important college or scholarship; you have a **specific way to improve**, such as a weak section you can study for; and there's **time for another test date** before your deadlines. If all three hold, a retest is usually worth it. Most students improve somewhat on a second attempt, especially after focused prep.

Don't retest endlessly, though. Two sittings are enough for most students. A third makes sense only if something specific changed, like a new prep plan or a first test disrupted by illness. Beyond that, the gains shrink, and the time is usually better spent on essays and grades.

### Superscoring

**Superscoring** is a college policy of combining your highest section scores from different test dates into a new, higher composite. If you took the SAT twice and scored higher in Math the second time but higher in Reading and Writing the first time, a college that superscores uses the best of each. For the ACT, superscoring averages your best English, Math, and Reading scores across dates. Many colleges superscore, but not all, so check each college's policy. Superscoring makes retesting more attractive, because you can focus prep on one section without worrying that another will slip.

#### Worked Example: Priya's Retest and Superscore

Priya's June SAT was Reading and Writing 680, Math 600, for a total of 1280. She meets the three retest conditions. College C is a judgment call and College A requires a score. Her Math section is clearly weaker. And there's an October test date before her November and January deadlines. She spends eight weeks on math prep and retests in October:

| Test date | Reading and Writing | Math | Total |
|-----------|---------------------|------|-------|
| June | **680** | 600 | 1280 |
| October | 660 | **650** | 1310 |
| **Superscore** | 680 | 650 | **1330** |

Her October total, 1310, is a modest gain, because her Reading and Writing score dipped. But at colleges that superscore, her score is now **1330**. That's above College C's estimated midpoint of 1305, so she'll submit there. College B's 25th percentile is 1350, still above her superscore, so she still won't submit there. ## Sending Scores

### The Official Score Report

An **official score report** is a record of your test scores sent directly from the testing agency, the College Board for the SAT or ACT, Inc. for the ACT, to a college. Colleges trust official reports because they come from the source, the same way they trust a transcript sent by your school. When you register for a test, you can usually name a few colleges to receive free score reports. After that, you order reports through your College Board or ACT account for a fee, and fee waivers cover some reports for eligible students.

Many colleges no longer require official reports at the time you apply. Instead, they let you **self-report** your scores on the application, then require an official report only if you enroll. That saves money and time. Chapter 12 covers how to enter self-reported scores on the Common App. But some colleges, especially test-required ones, still want official reports at the application stage. Check each college's policy and record it in your tracker.

Plan for timing. Official reports take time to arrive and be matched to your file, so send them well before the supporting document deadline. If you're testing late, in October or November of senior year, ask whether the college will accept scores from that date for your round.

#### Worked Example: Which Report Goes Where

Priya has to send scores to three colleges:

- **College A (test-required)** requires an official report at the time of application. She orders it from her College Board account the day her October scores post. College A superscores, so she sends both test dates.
- **College C (test-optional, superscores)** accepts self-reported scores. She enters her best Reading and Writing and best Math scores from different dates, as College C's instructions allow, and will send an official report only if she enrolls.
- **Her state flagship (test-optional)** also accepts self-reported scores. She enters them and adds a note in her tracker to send an official report after she commits in May.

By using self-reporting where it's allowed, Priya pays for one official report instead of three.

### Score Choice

**Score Choice** is a College Board option that lets you pick which SAT test dates to send to a college, rather than sending every score you've ever earned. ACT works similarly, since you choose which test dates to report. Score Choice means a weak test date doesn't have to follow you everywhere. But some colleges require you to send **all** scores from every test date. Others recommend it, mainly so they can superscore. Check each college's policy. Where a college superscores, sending every date usually helps you, because the college will use only your best section scores.

## Taking the Test

### Test Registration

**Test registration** is signing up for a specific SAT or ACT date and test center through the College Board or ACT website. You'll create an account, choose a date and a nearby test center, pay the fee (or apply a fee waiver if you're eligible, which your counselor can help with), and, for the SAT, install the Bluebook app on your device before test day. Register early. Regular deadlines fall several weeks before each test date, nearby test centers fill up, and late registration costs extra. Many high schools also offer the SAT or ACT free during the school day, so ask your counselor whether yours does.

### Test Date Planning

**Test date planning** is choosing when to take the SAT or ACT so that you have time to prepare, a chance to retest, and scores that arrive before your deadlines. The SAT and ACT are each offered about seven times a year, mostly in fall and spring, with a few summer dates. A plan that works for most students looks like this:

1. **Spring of junior year:** take your first official test after a few months of prep.
2. **Summer:** review your section scores and prep for a retest if needed.
3. **August–October of senior year:** retest once if your plan calls for it.
4. **November:** a last option for Regular Decision deadlines, but too late for most early deadlines.

Testing for the first time in senior fall leaves no room for a second try if things go badly.

### Test Accommodations

**Test accommodations** are changes to testing conditions, such as extended time, extra breaks, a reader, or a separate room, for students with documented disabilities or medical needs. Both the College Board and ACT offer them, and they don't appear on score reports sent to colleges. Students who receive accommodations at school through an IEP or 504 plan usually request them through the school's testing coordinator. Start early. Approval can take several weeks, and the request has to be approved before your registration deadline.

## Preparing for the Test

### Official Practice Tests

**Official practice tests** are full-length practice exams released by the College Board and ACT, made of real or real-format questions and scored on the official scale. They're the most accurate practice you can do, because no one knows the tests better than the companies that write them. For the SAT, full-length adaptive practice tests are free in the Bluebook app, which works just like the real digital test. For the ACT, free official practice tests are on ACT's website.

Use official tests for the most important parts of your prep: your **diagnostic** at the start, **timed practice** as you go, and a final full-length run a week or two before test day. Save them for those moments. If you use them all up early on casual practice, you'll have no realistic measure left when you need one. Third-party practice books and sites are fine for drilling specific skills, but their tests often don't match the real one in difficulty or scoring.

#### Worked Example: Rationing Practice Tests

Marcus has eight weeks before his SAT and access to six free official practice tests in Bluebook. He plans to use them like this:

- **Week 1:** one test as a diagnostic.
- **Weeks 2–5:** skill practice on his weak areas, plus one test at the end of week 3 to check progress.
- **Weeks 6–7:** two tests under real timing, one per week.
- **Week 8:** one final test eight days before the real exam, then light review.

That's five tests, with one in reserve in case his test date gets moved. Each one is used to measure progress, not wasted on casual practice.

### Free Test Prep

**Free test prep** means preparing for the SAT or ACT using no-cost resources rather than paid courses or tutors. For most students, free prep is enough. The strongest resources are official ones: Official SAT Practice on Khan Academy, built with the College Board, offers personalized practice linked to your PSAT or SAT results. Bluebook has free full-length SAT practice tests. ACT publishes free practice tests and sample questions. Your school or library may also offer free prep sessions. Paid options, such as classes, books, and tutors, can help students who need structure or have one narrow weakness. But what matters most is consistent practice over two to three months, and that costs nothing.

### Diagnostic Practice Test

A **diagnostic practice test** is a full-length official practice test taken at the start of your prep, under realistic conditions, to measure your starting score and find your weakest sections and question types. It tells you where to spend your time. Take it in one sitting, timed, without notes, then study the score report by section and question type. If you haven't chosen a test yet, a diagnostic of each, the SAT and the ACT, also tells you which one suits you better.

### Error Log

An **error log** is a running record of every practice question you get wrong, noting the question type, why you missed it, and what you'll do differently. It turns mistakes into a study plan. Most students who review only their score miss the pattern behind it.

A simple error log has these columns:

| Date | Section | Question type | Why I missed it | Fix |
|------|---------|---------------|-----------------|-----|
| Aug 3 | Math | Systems of equations | Set it up right, made an arithmetic slip | Check work on the built-in calculator |
| Aug 3 | Reading and Writing | Transitions | Didn't know "nevertheless" signals contrast | Review a list of transition words |
| Aug 10 | Math | Percent change | Used the new value as the base | Practice 10 percent-change questions |

After two or three practice tests, sort the log by question type and by reason. If most misses are careless errors, practice checking your work. If they cluster in one skill, study that skill.

### Timed Practice

**Timed practice** means practicing under the real test's time limits, for single sections or full tests, to build pacing and stamina. Untimed practice teaches content. Timed practice teaches you to use that content fast enough. Once your error log shows your content gaps shrinking, shift most of your practice to timed sections, and take at least two full-length timed tests before test day. Practice at the same time of day as your real test, usually Saturday morning, so test day feels familiar.

## Key Takeaways

- **Standardized testing** gives colleges a common measure. Most colleges are **test-optional**, some are **test-required**, and a few are **test-blind**. Check each college's current policy.
- The **test-optional myth** says scores no longer matter. Submitted scores can still help, and they can matter for scholarships and specific programs.
- The **SAT** (400–1600, digital) and the **ACT** (1–36) are accepted equally. Choose the one that suits you. The **PSAT/NMSQT** is free practice and, in 11th grade, a National Merit qualifier.
- **Section scores** show your strengths. The **composite score** is what colleges compare. **SAT-ACT concordance** converts between the tests.
- A **score benchmark comparison** places your score against each college's middle 50% range. Make the **score submission decision** college by college: submit at or above the midpoint, withhold below the 25th percentile, and use judgment in between.
- Make a **retesting decision** only when a higher score matters, you can improve, and there's time. Two sittings are enough for most students. **Superscoring** combines your best sections.
- **Official score reports** come from the testing agency. Many colleges accept self-reported scores until you enroll. **Score Choice** lets you pick which dates to send, unless a college requires all scores.
- Plan **test registration** and **test dates** early, and request **test accommodations** well ahead.
- **Free test prep** is enough for most students. Start with a **diagnostic practice test**, keep an **error log**, move to **timed practice**, and save **official practice tests** for measuring progress.

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "An SAT test-taker scored 640 in Reading and Writing and 590 in Math. What's their total, and roughly what ACT composite does it concord to?"
    640 + 590 = **1230**. On the concordance table, 1230 falls in the 1230–1250 range, which corresponds to an ACT composite of about **26**.

??? question "A test-optional college's middle 50% SAT range is 1300–1450. Your score is 1340. Using the chapter's rule, should you submit?"
    The estimated midpoint is (1300 + 1450) ÷ 2 = **1375**. Your 1340 is above the 25th percentile but below the midpoint, so it's a **judgment call**. Weigh the strength of the rest of your record, and remember the published range is probably inflated.

??? question "Your ACT scores from two dates are English 27/29, Math 24/23, Reading 28/26. What's your superscore?"
    Best of each: English 29, Math 24, Reading 28. (29 + 24 + 28) ÷ 3 = 81 ÷ 3 = **27**.

??? question "A college is test-blind. Should you send your 1500 SAT?"
    **Not for admission.** A test-blind college won't consider scores for admission even if you send them. Check whether it uses scores for course placement after you enroll.

??? question "Why should you save official practice tests instead of using them for everyday practice?"
    They're the most accurate measure of your real score. If you use them up early, you won't have a realistic way to measure progress before test day. Use other materials for drilling skills.
