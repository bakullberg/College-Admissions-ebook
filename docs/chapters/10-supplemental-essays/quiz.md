# Quiz: Supplemental Essays

Test your understanding of school-specific research, the common supplement types, and managing many supplements with these review questions.

#### 1. How long are short-answer questions typically, according to the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 400 to 650 words
2. 250 to 400 words
3. 25 to 100 words
4. 150 to 250 words
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Short-answer questions are brief supplemental prompts, often 25 to 100 words, asking for quick, direct responses such as a favorite book or the best advice you've received. The 150-to-250 range is the typical length of an activity essay, and Why Us, major, and community essays often run up to 400 words. The chapter says readers often get their clearest sense of your personality from short answers, because there's no room to hide.

#### 2. When adapting a core essay for a new college, how much does the chapter say you should rewrite with college-specific details?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. At least 30 to 40 percent
2. About 5 to 10 percent
3. Only the first and last sentences
4. Only the college's name
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter's adaptation method is to write one strong core version of each recurring type, then rewrite at least 30 to 40 percent for each college with specific, researched details. For Why Us essays, nearly everything except your personal goals should be new. Changing only the name is the recycled essay trap. You should also re-fit each version to the exact prompt and length and run the swap test before submitting.

#### 3. Which source does the chapter warn against relying on for school-specific research?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The course catalog
2. Conversations with current students
3. Department pages for your intended major
4. The admissions homepage and brochures
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says to avoid relying on the admissions homepage and brochures, because they describe the college in the same terms every college uses, such as "vibrant community" and "world-class faculty." Those are exactly the phrases readers don't want repeated back to them. Department pages, the course catalog, and conversations with current students are among the most useful sources, since they give specific, believable details you can connect to yourself.

#### 4. What does the chapter mean when it says a strong Why Us essay is "two-directional"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It compares the college with one other college on the list
2. It names concrete things about the college and connects each to something concrete about you
3. It describes both the college's strengths and its weaknesses
4. It is written once for the college and once for the major department
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A strong Why Us essay names specific things about the college, such as a program, course, professor, or organization, and connects each one to a goal, experience, or interest of yours. The essay should read as if only this applicant could have written it for this college. Comparing colleges or listing weaknesses isn't what the chapter describes. A good short structure is two or three specific details, each paired with a sentence about why it matters to you.

    **Concept Tested:** Why Us Essay

    **See:** [Supplemental Essays](index.md)

#### 5. At a college that tracks demonstrated interest, why can a generic supplement hurt an application?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It signals the opposite of interest, suggesting the student may not enroll
2. It lowers the student's academic rating in the reading round
3. It is automatically flagged and the application is rejected
4. It replaces the personal essay in the reader's file
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. At colleges that track demonstrated interest, a specific, well-researched supplement is one of the clearest signals that you'd enroll if admitted. The chapter says a generic supplement doesn't just miss that chance, it signals the opposite of interest. Nothing in the chapter suggests generic essays are automatically rejected or change the academic rating. The supplement also adds to the personal essay rather than replacing it.

#### 6. When does the chapter suggest using an open "additional information" box?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Only when something important, like a schooling gap or sharp grade change, needs explaining
2. To paste in a second personal essay on a different topic
3. Always, since leaving it empty suggests low interest
4. To repeat your strongest activities so readers notice them
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter separates optional prompts that ask real questions, which are usually wise to answer, from open "additional information" boxes. Use the open box only when something important isn't explained elsewhere, such as a gap in your schooling, a family circumstance, or a sharp change in grades. An empty additional-information box is completely normal, and filling it with more of the same doesn't help. A second essay or repeated activities would be exactly that.

#### 7. Which sentence from a Why Us essay would correctly fail the swap test, meaning it would stop making sense if another college's name were swapped in?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "Your world-class faculty will challenge me to grow as a person."
2. "Lakeshore's Software for Social Good course would let me keep building apps for local businesses like my family's restaurant."
3. "I know I will thrive academically and socially at your school."
4. "I am drawn to your welcoming community and beautiful campus."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The swap test replaces the college's name with another one. If the essay still makes sense, it isn't specific enough. The Lakeshore sentence names a real course at that college and ties it to the student's own experience, so it no longer works with a different name. The other three use generic marketing phrases, like "beautiful campus" and "world-class faculty," that could describe almost any college.

    **Concept Tested:** Swap Test

    **See:** [Supplemental Essays](index.md)

#### 8. Jamie's personal essay is about caring for a grandparent. One college asks for a 200-word activity essay. Which choice best follows the chapter's advice?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Copy Jamie's 150-character activity descriptions into the essay
2. Write a list of all of Jamie's activities to show breadth
3. Write about caring for the grandparent again, since it's Jamie's strongest story
4. Write about Jamie's job repairing phones, using one specific moment from it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says to choose an activity that matters to you and that your personal essay doesn't already cover, then use a specific moment or detail to show what you do and why it matters. Repeating the grandparent story wastes a chance to show something new. A list or copied descriptions doesn't go beyond what the activities list already shows, and the activity essay is meant to add depth.

    **Concept Tested:** Activity Essay

#### 9. A student is genuinely undecided between biology and psychology and must write a Why This Major essay. What does the chapter suggest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Pick one major and claim lifelong certainty about it
2. Leave the essay blank, since undecided students are exempt
3. Write about two or three interests honestly, if the college invites it
4. Write a general essay about why college matters
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says that if you're undecided, many colleges invite you to write about two or three interests instead, and exploring honestly reads far better than inventing certainty. A strong major essay still shows where your interest came from with a specific experience, and what you'd do with it at that college. Leaving it blank or writing about college in general wouldn't answer the prompt.

#### 10. Student A writes one Why Us essay and sends it to six colleges, changing only the names. Student B writes one core community essay, adapts it for two colleges, and does fresh research for each Why Us essay. Which analysis is most accurate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both are using the same smart strategy, since both reuse writing
2. Student B is in more danger, since adapting essays wastes time
3. Student A has fallen into the recycled essay trap and risks signaling low interest or leaving a wrong name
4. Student A saves time and faces no real risk, since readers rarely compare essays
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The recycled essay trap is sending one supplemental essay to several colleges with only the name changed. Readers can usually tell, it signals low genuine interest, and recycled essays sometimes keep the wrong college's name or details. Student B is doing essay adaptation: reusing material and thinking, which the chapter calls fine and smart, while writing new college-specific content. The difference is reusing material versus reusing sentences unchanged.

    **Concept Tested:** Recycled Essay Trap
