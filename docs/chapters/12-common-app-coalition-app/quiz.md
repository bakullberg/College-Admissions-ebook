# Quiz: Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App

Test your understanding of the application platforms, the core sections, each college's own section, and sending official documents with these review questions.

---

#### 1. On which platform does the Coalition App now run?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Parchment
2. Scoir
3. BigFuture
4. Naviance
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The Coalition App is a second shared application platform, now run on Scoir, that a smaller group of colleges accepts. Naviance and Parchment are named in the chapter as services some high schools use to send transcripts, and BigFuture is a college search website. Colleges that accept both the Common App and the Coalition App treat them equally, so most students use whichever platform covers their list.

    **Concept Tested:** Coalition App

#### 2. Which institution code is used to send your FAFSA to a college?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Your high school's CEEB code
2. The college's SAT code
3. The college's ACT code
4. The college's Federal School Code
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Each college has several institution codes, one for each system. The Federal School Code sends your FAFSA to the college, and the University of Iowa's is 001892. The SAT code sends official SAT and AP scores, the ACT code sends ACT scores, and the CEEB code identifies your high school on applications. A report sent with the wrong code goes to the wrong college and can't easily be redirected, so record each code in your tracker.

#### 3. What does the chapter say about disciplinary history questions on the Common App?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The Common App dropped them, but individual colleges can still ask in their own member questions
2. They were removed entirely, and no college is allowed to ask them
3. They appear only for students applying Early Decision
4. The Common App requires every applicant to answer them in the profile section
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Common App dropped its criminal history question in 2018 and its school discipline question starting with the 2021–22 application. But individual colleges can still ask either one in their own member questions. If a college asks, the chapter says to answer honestly, since a brief explanation that shows what you learned is far better than an omission discovered later. Nothing in the chapter ties these questions to Early Decision.

#### 4. Why does the chapter tell students not to decide for themselves whether they "count" as first-generation in the family section?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Colleges ignore the family section when reviewing applications
2. Each college applies its own definition to the parent education you report
3. First-generation status is decided by the student's counselor alone
4. First-generation status only matters for financial aid, not admission
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The family section is how colleges learn whether you're a first-generation student and whether you have legacy connections. The chapter says to report each parent's or guardian's highest completed education accurately and let each college apply its own definition to your answers. Colleges do use the section, so it isn't ignored. Counselors may mention it in their letter, but they don't decide the status for each college.

#### 5. How does using an application fee waiver affect an admission decision?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It requires the student to apply for need-based aid
2. It moves the application into a separate review pool
3. It has no effect, and fee waivers are confidential
4. It lowers the student's chances at colleges that consider finances
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter states that fee waivers are confidential and that using one has no effect on your admission. On the Common App, you answer fee waiver eligibility questions in the profile section, and your counselor may be asked to confirm. Students may qualify through free or reduced-price lunch, certain public benefits, or household income, and many colleges also waive fees for first-generation students or visitors. Nothing in the chapter ties waivers to aid applications.

#### 6. Why does the chapter recommend reading the application preview for every college, even after you've already submitted to others?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The preview is the only place your test scores appear
2. Colleges require a signed copy of each preview
3. Each preview automatically fixes typos in your essay
4. Each college's version has its own questions and supplements, and formatting can shift in the PDF
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The application preview is the PDF of your application exactly as a college will see it. Each college's version includes its own member questions and writing supplements, and formatting can shift between the entry form and the PDF. Line breaks, special characters, and essay paragraphs sometimes display differently. The preview doesn't fix anything for you, which is why reading it slowly is your last chance to catch errors before a reader does.

    **Concept Tested:** Application Preview

    **See:** [Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App](index.md)

#### 7. While filling out the Courses and Grades section, a student remembers earning a B+ in sophomore chemistry, but the unofficial transcript shows a B. What should the student enter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. B, exactly as the transcript reports it
2. A converted 4.0-scale number instead of a letter
3. B+, since that's the grade the student believes was earned
4. Nothing, and leave the course off to avoid confusion
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter says to complete the Courses and Grades section with your unofficial transcript open next to you and enter course names and grades exactly as the transcript reports them, even if you think a grade was unfair. Don't round or convert grades unless the form asks. If you're admitted, the college checks your self-report against the official transcript, and a discrepancy can raise questions or, in serious cases, lead to a rescinded offer.

    **Concept Tested:** Courses and Grades Section

    **See:** [Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App](index.md)

#### 8. A public university's member questions ask for a first-choice and an optional second-choice major. Its nursing program is capped and is a reach for the student. What does the chapter's example suggest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. List an easier major first, then nursing second
2. List only nursing, to show the college strong commitment
3. List nursing first and a related second choice, after checking how second choices are handled
4. Leave both answers blank, since majors can be chosen later
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Member questions can control which program you're applying to, and some can't be changed after submission. In Marcus's example, listing only a capped major risked a denial if the program said no, while listing a second choice let the university admit him to a related program. He confirmed on the university's website how second choices are handled. Leaving the question blank or reversing the order wouldn't reflect the student's real first choice.

    **Concept Tested:** Member Questions

    **See:** [Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App](index.md)

#### 9. A college's supporting documents deadline is November 9, and it requires an official ACT report. By which date should the student order the report?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. November 12
2. November 8
3. October 15
4. November 2
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says to order official score reports at least two to three weeks before each college's supporting document deadline, since they take time to be delivered and matched to your file. October 15 leaves more than three weeks, matching Casey's example with Iowa. November 2 and November 8 leave only days, and November 12 is after the deadline. Afterward, check the college's applicant portal to confirm the score arrived.

#### 10. Marcus almost left his two community college courses off the Education section. Why would that omission have caused two separate problems?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It would have changed his legal name on file and blocked his scores
2. It would have lowered his GPA and canceled his course credit
3. It would have made him ineligible for fee waivers and Early Action
4. It would have hidden strong rigor signals and created a discrepancy when the college transcript arrived
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Education section must list every school and every college course you've taken, including dual enrollment. Leaving off Marcus's courses would have hidden two of his strongest rigor signals in his intended major. It would also have created a discrepancy when the community college's transcript arrived later, which a college may treat as an omission. The other options describe effects the chapter never connects to the Education section.

    **Concept Tested:** Education Section
