#### 1. Which of these is NOT one of the five dimensions the chapter uses to compare finalists?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Outcomes, such as graduation and retention rates
2. Your gut sense
3. Social and campus fit
4. National rankings
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter compares finalists on five dimensions: cost, academic fit, social and campus fit, outcomes, and your gut sense. It says to weigh these by what matters to you, using the priorities you ranked in Chapter 3, not by national rankings or what others expect. A college ranked higher nationally isn't automatically the better choice for your goals, budget, and life. Outcomes, social fit, and gut sense are all on the list.

#### 3. A student's chosen college requires an enrollment deposit that would be a financial hardship for the family. What does the chapter suggest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Skip the deposit, since colleges hold places without one
2. Ask the college about a deposit waiver or deferral
3. Wait until after May 1 and pay the deposit with the first bill
4. Pay the deposit at a cheaper college instead to hold a spot
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. An enrollment deposit holds your place once you accept an offer, and it's usually due by May 1. If it's a hardship, the chapter says to ask the college about a deposit waiver or deferral, since many colleges waive or defer deposits for students with fee waivers or financial need. Iowa, for example, defers its acceptance fee to the first bill for students who used an application fee waiver. Skipping or paying late without approval can mean losing your place.

#### 4. A student has committed to one college but wants to stay on another college's waitlist. Which offers should the student decline?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The enrolled college's offer, to keep the waitlist option open
2. None, until the waitlist decision arrives in the summer
3. Every other offer except the enrolled college and that waitlist
4. Only the offers from colleges the student never visited
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Declining offers means formally telling every other college that admitted you that you won't attend, promptly after you commit. The chapter says that if you're staying on a waitlist elsewhere, decline everything except your enrolled college and that waitlist. Declining frees your place, and sometimes aid, for another student, possibly one on that college's waitlist. Holding offers until summer keeps seats others could use.

#### 5. A student weights Academic fit 5, Cost 4, and Social fit 3. College X scores 4, 3, and 5 on those factors. College Y scores 3, 5, and 3. What are the weighted totals?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. College X 47, College Y 44
2. College X 12, College Y 11
3. College X 44, College Y 47
4. College X 60, College Y 60
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. In a side-by-side comparison, you multiply each score by its weight and add. College X: (5 × 4) + (4 × 3) + (3 × 5) = 20 + 12 + 15 = 47. College Y: (5 × 3) + (4 × 5) + (3 × 3) = 15 + 20 + 9 = 44. The 12 and 11 totals add raw scores without weights, and the reversed option swaps the colleges. Even with X ahead, the chapter says to check which factors drive the gap before deciding.

    **Concept Tested:** Side-by-Side Comparison

    **See:** [Making Your Final Choice](index.md)

#### 6. Jamie has decided to attend a college. Which set of steps completes accepting the offer the way the chapter describes?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Email the admissions officer and wait until August to submit any forms
2. Accept every loan offered, since aid can't be partly declined
3. Submit the intent-to-enroll form and deposit, accept the aid you want while declining unneeded loans, and tell your counselor
4. Pay the deposit and wait for the college to accept the aid on your behalf
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Accepting an offer means completing the intent-to-enroll form and paying the enrollment deposit through the applicant portal. At the same time, accept your financial aid through the aid portal, where you can accept grants and decline or reduce loans you don't need. Then save the confirmation and tell your counselor, since your school will send your final transcript there. Aid isn't accepted automatically, and you're never required to take every loan.

#### 7. A student admitted to two finalists can't afford to travel to either admitted student day. What does the chapter suggest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Wait until summer orientation to visit both colleges
2. Choose the closer college, since visiting is required to enroll
3. Ask about travel grants, attend virtual events, and request a conversation with a current student
4. Skip comparing the colleges and decide by sticker price
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says many colleges offer virtual admitted student events, and some offer travel grants for admitted students with financial need, so ask the admissions office. If you can't revisit, attend a virtual event and ask to be connected with a current student in your intended major. Visiting isn't required to enroll. Waiting until orientation means committing first, and sticker price was set aside long ago in favor of net price.

#### 8. A student has five acceptances. One has a net price far above the family's budget, one admitted the student to a different program than intended, and three are affordable with strong fit. Which reasoning matches the chapter's narrowing step?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Remove the unaffordable college and weigh the different-program offer against direct-admit options before naming two or three finalists
2. Remove the affordable colleges first, since cost is the least important factor
3. Keep all five as finalists so no option is lost
4. Keep only the most selective college as the finalist
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter says to narrow offers to two or three finalists that are affordable and that you'd be glad to attend. Remove any college that fails a dealbreaker or whose net price your family can't manage without unreasonable debt. Emily also cut a university that admitted her to general studies instead of English, since other offers admitted her to English directly. Keeping all five skips the narrowing, and selectivity isn't a reason to choose.

#### 9. Marcus asked the same three questions at both of his admitted student days. Why did that approach help him decide?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It turned two good feelings into a direct comparison on the factors he cared about
2. It let him skip writing down his impressions afterward
3. Colleges give extra aid to students who ask the same questions
4. It guaranteed both colleges would give identical answers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Asking the same questions at each finalist, about intro class size and help, internships, and how aid changes, gave Marcus answers he could compare directly. The chapter says this turned two good feelings into a real comparison: Lakeshore had strong mentoring and internships, while Brookfield had small classes but fewer internships. The answers weren't identical, which was the point. The chapter still advises writing impressions down the same day.

1. College P, because a higher national ranking always outweighs cost and fit
2. College P, because the more expensive college must offer better outcomes
3. Neither, because Riley should wait for a waitlist offer instead of committing
4. College Q, because it wins on Riley's own priorities and a large cost gap usually isn't worth a higher ranking
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says to weigh offers by what matters to you, not by national rankings, and that a higher-ranked college isn't automatically the better choice. Cost, academic fit, social fit, and gut sense all favor College Q here, and Riley can explain the choice in a sentence. College P's only advantage is its ranking. Price doesn't prove better outcomes, and a waitlist is never a plan.
