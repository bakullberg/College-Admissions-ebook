## What You Do When No One Is Grading

Your transcript shows what you did in class. Everything else you do with your time, including clubs, sports, jobs, caring for family, volunteering, and teaching yourself to code or cook or repair bikes, shows something a transcript can't: what you choose to do when no one is assigning it. That's why colleges ask about it. It tells a reader what you care about, how you take on responsibility, and what you might add to a campus.

Many students underestimate this part of their application in one of two ways. Some assume only official, school-sponsored activities count, so they leave off a job or family duties that take twenty hours a week. Others assume more is always better, so they list every club they ever joined. Both mistakes come from the same misunderstanding of what readers are looking for.

This chapter corrects it. You'll start with what counts as an extracurricular activity, which is far more than most students think. Then you'll look at the qualities readers value, including leadership, sustained commitment, depth, and measurable impact, and why being well-rounded matters less than you've heard. Next comes the specific kinds of activities worth listing, from jobs and family responsibilities to summer experiences and personal interests. Finally, you'll learn to write the activities list itself, entry by entry, and build an activities resume that also feeds your essays and recommendations.

## Extracurricular Activities

**Extracurricular activities** are the things you do outside of your regular classes that take real time and commitment. That includes school clubs and sports, but it also includes jobs, family responsibilities, volunteering, religious and cultural groups, arts, independent projects, and serious personal interests. If you spend real time on it and it says something about who you are, it's probably an activity.

The word "extracurricular" suggests something extra, on top of school. For admissions purposes, a better way to think of activities is as **evidence of character**. Readers look at your activities for signs of qualities that don't show up on a transcript:

- **Initiative:** Did you start something, or take on more than was asked?
- **Responsibility:** Did people depend on you?
- **Follow-through:** Did you stick with things over time?
- **Impact:** Did something change because you were there?
- **Interests:** What do you care about enough to give your free time to?

That evidence can come from anywhere. A part-time job can show reliability as clearly as a club presidency. Caring for younger siblings every afternoon shows responsibility as clearly as a varsity sport. The Common App's activities section reflects this. Its list of activity types includes paid work, family responsibilities, and independent projects alongside clubs, athletics, and the arts.

Activities matter more at some colleges than others. At formula-based public universities like Iowa, they may not affect basic admission at all, though they still matter for honors programs and scholarships. At holistic colleges, the activities section is one of the main sources for a reader's activities rating, and at the most selective colleges, where nearly every applicant is academically strong, it often helps separate one strong applicant from another.

#### Worked Example: Seeing Activities Clearly

Ana thinks she has almost nothing to list. "I'm not in any clubs," she tells her counselor. "I just go home after school." Her counselor asks what she does at home. Ana explains that she picks up her two younger brothers from elementary school every day, helps them with homework, cooks dinner three nights a week while her parents work, and translates at medical appointments and parent-teacher conferences. On weekends, she helps at her church's food pantry, and she's taught herself to edit videos for the church's social media.

By the end of the conversation, Ana has five strong activities totaling more than twenty-five hours a week. She didn't have nothing to list. She had activities she hadn't been taught to count.

## The Well-Rounded Applicant Myth

The **well-rounded applicant myth** is the belief that colleges want every applicant to do a little of everything: a sport, a club, an instrument, volunteering, and student government. In reality, colleges aim for a **well-rounded class** made up of students who are each deeply committed to one or two things. One student brings serious music, another competitive debate, another a business they built, another years of caring for family. Together, they make a varied community. A student with a shallow list of ten activities is usually a weaker applicant than one with real depth in two or three, because depth reads as genuine interest and breadth alone can read as résumé-padding. You don't need to give up variety you truly enjoy. Just don't add activities to look balanced.

## What Readers Value

### Leadership Roles

**Leadership roles** are positions in which you take responsibility for guiding others, organizing work, or making something happen. Club officer, team captain, and section leader are the familiar ones. But leadership is broader than titles. Training new employees at your job, organizing a neighborhood cleanup, running a study group, or managing your family's schedule while your parents work are all forms of leadership, even without a formal title.

Readers care about leadership because it shows that others trusted you and that you could get things done through people. What they care about most isn't the title. It's what you **did** with it. A club president who held two meetings a year shows less leadership than a member who planned and ran the club's biggest event. When you describe a leadership role, name the responsibility and the result, not just the position.

Leadership also tends to grow over time. Readers like to see a path: a member in 9th grade, a committee head in 10th, an officer by 11th. That path shows that people noticed your commitment and gave you more responsibility.

#### Worked Example: Leadership Without a Title

Marcus works at his family's restaurant. He has no official title, but over two years he took on more. He started by bussing tables. Then he trained two new servers, and last summer he set up a spreadsheet to track weekly inventory, cutting food waste noticeably.

On his activities list, Marcus describes it this way:

- **Position:** Server and inventory lead (family restaurant)
- **Description:** Trained 2 new servers; built inventory spreadsheet that cut weekly food waste about 20%; work 15 hrs/wk during school year.

No one would call this a typical leadership position. But a reader sees exactly what leadership looks like: trust, responsibility for others, and a problem solved.

### Sustained Commitment

**Sustained commitment** means sticking with an activity over a long period, usually multiple years, and continuing to invest in it. It's one of the clearest signals of genuine interest, because it's hard to fake. Anyone can join a club for a semester. Staying for three or four years, through busy seasons and boring stretches, shows that the activity matters to you.

On the Common App, sustained commitment shows up in two places: the **grade levels** you participated (9, 10, 11, 12) and the **hours per week and weeks per year** you spent. A reader can see at a glance whether an activity has been part of your life for one year or four.

Commitment doesn't require that you started in 9th grade. Many students discover their most important activity later. An activity you started in 10th grade and stuck with through senior year shows sustained commitment too. What weakens an application is a list where nothing lasts longer than a year.

#### Worked Example: One Year or Three?

Two students each list "Robotics Team" on their applications.

- **Student 1:** Grade 11 only. 3 hours per week, 20 weeks per year. "Member; helped build robot."
- **Student 2:** Grades 10, 11, and 12. 8 hours per week, 30 weeks per year. "Programming lead (12); wrote autonomous navigation code; mentor 4 new members."

The activity has the same name, but the commitment is very different. Student 2's entry shows years of investment, a growing role, and specific contributions. Student 1's shows a brief try. Neither is wrong to list, but Student 2's says much more.

### Hours per Week

**Hours per week** is the average number of hours you spend on an activity in a typical week while you're doing it. The Common App asks for it alongside **weeks per year**, the number of weeks you're active. Together, the two numbers let a reader estimate your total time commitment.

The arithmetic is simple:

\[
\text{Total hours per year} = \text{hours per week} \times \text{weeks per year}
\]

For example, a job worked 15 hours per week for 40 weeks totals 600 hours a year. A club that meets 2 hours per week for 30 weeks totals 60 hours. Readers do this math quickly, even if they never write it down.

Be **honest and realistic.** Give a typical week, not your busiest week. Inflated numbers are easy to spot: a student claiming 20 hours a week in six activities is claiming 120 hours a week on top of school. Count real time, including practices, rehearsals, meetings, shifts, and preparation, but not time you only thought about it. If an activity is seasonal, like a fall sport or a summer job, use the hours during the season and the number of weeks it actually runs.

#### Worked Example: Calculating Real Hours

Priya edits her school's literary magazine. During the school year, the staff meets once a week for 1.5 hours, and she spends about 2 more hours a week reading submissions and editing. In the three weeks before each of the two issues, she adds about 5 extra hours a week for layout.

- **Regular weeks:** 3.5 hours a week for 30 weeks = 105 hours.
- **Production weeks:** 8.5 hours a week for 6 weeks = 51 hours.
- **Total:** 156 hours over 36 weeks, which averages about 4.3 hours a week.

Priya reports **4 hours per week, 36 weeks per year.** The average is honest, and the total it implies, 144 hours, is close to her real time. She doesn't report her busiest production week as typical.

### Depth Over Breadth

**Depth over breadth** is the principle that a few activities pursued seriously, with growing skill, responsibility, and results, make a stronger application than many activities pursued lightly. It combines sustained commitment and leadership. Depth means you went *further* into something over time, not just that you stayed.

Depth shows up as **progression**. Your skill rose, your role grew, your work produced something, or your involvement changed other people's experience. A student who played the violin for six years and now teaches younger students and plays in a regional orchestra has depth. A student who joined eight clubs and attended a few meetings of each has breadth without depth.

When you review your list, ask of each activity: *Did I grow in this? Did I take on more over time? Did something come out of it, like a result, a skill, or a change for someone else?* If the honest answer is no, that activity can get a short entry or be cut. It's better to leave a slot empty than to fill it with something that says nothing about you.

### Measurable Impact

**Measurable impact** is a result of your activity that can be counted, described specifically, or seen, such as money raised, people served, members recruited, events run, products built, or problems solved. It answers the reader's most important question about any activity: *What difference did you make?*

Numbers are the clearest way to show impact, because they're specific and quick to read: "raised $3,000," "tutored 12 students weekly," "grew membership from 8 to 30." But not all impact is numerical. "Wrote the code that lets the robot navigate on its own" or "designed the poster now used for every school play" are specific and visible without a number.

Two cautions. First, **impact must be honest.** Don't take credit for a team's result as if it were yours alone. Say "led the team that..." or "helped raise..." when that's what happened. Second, **don't force numbers where they don't fit.** "Practiced piano 400 hours" is less meaningful than "performed a solo recital of Chopin for 80 people." Choose the detail that best shows what changed.

#### Worked Example: From Vague to Measurable

Applicant B from Chapter 2 volunteers at a community bike shop. Here's how the description improves draft by draft:

1. *"Volunteer at bike shop."* This says what, but not what they did or what difference it made.
2. *"Repair bikes for community members at local bike shop."* Better, but there's no scale or result.
3. *"Repair and refurbish donated bikes; now help run Saturday shop; about 150 bikes given to kids each year."* This one shows a growing role and a result anyone can picture.

The third version uses about the same space as the second, but a reader now knows the activity's scale and the applicant's role in it.

### Community Service

**Community service** is unpaid work that benefits other people or your community, such as tutoring, food banks, hospital volunteering, environmental cleanups, animal shelters, or helping at a place of worship. Colleges value it as evidence that you care about people beyond yourself.

What makes service strong on an application is the same as for any activity: **commitment and impact,** not the number of organizations or a large total of one-time hours. A student who volunteers at the same food pantry every Saturday for three years and now trains new volunteers shows more than one who logged a hundred hours across ten different one-day events. Service connected to your interests also reads well, such as a future nurse volunteering at a clinic or a future teacher tutoring younger students.

Be cautious about expensive "voluntourism" trips, short paid trips to volunteer abroad. Readers often see them as experiences purchased rather than commitments made. Service close to home, sustained over time, usually says more. If you did go on such a trip, describe what you actually did and learned, without exaggerating your impact.

#### Worked Example: Describing Service

Ana helps at her church's food pantry most Saturdays. Her first draft says "Volunteer at food pantry." After thinking about what she actually does, she writes:

- **Organization:** St. Luke's Community Food Pantry
- **Description:** Sort donations and pack about 60 family boxes each Saturday; translate for Spanish-speaking families at intake; 3 years.

The translation detail matters. It's a skill she brings that other volunteers don't, and it connects to her family responsibilities, which reinforces a theme in her application.

## Kinds of Activities Worth Listing

### Part-Time Jobs

A **part-time job** is paid work you do during the school year, summers, or both, whether for a business, a family business, or yourself, such as babysitting, lawn care, or tutoring. Many students leave jobs off or list them last because a job doesn't seem as impressive as a club. That's a mistake. Readers know that a job means showing up on time, handling customers, being trusted with money or safety, and managing a schedule around school. Fifteen hours a week at a grocery store is real commitment, and readers recognize it.

Jobs matter even more when they're part of your family's finances. If you work to help pay bills or save for college, that's important context, and you can say so briefly in the description or in the additional information section. A reader who knows you work twenty hours a week to support your family reads the rest of your application differently. They don't expect a long list of clubs from a student who has no time for them.

When you describe a job, include the responsibilities that grew over time, such as training others, handling the register, opening or closing, or supervising. Those are your leadership.

#### Worked Example: A Grocery Store Job

Sam works at a grocery store: 15 hours a week during the school year and 25 hours a week in summer. Sam's first draft says "Cashier at FreshMart." The revised entry reads:

- **Position:** Cashier, then front-end trainer
- **Organization:** FreshMart Grocery
- **Description:** Promoted to trainer after 1 year; train new cashiers on register and customer service; close store 2 nights/wk; save earnings for college.

A reader now sees reliability, a promotion, responsibility for others, and a purpose. Together, those are more convincing evidence of maturity than many club titles.

### Family Responsibilities

**Family responsibilities** are regular duties you carry out for your family that take significant time, such as caring for younger siblings or a relative, cooking, translating for family members, managing household tasks, or working in a family business. They're legitimate, valuable activities, and the Common App includes "Family responsibilities" as an activity type. If they're part of your life, list them plainly and without apology.

Students often hesitate because family duties feel private, or because they don't seem like "achievements." But readers are trained to see them as exactly what they are: evidence of responsibility, maturity, and time commitment. They also explain things. A student who spends twenty hours a week caring for siblings can't also be in five clubs, and a reader who sees the family responsibility understands why the list is short.

Describe what you do, how often, and what it involves. You don't need to explain why your family needs your help unless you want to. If there's important context, such as a parent's illness or a family financial crisis, the additional information section is the place for a brief, factual note.

#### Worked Example: Listing Family Duties

Ana lists her family responsibilities as two separate entries, because they're different kinds of work:

1. **Family responsibilities: sibling care.** *"Pick up 2 younger brothers daily; supervise homework; cook dinner 3 nights/wk while parents work."* 15 hours a week, 40 weeks, grades 9–12.
2. **Family responsibilities: translation.** *"Translate for parents at medical, school, and bank appointments; help complete forms in English."* 3 hours a week, 52 weeks, grades 9–12.

Together, these two entries account for eighteen hours of Ana's week and show four years of sustained responsibility. They're among the strongest entries on her list.

### Summer Experiences

**Summer experiences** are the ways you spend your summers, such as jobs, academic programs, internships, research, camps, travel, caring for family, independent projects, or simply working more hours at an activity you already do. Colleges look at summers because summers are mostly unstructured time. How you use them shows what you choose to do when no one is scheduling your day.

A common misconception is that impressive summers require expensive programs. Readers know that many prestigious-sounding summer programs simply admit anyone who can pay, and they don't weigh them heavily. A summer job, a free program at a local college, a self-directed project, or more time with family responsibilities can be just as meaningful. What matters is what you did and what came of it.

On the Common App, a summer activity can be its own entry, with the timing marked as "school break," or it can be folded into a year-round activity if it continues one you already do.

#### Worked Example: A Low-Cost Summer

Marcus couldn't afford a paid summer coding camp. Instead, he spent the summer before senior year working more restaurant shifts and, in the evenings, building a small app that lets his family's restaurant take pickup orders by text. He used free online tutorials and spent about 8 hours a week on it for 10 weeks.

He lists it as its own activity: *"Built text-message ordering app for family restaurant using free tutorials; now handles about 30 pickup orders a week."* The entry is more distinctive than a camp certificate would have been, and it gives him a ready story for a supplemental essay about his interest in computer science.

### Personal Interests

**Personal interests** are activities you pursue on your own because you enjoy them, outside any organization: teaching yourself an instrument, writing fiction, building furniture, cooking, coding, running a YouTube channel, birdwatching, restoring a car. They count. The Common App doesn't require an activity to be school-sponsored, and some of the most memorable entries are things no one asked a student to do.

Personal interests are especially valuable because they're **self-directed**. Nobody signed you up or graded you, so they show genuine curiosity and initiative. A personal interest belongs on your list when you've spent real, regular time on it and can describe something specific: what you've made, learned, or done. "Like to read" is too thin. "Read and review about 40 books a year on my book blog, 200 subscribers" is an activity.

#### Worked Example: A Hobby That Counts

Priya has baked bread since 9th grade. She thought of it as just a hobby until her counselor asked how much time she spends on it. The answer: about 4 hours a week, and she now bakes for her family, sells loaves at a farmers market stand once a month in summer, and has taught three friends to make sourdough.

Her entry reads: *"Self-taught sourdough baker; sell loaves at monthly summer farmers market; taught 3 friends."* It adds something her other activities don't. Her list is otherwise all reading and writing, and this entry shows she builds things with her hands, sticks with a craft, and shares it. It also gives her a possible essay topic.

### Honors and Awards

**Honors and awards** are formal recognitions you've received for achievement, such as academic awards, competition results, scholarships, honor societies, and athletic or artistic distinctions. On the Common App, they have their own section, separate from activities, with room for up to five entries. For each, you list the honor, the grade levels you received it, and its level of recognition.

| Level | Examples |
|-------|----------|
| School | Department award, honor roll, student of the month |
| State or regional | All-state orchestra, regional science fair placement, state writing award |
| National | National Merit recognition, national competition finalist, national scholarship recognition |
| International | International olympiad or competition placement |

Choose the most significant honors first, and keep descriptions specific: "1st place, regional science fair (biology)" rather than "science fair." Don't worry if you have few or none. Many strong applicants have one or two school-level honors. An empty honors section isn't a red flag, especially for students whose time goes to work or family, and readers won't penalize you for it.

#### Worked Example: Choosing Five Honors

Priya has seven possible honors. She ranks them by level and significance and lists five:

1. State young writers award, poetry (grade 11), state level.
2. Scholastic Art & Writing Awards Gold Key, short story (grade 11), regional level.
3. National Honor Society (grades 11–12), school level.
4. English Department Award (grade 10), school level.
5. Honor roll, all semesters (grades 9–11), school level.

She leaves off a 9th-grade spelling bee and a perfect-attendance award, which add little. Her strongest recognitions appear first, so a reader who skims sees the state award immediately.

## Writing the Activities List

### The Activities List

The **activities list** is the section of your application where you record your extracurricular activities in a fixed, structured format. On the Common App, you can list up to **10 activities**, and each entry has the same set of fields:

- **Activity type:** chosen from a menu (for example, Work (paid), Family responsibilities, Community service, Athletics, Art, Research, Other club or activity).
- **Position or leadership description:** up to 50 characters.
- **Organization name:** up to 100 characters.
- **Description:** up to **150 characters**, about one or two short lines.
- **Grade levels:** 9, 10, 11, 12, or post-graduate.
- **Timing:** during the school year, during school breaks, or all year.
- **Hours per week and weeks per year.**

Those limits are tight. A 150-character description is shorter than most text messages people write when they're explaining something. Every word has to work. The activities list rewards a writing style closer to a headline than a paragraph. That's why the next sections cover action verbs and description writing.

You don't need to fill all ten slots. Many strong applicants list six or seven. An empty slot is better than a weak entry that dilutes the strong ones.

### Action Verbs

**Action verbs** are specific verbs that describe what you actually did, such as *founded*, *organized*, *trained*, *built*, *raised*, *taught*, *designed*, or *led*. They replace vague, passive phrasing like "was involved in," "participated in," or "helped with." In a 150-character description, an action verb is the fastest way to show a reader your role.

Compare two openings:

- *"Was a member of the environmental club and participated in events."* That's 66 characters, and the reader still doesn't know what you did.
- *"Organized 4 park cleanups; recruited 25 volunteers."* That's 51 characters of specific action and results.

Strong action verbs fall into a few families, depending on what you want to show:

| To show | Verbs |
|---------|-------|
| Starting something | Founded, launched, created, established, initiated |
| Leading people | Led, trained, mentored, coached, recruited, supervised |
| Organizing | Organized, coordinated, planned, scheduled, managed |
| Making things | Built, designed, wrote, programmed, composed, produced |
| Helping others | Tutored, taught, translated, cared for, served, supported |
| Improving things | Raised, grew, increased, reduced, improved, expanded |

Choose verbs that are **true and precise**. "Led" is wrong if you helped. "Founded" is wrong if you joined. Honesty matters more than sounding impressive. Readers, recommenders, and interviewers may all ask about your activities, and the details have to hold up.

#### Worked Example: Upgrading Verbs

Sam's tutoring entry starts as *"Helped with tutoring at the middle school."* Sam asks what "helped" really meant. Sam met weekly with the same three students, planned practice problems, and led a review session before their state math test. The revised entry: *"Tutored 3 middle schoolers weekly in math; planned practice sets; led pre-test review for 20 students."* Every verb now names an action, and the entry shows both one-on-one teaching and a moment of leadership.

### Activity Descriptions

An **activity description** is the short text, up to 150 characters on the Common App, that explains what you did in an activity and what came of it. It's where readers learn the substance behind a title. A strong description follows a simple pattern: **action + specifics + result.** Lead with your strongest action verb, add a number or concrete detail, and end with the result or scope.

A few techniques help you fit more into less space:

- **Skip "I" and full sentences.** Write in clipped phrases, like a résumé: "Trained 2 new servers," not "I trained two new servers."
- **Use numerals and common abbreviations.** "3 hrs/wk" saves space, and readers expect it.
- **Separate items with semicolons** instead of using connecting words.
- **Don't repeat information from other fields.** If your position says "Captain," don't open the description with "Team captain."
- **Draft long, then cut.** Write everything you'd want a reader to know, then cut it down to the strongest details.

Write descriptions so they make sense to someone who has never seen your school. "Ran SGA spirit week" may be clear to you, but "Planned school-wide spirit week for 900 students" is clear to anyone.

#### Worked Example: Cutting to 150 Characters

Priya's first draft for her literary magazine entry runs 217 characters:

*"I am the editor-in-chief of our school's literary magazine, which publishes two issues each year. I am responsible for reading all the submissions and working with the staff on editing and on the layout of each issue."*

She cuts it in three passes:

1. **Remove "I am," "which," and filler:** *"Editor-in-chief of school literary magazine; 2 issues/yr; read all submissions; work with staff on editing and layout of each issue."* (132 characters)
2. **Move the title to the position field,** since it doesn't need to be in both places, and add a result: *"Read 200+ submissions/yr; lead staff of 8 in editing and layout; 2 issues/yr; started first online issue."* (105 characters)
3. **Lead with the strongest verb:** *"Lead staff of 8; started first online issue; read 200+ submissions/yr; edit and lay out 2 print issues/yr."* (106 characters)

The final version is about half the length of her first draft, and it says more: her leadership, an initiative she started, and the scale of the work.

### Activity Ordering

**Activity ordering** is the order in which you list your activities. On the Common App, readers see them in the order you enter them, so your order is itself a signal of what you consider most important. List your **most significant activities first**: the ones with the most time, the longest commitment, the most responsibility, or the deepest personal meaning.

Readers often spend only a short time on the activities section, so the first three or four entries do most of the work. Don't bury your best activity in slot seven because you joined it most recently or because it's not school-sponsored. A family responsibility or job that takes twenty hours a week usually belongs near the top, not at the bottom.

A useful way to decide is to rank your activities by three questions:

1. **Which ones matter most to me?** Which would I be most sorry to give up?
2. **Which show the most commitment?** Years, hours, and growing roles.
3. **Which show the most impact?** What changed because of me?

Activities that rank high on all three go first. Then check the order against your application as a whole. If your essay is about your family responsibilities, those should appear near the top of your list as well, so the two parts of the application reinforce each other.

#### Worked Example: Reordering Marcus's List

Marcus entered his activities in the order he thought of them: Computer Science Club, Intramural Basketball, Robotics Team, Restaurant Job, National Honor Society, the ordering app, and Math Tutoring. After ranking them by meaning, commitment, and impact, he reorders:

1. **Restaurant job** (15 hrs/wk, 3 years, trained staff, inventory system)
2. **Ordering app** (self-directed, real users, ties to his intended major)
3. **Robotics team** (3 years, programming lead)
4. **Computer Science Club** (2 years, member)
5. **Math tutoring** (1 year)
6. **National Honor Society**
7. **Intramural basketball**

His first three entries now show work, initiative, and depth in computer science, which is the story he wants a reader to take away. Intramural basketball is still there, but at the end, where its light commitment fits.

## The Activities Resume

An **activities resume** is a one- to two-page document that lists your activities, jobs, family responsibilities, honors, and summer experiences in more detail than the application's activities list allows. It's not usually submitted with your application, though some colleges and scholarships accept or request one. Its real value is as a **master document** you use everywhere else:

- **For the Common App activities list:** You write fuller descriptions on the resume, then cut each down to 150 characters.
- **For recommenders:** Teachers and your counselor use it to write specific, detailed letters. [Chapter 11](../11-letters-of-recommendation/index.md) covers the brag sheet, which builds on it.
- **For essays:** Reviewing your activities in one place often reveals essay topics and themes. Chapter 10 covers the activity essay.
- **For interviews and scholarships:** It's a quick reference for the stories you'll be asked about.

A good activities resume is organized by section, most important first, with a few lines for each entry:

- **Heading:** your name, school, graduation year, and contact information.
- **Activities and leadership:** each with organization, your role, years, hours, and two or three bullet points of what you did and achieved.
- **Work experience:** jobs with responsibilities and any promotions.
- **Family responsibilities:** described plainly, with hours.
- **Community service.**
- **Summer experiences.**
- **Honors and awards.**
- **Skills and interests:** languages, technical skills, and personal interests.

Start your resume early. The five first steps in Chapter 5 included drafting it, and keep it updated as senior year goes on. Because it isn't limited by character counts, it's also the place to record details you'll want later: dates, numbers, names of supervisors, and specific moments that could become essay material.

#### Worked Example: One Entry, Three Uses

Here's one entry from Ana's activities resume:

> **Sibling care and household support** (family responsibility) — Grades 9–12, about 15 hours/week
>
> - Pick up two younger brothers from school daily and supervise homework until parents return from work.
> - Cook dinner three nights a week; plan weekly grocery list within family budget.
> - Helped my younger brother raise his reading level by two grades through nightly reading practice in 7th grade.

From this one entry, Ana gets three things. For the **Common App**, she cuts it to *"Pick up 2 brothers daily; supervise homework; cook dinner 3 nights/wk; helped brother gain 2 reading levels."* For her **English teacher's recommendation**, the reading detail gives the teacher a specific example of Ana's patience to mention. For her **personal essay**, the nightly reading sessions become her topic. One well-written resume entry feeds every part of her application.

## Key Takeaways

- **Extracurricular activities** are anything outside class that takes real time and says something about you, including **part-time jobs**, **family responsibilities**, **community service**, **summer experiences**, and **personal interests**, not just school clubs.
- The **well-rounded applicant myth** is wrong. Colleges build a well-rounded class from students with real depth.
- Readers value **leadership roles** (titled or not), **sustained commitment** across years, **depth over breadth**, and **measurable impact**.
- Report **hours per week** and weeks per year honestly. Readers multiply them.
- The Common App **activities list** has 10 slots and a 150-character **activity description** for each. Lead with **action verbs**, add specifics, and end with a result.
- **Activity ordering** is a signal. Put your most meaningful, committed, and impactful activities first.
- List up to five **honors and awards** by significance. Few or none is fine.
- An **activities resume** is your master document. It feeds your activities list, your recommenders, your essays, and your interviews.

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "A student volunteers 3 hours a week for 35 weeks a year. What total yearly hours does that imply?"
    3 × 35 = **105 hours per year**.

??? question "Rewrite this description with an action verb and a specific detail: 'Was a member of the debate team.'"
    One possible answer: *"Competed in 12 tournaments/yr; reached state quarterfinals; coach 4 novice debaters."* Any revision that leads with a specific verb and includes concrete detail works, as long as it's true.

??? question "Why might a twenty-hour-a-week family responsibility belong at the top of an activities list?"
    Ordering signals importance. It represents the **largest time commitment** and strong evidence of responsibility, and it explains why the student has less time for other activities.

??? question "Is it a problem to leave three of the ten activity slots empty?"
    **No.** Many strong applicants list six or seven activities. An empty slot is better than a weak entry that dilutes the strong ones.

??? question "What's wrong with the idea that colleges want well-rounded applicants?"
    Colleges aim for a **well-rounded class** made of students with **depth** in a few areas. A shallow list of many activities usually reads as weaker than serious commitment to a few.
