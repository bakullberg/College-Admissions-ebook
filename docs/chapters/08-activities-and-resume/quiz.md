# Quiz: Activities and Resume

Test your understanding of what counts as an activity, what readers value, and how to write a strong activities list and resume with these review questions.

---

#### 1. On the Common App, how many activities can you list, and how long can each description be?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Up to 20 activities, with descriptions up to 50 characters
2. Up to 5 activities, with descriptions up to 250 characters
3. Up to 10 activities, with descriptions up to 650 words
4. Up to 10 activities, with descriptions up to 150 characters
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Common App activities list holds up to 10 activities, and each description is limited to 150 characters, about one or two short lines. The position field allows 50 characters and the organization field allows 100. The chapter notes that you don't need to fill all ten slots, since many strong applicants list six or seven. Up to five entries is the limit for the separate honors section.

#### 2. How many entries does the Common App's honors and awards section allow?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Up to five
2. Unlimited
3. Up to ten
4. Up to three
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Honors and awards have their own section on the Common App, separate from activities, with room for up to five entries. For each, you list the honor, the grade levels, and its level of recognition: school, state or regional, national, or international. The chapter advises listing the most significant honors first. It also says an empty honors section isn't a red flag, especially for students whose time goes to work or family.

#### 3. What simple pattern does the chapter recommend for a strong activity description?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Feeling + reflection + lesson
2. Goal + challenge + plan
3. Action + specifics + result
4. Title + organization + dates
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A strong activity description follows the pattern of action plus specifics plus result. You lead with your strongest action verb, add a number or concrete detail, and end with the result or scope. Title and organization have their own fields, so repeating them wastes space. Reflection and lessons belong in essays, not a 150-character description that should read more like a headline than a paragraph.

#### 4. According to the chapter, what do colleges actually aim for instead of well-rounded applicants?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Applicants who list all ten activities across different categories
2. Applicants who hold leadership titles in at least three clubs
3. A class made up mostly of students with school-sponsored activities
4. A well-rounded class made up of students who each have depth in one or two things
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The well-rounded applicant myth says colleges want every student to do a little of everything. In reality, colleges aim for a well-rounded class of students who are each deeply committed to one or two things. A shallow list of ten activities usually reads as weaker than real depth in two or three, because breadth alone can look like résumé-padding. The chapter also stresses that jobs and family duties count as fully as school clubs.

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says readers know that many prestigious-sounding summer programs admit anyone who can pay, so they don't weigh them heavily. Summers do count, because they show what you choose to do with unstructured time. A summer job, a free local program, a self-directed project, or family responsibilities can be just as meaningful. What matters is what you did and what came of it, as Marcus's ordering app shows.

#### 7. A student swims on the school team for a 14-week season and spends about 12 hours a week at practices and meets. What total yearly time does this imply, and how should it be reported?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 168 hours, reported as 3 hours per week for 52 weeks
2. 624 hours, reported as 12 hours per week for 52 weeks
3. 26 hours, reported as 12 hours per week plus 14 weeks
4. 168 hours, reported as 12 hours per week for 14 weeks
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Total hours per year equals hours per week times weeks per year, so 12 × 14 = 168 hours. The chapter says that for a seasonal activity, you use the hours during the season and the number of weeks it actually runs. Reporting 52 weeks would inflate the commitment. Spreading the hours thin across the whole year misrepresents a typical week. Adding the two numbers instead of multiplying them is simply a math error.

    **Concept Tested:** Hours per Week

#### 8. Which revision of "Participated in fundraising for the animal shelter" best uses action verbs as the chapter describes?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "I really enjoyed fundraising because I love animals"
2. "Organized 3 bake sales; raised $1,200 for local animal shelter"
3. "Animal shelter fundraising participant and volunteer member"
4. "Was very involved in helping the animal shelter with fundraising"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Action verbs such as organized and raised replace vague phrasing like "participated in" or "was involved in," and they show your role fast. The strong revision also adds specifics and a result, following the action plus specifics plus result pattern. The other options keep passive phrasing, use only nouns, or describe feelings instead of actions. The chapter also warns that verbs must be true, so "organized" works only if the student really did.

    **Concept Tested:** Action Verbs

    **See:** [Activities and Resume](index.md)

#### 9. Kai's activities include a part-time job (20 hours a week for 3 years, now a shift lead), a club joined this year (1 hour a week), and National Honor Society. Which activity should Kai list first?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The club, because it was joined most recently
2. National Honor Society, because it is school-sponsored
3. The part-time job, because it shows the most time, commitment, and responsibility
4. Whichever activity Kai entered into the Common App first
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Readers see activities in the order you enter them, so ordering signals what matters most. The chapter says to list your most significant activities first, those with the most time, longest commitment, most responsibility, or deepest meaning. A job that takes twenty hours a week usually belongs near the top. Recency and school sponsorship aren't reasons to move an activity up, and entering them in the order you thought of them is the mistake Marcus fixed.

#### 10. A student's club raised $5,000 through an event that a team of six planned together, with the student as one of two leads. Which description best balances measurable impact with honesty?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "Raised $5,000 for the club single-handedly through my event"
2. "Co-led 6-person team that planned event raising $5,000"
3. "Practiced event planning for about 100 hours this year"
4. "Participated in a club event; money was raised"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Measurable impact answers what difference you made, and numbers like $5,000 show it clearly. But the chapter cautions that impact must be honest: don't take credit for a team's result as if it were yours alone. "Co-led 6-person team" shows the real role and the real result. Claiming sole credit is dishonest, the vague version hides the impact, and the hours-practiced version forces a number that doesn't show what changed.

    **Concept Tested:** Measurable Impact
