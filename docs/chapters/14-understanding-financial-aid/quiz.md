# Quiz: Understanding Financial Aid

Test your understanding of net price, the FAFSA and CSS Profile, the Student Aid Index, need, and the main types of aid with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is the maximum Pell Grant for a full-time student in the 2026–27 aid year?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $5,500
2. $740
3. $14,790
4. $7,395
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter says the maximum Pell Grant for 2026–27 is $7,395 per year for a full-time student, and the minimum award is $740. The figure of about $14,790 is the Student Aid Index cutoff, twice the maximum Pell, above which students aren't eligible. $5,500 is the federal loan amount that appears in several examples. You apply for the Pell Grant simply by filing the FAFSA, and it follows you to any eligible college.

    **Concept Tested:** Pell Grant

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 2. A senior applying for aid for fall 2027 will report income from which year's tax return on the FAFSA?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 2026, the year the student files the form
2. 2027, the year the student starts college
3. 2025, using prior-prior year tax information
4. 2024, three years before enrollment
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The FAFSA uses prior-prior year tax information, so a student applying for aid in fall 2027 uses the family's 2025 tax return. That's why families can file in the fall, long before the school year starts. The form draws tax information directly from the IRS once each contributor gives consent. The student files the 2027–28 FAFSA, but the tax year is two years earlier than the year of enrollment.

    **Concept Tested:** FAFSA

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 3. How much does the CSS Profile cost to send?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $25 for the first college and $16 for each additional college
2. $90 per college, the same as an application fee
3. It is free for every applicant, like the FAFSA
4. $50 for any number of colleges
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The CSS Profile, run by the College Board, costs $25 for the first college and $16 for each additional one, and fee waivers are automatically granted to many lower- and middle-income families. It's the FAFSA, not the CSS Profile, that is free. About 250 colleges, mostly private, require the CSS Profile in addition to the FAFSA to award their own aid. Many colleges, including Iowa, require only the FAFSA.

    **Concept Tested:** CSS Profile

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 4. A family's FAFSA Submission Summary shows a Student Aid Index of 9,500. What does that number mean?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The family's need is $9,500 at every college on the list
2. It is an index colleges use to calculate eligibility, not the amount the family will pay
3. The student will receive $9,500 in grants from each college
4. The family will pay exactly $9,500 a year at any college
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter stresses that the Student Aid Index isn't the amount your family will pay, and it isn't the amount of aid you'll receive. It's an index colleges use to calculate eligibility, and colleges use it in different ways. Marcus's mother made the mistake of reading 9,500 as "we'll pay $9,500." Demonstrated need varies by college, because it equals each college's cost of attendance minus the SAI.

    **Concept Tested:** Student Aid Index

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 5. Two aid packages both total $20,000. One is all grants, and the other is half loans. Why is the all-grant package worth more?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Loans are only offered to students who apply Early Decision
2. Grants are gift aid you don't repay, while loans are self-help aid you must repay
3. Loans can only be used for tuition, while grants cover every cost
4. Grants count toward merit scholarships, while loans do not
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Grants and scholarships are gift aid, because you don't pay them back. Loans and work-study are self-help aid, because you either repay them or earn them. A $20,000 package made entirely of grants lowers what your family pays by the full amount, while a package that's half loans leaves you owing money later. The chapter's Marcus example shows equal totals hiding an $8,000-a-year difference in grants.

    **Concept Tested:** Financial Aid

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 6. A student who needs aid is applying to a need-aware college. What does the chapter advise?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Apply to need-blind colleges only, and skip need-aware ones
2. Apply for aid only after being admitted in the spring
3. Apply for aid, since being admitted without aid you need is far worse
4. Don't apply for aid, since it lowers admission chances
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Need-aware admissions means a college may consider ability to pay for some decisions, usually the last portion of the class. The chapter admits that applying for aid at a need-aware college can slightly lower your chances at the margin. But not applying when you need aid is far worse, because you'd be admitted without aid you can't do without. The chapter's conclusion is to apply for aid if you need it, and it doesn't tell students to avoid need-aware colleges.

    **Concept Tested:** Need-Aware Admissions

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 7. A college's cost of attendance is $52,000. A student is offered a $20,000 grant, an $8,000 merit scholarship, a $5,500 federal loan, and $3,000 in work-study. What is the net price?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $24,000
2. $32,000
3. $18,500
4. $15,500
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Net price equals the cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships: $52,000 − ($20,000 + $8,000) = $24,000. Loans and work-study are not subtracted, because they're not discounts. Loans must be repaid and work-study must be earned. Subtracting all four forms of aid gives $15,500, which understates the real cost. The loan and work-study can help pay the $24,000, but they don't reduce it.

    **Concept Tested:** Net Price

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 8. A student's Student Aid Index is 15,000. A college costs $60,000 and offers $30,000 in aid. What is the student's unmet need there?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $45,000
2. $15,000
3. $0
4. $30,000
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Demonstrated need equals cost of attendance minus the SAI: $60,000 − 15,000 = $45,000. Unmet need is the part of that need the aid package doesn't cover: $45,000 − $30,000 = $15,000. The college is said to "gap" the student, and the family must cover that $15,000 on top of the SAI. The $45,000 figure is the need itself, and $0 would apply only at a college that meets full need.

    **Concept Tested:** Unmet Need

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 9. Riley's parents are divorced and live apart. Riley spends more nights at a father's home, but the mother provided more financial support over the last 12 months and has remarried. Who are Riley's parent contributors on the FAFSA?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both biological parents
2. The father only
3. The father and the mother's spouse
4. The mother and her spouse
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. When parents are divorced, separated, or never married and don't live together, the FAFSA contributor is the parent who provided more financial support in the last 12 months, plus that parent's spouse if they've remarried. Here that's the mother and her spouse. Where Riley spends more nights doesn't decide it. Both biological parents are contributors only when they're married to each other.

    **Concept Tested:** FAFSA Contributors

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)

#### 10. Jordan's record is near the top of the admitted class at a target university and about average at a highly selective private college. Where is Jordan most likely to receive a large merit award, and why?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both equally, because merit aid is set by the FAFSA
2. The selective college, because higher-ranked colleges have bigger merit budgets
3. The target university, because merit aid is largest where a student stands out
4. Neither, because merit aid depends only on financial need
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Merit aid rewards achievement, not need. The chapter says it's most common at colleges trying to attract strong students, so large awards usually come where your record is near the top of the admitted class, often targets and safeties. The most selective colleges usually offer no merit aid at all, since every admitted student is strong and they award aid only by need. The FAFSA measures need, so it doesn't set merit awards.

    **Concept Tested:** Merit Aid

    **See:** [Understanding Financial Aid](index.md)
