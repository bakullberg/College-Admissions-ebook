# Quiz: How Colleges Evaluate Applicants

Test your understanding of how admissions offices read transcripts, GPA, rigor, and the rest of an application with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is a school profile?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A list of the activities and honors a student earned during high school
2. A short essay the student writes describing their high school experience
3. A one- or two-page summary of a high school that the counselor sends with the transcript
4. A college's annual report of the test scores and GPAs of its enrolled students
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A school profile is a one- or two-page summary of your high school that your counselor sends along with the transcript. It lists the advanced courses offered, how GPA is calculated, whether the school ranks, and what a typical graduating class looks like. It gives the transcript context, such as showing that a school offers only four AP courses. A college's report of enrolled students' scores is the Common Data Set, not a school profile.

    **Concept Tested:** School Profile

#### 2. Which of these courses is NOT one of the core academic courses colleges care about most?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Spanish III
2. Intro to Computer Science
3. U.S. Government
4. Chemistry
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter lists five core subjects: English, mathematics, laboratory science, social studies, and world language. Chemistry is a lab science, Spanish III is a world language, and U.S. Government is social studies. Computer science is named in the chapter as an elective. Electives can strengthen an application, but they do not replace the core, and many colleges leave them out when they recalculate GPA.

#### 3. A college's middle 50% range describes which part of its students?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The half of admitted students who chose to submit test scores
2. The students who scored above the college's published minimum
3. The students between the 25th and 75th percentiles
4. The students between the 50th and 100th percentiles
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The middle 50% range is the span between the 25th and 75th percentile of a college's students on a measure such as GPA or test scores. It holds the middle half of the class. The 25th percentile is not a minimum, so a quarter of the class falls below the bottom of the range. The 50th to 100th percentile would be the top half, not the middle half.

    **Concept Tested:** Middle 50% Range

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 4. On the Common App's school report, who rates the rigor of a student's course selection compared with other students at the same school?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The student, in the additional-information section
2. The student's school counselor
3. The student's AP teachers
4. The student's admissions officer at each college
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The school counselor rates the rigor of your course selection on the school report, using a five-step scale from "most demanding" down to "less than demanding." Selective colleges pay close attention to that rating because the counselor knows what courses were actually available to you. Admissions officers use the rating and the school profile to judge rigor, but they do not fill out the school report. Teachers write recommendations, not the rigor rating.

    **Concept Tested:** Course Rigor

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 5. Why do many colleges recalculate an applicant's GPA instead of using the one printed on the transcript?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. High schools differ in scale and in which courses count, so colleges put grades on common rules
2. Recalculating lets colleges include senior-year grades before they are earned
3. Colleges want to add extra points for every honors and AP course taken
4. High school GPAs are often reported incorrectly by counselors
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. GPA is less standard than it looks. High schools use different scales, treat pluses and minuses differently, include different courses, and may add weighting. To compare applicants fairly, many colleges keep only core courses from certain grades and put every school's grades on the same scale. The chapter does not suggest transcripts are usually wrong. Recalculation often removes weighting and electives rather than adding points.

    **Concept Tested:** Grade Point Average

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 6. What most distinguishes holistic review from simply ranking applicants by their numbers?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Readers ignore grades and focus mainly on essays and recommendations
2. Readers add up separate ratings into a single total score for each applicant
3. Readers judge each applicant in context, asking what the student did with the opportunities they had
4. Readers consider only applicants whose test scores meet a set cutoff
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Holistic review evaluates the whole application and judges each applicant in context. Readers ask what a student did with the opportunities they had, not whether they are the best possible applicant. The academic record still comes first at most holistic colleges, so grades are not ignored. Reader ratings help committees compare files, but the chapter says they do not add up to a single score. Score cutoffs describe formula-based admissions.

    **Concept Tested:** Holistic Review

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 7. Using the Regent Admission Index (RAI = 3 × ACT + 30 × GPA + 5 × core years), what is the RAI for a student with a 23 ACT, a 3.50 GPA, and 16 years of core courses, and which threshold does it clear?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 249, clearing neither the resident nor the nonresident minimum
2. 254, clearing the resident minimum of 245 but not the nonresident minimum of 255
3. 244, falling just short of the resident minimum of 245
4. 259, clearing both the resident and nonresident minimums
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The calculation is (3 × 23) + (30 × 3.50) + (5 × 16) = 69 + 105 + 80 = 254. That clears the 245 minimum for Iowa residents but falls one point short of the 255 minimum for nonresidents. The other totals come from arithmetic slips in one of the three terms. Like the chapter's example of Casey, this student would be a clear admit as a resident but a borderline case as an out-of-state applicant.

    **Concept Tested:** Regent Admission Index

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 8. At a test-optional college, why might the published middle 50% ACT range be higher than the scores of all admitted students?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Test-optional colleges convert all SAT scores to higher ACT equivalents
2. Students who submit scores to test-optional colleges must meet a higher minimum
3. Test-optional colleges only report the scores of students admitted Early Decision
4. Only students who chose to submit are included, and they tend to have higher scores
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. At test-optional colleges, the published test ranges include only students who chose to submit scores. Those students tend to be the ones with higher scores, which pushes the published range up. The chapter notes that a score near the bottom of such a range may be closer to the middle of all admitted students than the table suggests. The chapter does not describe special conversions, early-round-only reporting, or a higher minimum for submitters.

    **Concept Tested:** Percentile

#### 9. Riley's GPA fell from 3.9 freshman year to 3.1 junior year because of a serious illness. What does the chapter suggest Riley do about it on the application?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Ask the counselor to remove the lower grades from the transcript
2. Make the illness the topic of every supplemental essay
3. Leave it out, since readers only look at the cumulative GPA
4. Explain the cause briefly in the application's additional-information section
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Readers look at the trend of grades over time as part of the academic record, so a falling trend makes a reader wonder what changed. If the decline had a cause, such as an illness, a family crisis, or a move, the chapter says the additional-information section is the place to explain it briefly. A reader cannot give context they don't know about. Readers do not look only at the cumulative number, and transcripts are official records the student cannot edit.

    **Concept Tested:** Academic Record

    **See:** [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](index.md)

#### 10. According to the chapter, why does a rejection from a selective college often say more about that year's class than about the applicant?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The college is building a class to meet goals like filling majors, teams, and mission priorities
2. Selective colleges admit students mainly by formula and ignore the rest of the file
3. Selective colleges reserve most seats for legacy and donor applicants
4. Selective colleges reject most applicants at random once they meet a minimum
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Institutional priorities are the goals a college sets for each class, such as balancing majors, geographic reach, access, needed talents, and finances. An admissions office is building a class, not just picking the best individuals one at a time, so needs like string players or an under-enrolled major can decide close cases. The chapter calls the legacy claim a myth, and it does not describe selective admissions as random or formula-driven.

    **Concept Tested:** Institutional Priorities
