# Quiz: Scholarships

Test your understanding of the kinds of scholarships, choosing which to apply for, keeping renewable awards, and avoiding scams and displacement with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is the Federal Trade Commission's simplest rule for avoiding scholarship scams?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Only accept scholarships worth more than $1,000
2. Respond to every scholarship offer within 24 hours
3. Never pay to apply for a scholarship
4. Apply only to scholarships listed on national search engines
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter says the FTC's simplest rule covers most scams: never pay to apply for a scholarship. Legitimate scholarships don't charge application fees, and legitimate help from your counselor, your college's aid office, and the free FAFSA doesn't cost anything either. Pressure to respond within 24 hours is actually one of the scam warning signs. Award size and search engine listings don't determine whether an offer is legitimate.

#### 2. Which set describes common renewal criteria for a renewable scholarship?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A minimum college GPA, often 3.0, and full-time enrollment, often 12 credits a semester
2. Reapplying to the college each year and retaking the SAT
3. Living on campus all four years and joining an honors society
4. Keeping a perfect 4.0 GPA and taking at least 18 credits
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Scholarship renewal criteria commonly include a minimum college GPA, often 3.0 or higher, and full-time enrollment, often at least 12 credits per semester. Some awards also require staying in a particular major, filing the FAFSA or CSS Profile each year, community service, or a renewal application. A perfect 4.0 and 18 credits is far stricter than the typical criteria the chapter lists. The chapter advises saving the criteria and adding renewal deadlines to your calendar.

#### 3. Which place does the chapter list first, as offering the best payoff for your time, when looking for outside scholarships?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Professional associations in your intended field
2. Paid scholarship-matching services
3. National scholarship search engines
4. Your high school counseling office
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter lists places to look roughly in order of payoff for your time, starting with your high school counseling office, which often keeps a list of local and regional scholarships. Employers, community organizations, and professional associations come next. National search engines come last, since they're useful for volume but heavily competitive. Paying a service to find scholarships is a warning sign, because the best search tools are free.

#### 4. At a test-optional college, why can an automatic merit scholarship with a score cutoff be a strong reason to submit a test score?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A score that clears the cutoff can earn money even if it doesn't affect admission
2. Submitting a score guarantees admission at test-optional colleges
3. Test-optional colleges require scores from all scholarship winners after enrollment
4. Automatic merit scholarships are open only to Early Decision applicants
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Automatic merit scholarships go to admitted students who meet published criteria, usually GPA and test scores, with no separate application. The chapter says that at test-optional colleges, an award with a score cutoff is one of the strongest reasons to submit, since a score that clears it can be worth money. Submitting a score never guarantees admission. Some automatic awards do require an early deadline such as Early Action, but the chapter doesn't limit them to Early Decision.

    **Concept Tested:** Automatic Merit Scholarships

#### 5. Why does the chapter recommend reading a scholarship's full eligibility rules before starting the application?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A buried requirement can make you ineligible, and narrow rules also mean fewer applicants
2. Sponsors reject anyone who asks questions about eligibility
3. Eligibility rules tell you exactly how much you'll win
4. Eligibility rules change only after the deadline passes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter says many students waste hours on scholarships they can't win because of a requirement buried on page two, such as a county residence rule or a minimum GPA. Eligibility also shapes your odds: the narrower the eligibility, the fewer the applicants. That's why Ana's narrowly defined first-generation education scholarship had only a few eligible applicants. Eligibility rules don't set the award amount.

#### 6. What rule does the chapter give for reusing essays in scholarship applications?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Submit your personal essay unchanged to every scholarship
2. Change only the sponsor's name at the top of the essay
3. Reuse the material, rewrite at least a third, and address the sponsor's purpose
4. Never reuse any material from your college applications
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Scholarship essay reuse follows the same rule as essay adaptation: reuse the material, not the exact text. Start from your best existing essay, rewrite at least a third of it to answer the specific prompt, and make sure it speaks to the sponsor's purpose. Ana's teaching essay became three different scholarship essays this way, each taking about a third of the time a new essay would. Changing only the name is the recycled-essay trap.

#### 7. Scholarship X is $3,000, with 3 awards and about 60 applicants, and takes 5 hours. Scholarship Y is $600, with 1 award and about 3 applicants, and takes 1 hour. Using expected value per hour, which is the better use of time?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Scholarship X, because its award amount is five times larger
2. Scholarship Y, at about $200 per hour, compared with about $30 for X
3. They are equal, because both take roughly the same effort per dollar
4. Scholarship X, at about $600 per hour
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Expected value per hour equals award amount times chance of winning, divided by hours. For X: $3,000 × (3 ÷ 60) ÷ 5 = $30 per hour. For Y: $600 × (1 ÷ 3) ÷ 1 = $200 per hour. Choosing X because its award is bigger ignores the odds and the hours, which is exactly the mistake the formula prevents. The $600 figure divides X's award by hours without accounting for the chance of winning.

    **Concept Tested:** Expected Value of Applying

    **See:** [Scholarships](index.md)

#### 8. A student is offered a $2,500-per-year scholarship, renewable for four years, and a $6,000 one-time scholarship. If the student keeps the renewable award all four years, what is it worth in total?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $2,500
2. $10,000
3. $7,500
4. $6,000
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A renewable scholarship continues each year while you meet the criteria, so $2,500 × 4 years = $10,000, more than the $6,000 one-time award. The chapter warns that renewable awards are worth far more than they first appear, but they carry a risk if you lose them. That's why Casey estimated expected value using the share of recipients who keep the award. The $7,500 total would cover only three years.

    **Concept Tested:** Renewable Scholarships

#### 9. A student gets an email saying they've been selected for a scholarship and must provide a bank account number to "confirm eligibility." What does the chapter say this is?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A normal step that most legitimate scholarships require
2. Part of the FAFSA verification process
3. A scam warning sign, so the student should not provide the number
4. A sign the scholarship is renewable for four years
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter lists requests for a bank account or credit card number to "confirm eligibility" as a scam warning sign, along with being "selected" for a scholarship you never applied for. If something looks off, search the scholarship's name with the word "scam," ask your counselor, and report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov. FAFSA verification involves documents like tax transcripts sent to your college's aid office, not bank numbers sent to an email sender.

    **Concept Tested:** Scam Warning Signs

#### 10. A student's aid already meets full need at two colleges. After winning a $3,000 outside scholarship, College 1 cuts its federal loan by $3,000, and College 2 cuts its own grant by $3,000. How does the scholarship's real value differ?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is worth nothing at either college, so the student should decline it
2. At College 2 it lowers the net price, while at College 1 it raises it
3. It saves the student $3,000 at both colleges
4. At College 1 it replaces $3,000 of debt, while at College 2 it saves the student nothing this year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. This is award displacement, when a college reduces its aid because of an outside scholarship. At College 1, the scholarship replaces a loan, so the student borrows $3,000 less. At College 2, it replaces the college's own grant, so the student's net price and loans stay the same. That's why the chapter says to ask each aid office whether it reduces loans and work-study first or its own grants. The money is still worth winning.

    **Concept Tested:** Award Displacement
