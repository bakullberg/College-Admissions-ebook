# College Admissions Roadmap FAQ

## Getting Started Questions

### What is this book about?

College Admissions Roadmap walks you through the whole college application process, from your first college list in the summer before senior year to move-in day the following fall. Its 20 chapters cover how admissions works, building and researching a list, getting organized, testing, senior-year courses, activities, essays, recommendations, the Common App, interviews, financial aid, scholarships, award letters, waiting, decisions, the final choice, and the summer before college. Each chapter explains the key ideas, works through an example, and includes interactive MicroSims so you can try the ideas with your own numbers. The goal is a calm, organized senior year in which every decision is made on purpose. Start with the [course description](course-description.md) for the full outline.

**Example:** A student can use Chapter 4's college research tools to build a final college list, then use Chapter 16 to read an award letter from each school on it.

### Who is this book written for?

The book is written to high school students, mostly rising seniors and seniors, who are applying to four-year colleges in the United States. It assumes no prior knowledge of admissions, so it works well for first-generation students whose families haven't been through the process. Parents, guardians, and school counselors can read it alongside a student, because every chapter explains the reasons behind each step, not just the steps. It's also useful for juniors who want to understand what's ahead before senior year starts. See the [course description](course-description.md) for the intended audience and learning goals.

**Example:** A first-generation student whose parents never applied to a four-year college can follow every step, while a parent reads the financial aid chapters alongside them.

### Do I need to read the chapters in order?

No. The chapters follow the order of senior year, so reading front to back gives you the clearest picture, but each chapter is written to stand on its own when its topic becomes urgent. If your Early Action deadline is next month, you can jump straight to [The Personal Essay](chapters/09-personal-essay/index.md). If an award letter just arrived, go to [Comparing Award Letters](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md). Chapters point back to earlier ones when they rely on an idea from them. If you're starting from zero, read [Chapter 1](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) first, because its vocabulary appears throughout the book.

### Where should I start if I feel completely overwhelmed?

Start with the map, not the details. [Chapter 1](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) lays out the whole process as a timeline, so you can see that it's a sequence of manageable stages spread over a year, not one giant task. Then go to [Getting Organized](chapters/05-getting-organized/index.md), which ends with five first steps you can do this week: set up a deadline tracker, create your Common App account, ask two teachers for recommendations, draft an activities resume, and start a running list of essay ideas. Those steps turn a vague worry into a short list of things you can check off.

**Example:** A student who spends one Saturday building a tracker and creating accounts usually feels far calmer by Sunday, because the process now has a shape.

### What are MicroSims and how do I use them?

MicroSims are small interactive tools built into the chapters. Each one focuses on a single idea and lets you change inputs and see the result right away. You can calculate your GPA, plot your test score against a college's middle 50% range, sort colleges into reach, target, and safety, compare award letters, or decode the terms on an aid offer. Every MicroSim appears inside its chapter, and each also has its own page with a fullscreen link. The full set is on the [List of MicroSims](sims/index.md) page. Use them with your own numbers; that's where they're most useful.

**Example:** The [GPA Calculator](sims/gpa-calculator/index.md) shows how one grade change moves your weighted and unweighted GPA.

### Who is Priya and why does she appear in so many chapters?

Priya is a fictional senior whose application season runs through the book as a worked example. You see her build a tracker, choose recommenders, decide whether to submit test scores, draft essays, compare award letters, and make her final choice. Following one student through the whole process shows how the pieces connect, which is hard to see when each topic is explained separately. Her choices aren't the "right" answers for everyone. They show the reasoning so you can apply the same steps to your own situation. The book's capstone, in [Getting Ready for Fall](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md), builds a complete application roadmap for one real school, the University of Iowa.

### How long does the college application process take?

Plan on about a year and a half from first research to the first day of classes. Most of the work happens from the summer before senior year through the following spring. You build your list and draft your essay in summer, submit early applications in October and November, file the FAFSA in the fall, submit regular applications by January, receive decisions from December through April, choose by May 1, and handle housing, orientation, and health forms over the summer. The [Admissions Timeline](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) and the month-by-month planner in [Chapter 20](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md) break it into steps. It takes months, not days, so starting early lowers stress.

**Example:** A student who submits an Early Action application on November 1 might see a decision release date in mid-December, then wait until spring for regular decisions.

### What does the book mean by the college admissions process?

The college admissions process is the full sequence of steps from researching colleges to enrolling in one. The book groups it into six stages: build your list, test, apply, seek aid, decide, and enroll. Each stage produces something the next one needs. Your list tells you which deadlines and requirements to track, your applications produce decisions, and your aid offers shape your final choice. Seeing it as connected stages helps you avoid two common mistakes: rushing to essays before you know where you're applying, and comparing acceptances without knowing what each one will cost. See [The College Landscape and Application Rounds](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md).

**Example:** Selective admissions offices run an application review process in which a file moves from data entry to a first reader, sometimes a second reader, and then a committee before an admission decision is released.

### Do I need to hire a private admissions counselor?

No. Independent counselors can help some families, but the book is designed so you can do the whole process well without paying for one. Your school counselor writes your counselor recommendation, sends your transcript, and knows which colleges past students from your school were admitted to. Free tools cover the rest: the College Scorecard and Common Data Set for research, official practice tests for test prep, and each college's net price calculator for cost. If you do hire an independent counselor, make sure the essays and decisions stay yours. See [The People Who Help](chapters/05-getting-organized/index.md) in Chapter 5.

### What is the role of parents and family in the process?

Families work best as partners, not managers. A parent or guardian is often essential for financial aid, because the FAFSA and CSS Profile need their tax and income information, and their consent as FAFSA contributors. Families can also help with campus visits, deadline reminders through a shared tracker, and talking through costs honestly. The essays, the activities list, and the communication with colleges should stay in the student's voice. Admissions officers can tell when an adult wrote the essay, and the student is the one who will attend. [Getting Organized](chapters/05-getting-organized/index.md) covers how to share the work.

**Example:** A parent might own the family section and the FAFSA, while the student owns the essay, the activities list, and every email to the admissions office.

### What should I do the summer before senior year?

The summer before senior year is the best time to get ahead, because school isn't competing for your time. Build a long college list and start researching it. Visit nearby campuses if you can. Set up your deadline tracker, a dedicated email address, and your Common App account, which opens in early August. Brainstorm and draft your personal essay. Update your activities resume, and if you plan to test again, take a diagnostic practice test and set a test date. A student who arrives at September with a draft essay and a list in progress has a much calmer fall. See [The Senior Year Calendar](chapters/05-getting-organized/index.md).

### Where can I look up a term I don't understand?

Use the [Glossary](glossary.md). It defines 342 terms from the book in plain language, many with an example and links to related terms, from "Academic Fit" to "Yield." The site's search bar, at the top of every page, also finds terms inside the chapters. When a chapter introduces a new term, it appears in **bold** with its definition nearby. If you're comparing two similar terms, such as Early Decision and Early Action, or subsidized and unsubsidized loans, the glossary's "See also" links take you to each one so you can read them side by side.

## Core Concept Questions

### What is holistic review?

Holistic review is an admissions approach in which readers weigh your whole application together instead of plugging numbers into a formula. Your transcript and course rigor usually matter most, but readers also consider your essays, activities, recommendations, background, and the context of your school. A strong essay can't rescue a weak academic record, but it can help a reader see who you are and how you'd contribute to campus. Most selective private colleges and many selective public universities use holistic review. Contrast this with formula-based admissions, which relies mostly on GPA, test scores, or required courses. See [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md).

**Example:** Two applicants with the same GPA may get different decisions because one took harder courses and wrote an essay that showed real curiosity.

### What do colleges look at most when they read an application?

At almost every college, your **academic record** comes first: the grades on your transcript and how challenging your courses were. Readers look at your GPA, the rigor of your schedule compared with what your school offers, and your grade trend over time. After that, colleges differ. Selective colleges using holistic review also weigh essays, activities, recommendations, and sometimes interviews and demonstrated interest. Formula-based colleges may admit mostly on GPA and required courses. Colleges also have institutional priorities, such as enrolling first-generation students or filling a particular program. See [How Colleges Evaluate Applicants](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md) and try the [Holistic Review Explorer](sims/holistic-review-explorer/index.md).

**Example:** A reader opens the high school transcript first, checks course rigor against the school profile, and only then turns to essays and extracurricular activities.

### What is the difference between Early Decision and Early Action?

Both let you apply early, usually in November, and hear back early. The difference is commitment. **Early Decision (ED) is binding**: if you're admitted, you must attend, so you may apply ED to only one college. **Early Action (EA) is not binding**: you hear early but can still decline and wait until May 1 to decide, and you can usually apply EA to as many colleges as you like. ED makes sense only when one college is your clear first choice and you've confirmed you can afford it, because you won't be able to compare aid offers. See [Application Rounds](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) and try the [Application Round Explorer](sims/application-round-explorer/index.md).

**Example:** Iowa's November 2 deadline is Early Action, so applying there early gives you an early answer with no obligation.

### What are reach, target, and safety schools?

These labels describe how likely you are to be admitted at a given college. A **reach school** is one where admission is unlikely even with a strong application, either because the college admits a small share of applicants or because your academics fall below its typical range. A **target school** is one where your grades and scores sit inside the range of admitted students, so admission is reasonably likely but not certain. A **safety school** is one where you are very likely to be admitted and that you can afford. The labels are personal: the same college can be a reach for one student and a target for another. See [Finding Your Fit](chapters/03-finding-your-fit/index.md) and the [Reach, Target, Safety Classifier](sims/reach-target-safety-classifier/index.md).

### What is a balanced college list?

A balanced college list mixes reach, target, and safety schools so that you have real choices in the spring. A common guideline for a final list of eight to twelve colleges is two or three reaches, four to six targets, and two or three safeties. These numbers aren't rules. A student applying mostly to predictable, formula-based public universities might need fewer schools, while a student aiming at highly selective colleges might need more targets. The principle matters more than the count: at least one affordable college you'd love should be very likely to say yes. See [The Balanced College List](chapters/03-finding-your-fit/index.md) and try the [Balanced List Builder](sims/balanced-list-builder/index.md).

### What does college fit mean?

College fit is how well a college matches what you need to thrive, across several dimensions: **academic fit** (programs, majors, teaching style), **social fit** (campus culture and the people), **financial fit** (whether your family can afford it), and practical factors like size, setting, and distance from home. Fit matters more than prestige because you'll spend four years there, and students who fit their college are more likely to stay and graduate. The book rejects the idea that there's one "right" school. Most students could be happy and successful at many colleges. See [Finding Your Fit](chapters/03-finding-your-fit/index.md) and the [College Self-Assessment](sims/college-self-assessment/index.md).

**Example:** A student who wants small classes might weigh school size and campus setting heavily and choose a 2,000-student liberal arts college over a 40,000-student research university.

### What is the difference between sticker price and net price?

The **sticker price** is a college's published cost of attendance: tuition, fees, room and board, and other expenses, before any aid. The **net price** is what your family actually pays for one year: the cost of attendance minus all grants and scholarships. Net price is the number that matters when you compare colleges, because many expensive private colleges give large grants and can end up costing less than a cheaper-looking public option. Every college must post a net price calculator on its website, which estimates your net price from your family's finances. See [Net Price](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

**Example:** A college with a $75,000 sticker price and $55,000 in grants has a net price of $20,000.

### What is the FAFSA and who should file it?

The FAFSA, the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, is the federal form that determines your eligibility for federal grants, loans, and work-study. Most states and many colleges also use it for their own aid. **Every student should file it**, even families who think they earn too much, because some colleges require it for merit scholarships and it's the only route to federal loans. By law it opens by October 1 for the following year, and recently it has opened in late September. Each FAFSA contributor, usually you and your parents, needs an FSA ID and must give consent. See [The FAFSA](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md) and the [Financial Aid Map](sims/financial-aid-map/index.md).

**Example:** A student whose family earns too much for a Pell Grant still files the FAFSA so they can take a Federal Direct Loan and qualify for institutional aid at colleges that require it.

### What is the Student Aid Index?

The Student Aid Index (SAI) is the number the FAFSA calculates from your family's financial information to measure ability to pay for college. It replaced the older Expected Family Contribution starting with the 2024–25 aid year, and it can range from −1,500 up. A lower SAI means greater need, and an SAI of 0 or below signals the highest need. What matters most is what the SAI is *not*: it isn't the amount your family will pay, and it isn't the amount of aid you'll get. Colleges use it as an input to calculate need, and colleges that also use the CSS Profile may reach a different figure. See [The Student Aid Index](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

### What is the difference between need-based aid and merit aid?

**Need-based aid** is awarded according to your family's financial need, which colleges calculate from the FAFSA and sometimes the CSS Profile. It includes federal Pell Grants, state grants, and colleges' own need-based grants. **Merit aid** is awarded for academic achievement, talent, or other qualities, regardless of need. Some merit scholarships are automatic, based on GPA or test scores, while others are competitive and require a separate application. Many students receive both. Colleges with the largest endowments often give only need-based aid, while many public universities and less selective private colleges use merit aid to attract strong students. See [Need-Based and Merit Aid](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

**Example:** One college might offer a $15,000 need-based grant, while another offers a $10,000 automatic merit scholarship that's a renewable scholarship as long as the student keeps a 3.0 GPA.

### What does test-optional really mean?

A test-optional college lets you decide whether to submit SAT or ACT scores. If you don't submit, your application is read without them and you aren't penalized. That doesn't mean tests never matter. At a test-optional college, a score near or above the middle of the college's range can still help, because it adds evidence of academic readiness. A score well below the range usually won't help. Test-optional is different from **test-blind**, where scores aren't considered at all, and **test-required**, where you must submit. Policies change often, so check each college every year. See [Testing Policies](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md) and the [Score Submission Planner](sims/score-submission-planner/index.md).

**Example:** Under a test-optional policy, a student with a 1200 SAT can apply without scores, but under a test-required policy the same student must send them.

### How do I decide whether to submit my test scores?

Decide college by college, using the college's middle 50% range for admitted students, which you can find in its Common Data Set. If your score is at or above the middle of the range, submit it. If it's below the 25th percentile, usually don't submit to a test-optional college. If it falls in the lower part of the range, consider the rest of your application and the college's guidance. A test-required college always gets your scores. Because the decision depends on each college's range, you may send scores to some colleges and not others. See [Deciding Where to Send Scores](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md) and plot your score in the [Middle 50% Range Plotter](sims/middle-50-range-plotter/index.md).

**Example:** A 1300 SAT is worth sending to a college whose middle 50% is 1180–1340, but probably not to one whose range is 1450–1560.

### What is the middle 50% range?

The middle 50% range shows the test scores or GPAs of the middle half of a college's admitted or enrolled students. The lower number is the 25th percentile, meaning a quarter of students scored below it. The upper number is the 75th percentile, meaning a quarter scored above it. It's one of the most useful numbers in your research because it tells you where your record sits compared with the students the college actually admits. Being below the 25th percentile doesn't rule you out, but it makes that college more of a reach. Find it in the college's Common Data Set or on its admissions website. See [The Middle 50% Range](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md).

**Example:** If a college's SAT middle 50% range is 1250–1420, a 1300 sits inside it, which is a useful score benchmark comparison when deciding where to send scores.

### Why do senior-year grades still matter after I apply?

Colleges see your senior grades twice after you apply. Your counselor sends a **mid-year report** with your first-semester grades, which colleges use for regular decisions and for students who were deferred or waitlisted. Then your school sends a **final transcript** in the summer. Admission offers are conditional on finishing senior year as strong as you started it. A sharp drop in grades, failing a course, or dropping a hard class without telling the college can lead to a warning letter or, in serious cases, a rescinded offer. Senioritis is real, but the stakes are too. See [Senior Grades](chapters/07-senior-year-course-load/index.md) and the [Senior Grades Timeline](sims/senior-grades-timeline/index.md).

**Example:** A student with a heavy senior year course load who drops from A's to D's in two classes may receive a letter asking for an explanation before the offer is confirmed.

### Is it better to be well-rounded or to go deep in a few activities?

Depth usually beats breadth. The well-rounded applicant myth says colleges want every student to do a little of everything. In reality, colleges try to build a well-rounded **class** made up of students who are each deeply committed to one or two things. Two or three activities with years of commitment, growing responsibility, and real impact usually read more strongly than ten activities at an hour a week, which can look like résumé-padding. You don't need to drop activities you love, but don't add new ones just to look balanced. See [Activities and Resume](chapters/08-activities-and-resume/index.md) and the [Depth vs. Breadth Map](sims/depth-vs-breadth-map/index.md).

**Example:** Four years in the school orchestra at 8 hours per week, rising to section leader, shows sustained commitment, leadership roles, and depth over breadth.

### What kinds of activities can I list on my application?

Far more than clubs and sports. Colleges want to know how you spend your time outside class, so part-time jobs, family responsibilities like caring for siblings or translating for parents, summer experiences, community service, independent projects, and personal interests all count. Many students undersell themselves by leaving out a job or caregiving because it doesn't feel like an "activity." These often show maturity and responsibility better than a club membership does. The Common App's activities section has room for up to ten entries, and honors and awards go in their own section. See [Kinds of Activities Worth Listing](chapters/08-activities-and-resume/index.md) and try the [What Counts Activity Sorter](sims/what-counts-activity-sorter/index.md).

### What makes a strong personal essay?

A strong personal essay shows something about you that the rest of the application can't. It usually tells one specific story well rather than covering your whole life, uses concrete detail to **show** rather than **tell**, sounds like you, and ends with genuine reflection on what the experience reveals about who you are. The topic matters less than the insight. A small, ordinary moment told with honesty often beats a dramatic story told in general terms. The Common App personal essay has a 650-word limit, which leaves room for one story told well. See [The Personal Essay](chapters/09-personal-essay/index.md) and the [Essay Structure Explorer](sims/essay-structure-explorer/index.md).

**Example:** An essay about learning to cook with a grandmother can show authentic voice and reflection and insight in a way that a list of achievements can't.

### What are supplemental essays?

Supplemental essays are additional essays that individual colleges ask for beyond the main personal essay. The most common is the "Why us?" essay, but you'll also see "Why this major?", community essays, activity essays, and short-answer questions. They let a college see whether you understand what makes it distinctive and how you'd fit there, so they reward specific research. A strong supplement names particular programs, courses, or opportunities and connects them to your own interests. Because selective colleges often ask several, supplements can take more total time than the personal essay. See [Supplemental Essays](chapters/10-supplemental-essays/index.md) and the [Supplement Planner](sims/supplement-planner/index.md).

### Who should I ask for letters of recommendation?

Most selective colleges ask for one or two teacher letters plus a counselor letter, while many less selective and formula-based colleges ask for none. For teacher letters, choose teachers from core academic subjects, ideally from junior year, who know you well and saw you grow, participate, or work through difficulty. A teacher who gave you a B+ but watched you improve can write a stronger letter than one who gave you an easy A and barely knows you. If you have an intended major, one letter from a related subject helps. See [Choosing Recommenders](chapters/11-letters-of-recommendation/index.md) and the [Recommender Comparison](sims/recommender-comparison/index.md).

**Example:** Priya asks her junior-year chemistry teacher, who watched her raise a C on the first test to an A, for her main teacher recommendation.

### What is the Common App?

The Common App is an online application platform accepted by more than a thousand colleges. You fill out shared sections once, including your profile, family, education, testing, activities, and personal essay, and send them to every college on your list. Each college then adds its own section with member questions and writing supplements. You also use the Common App to invite recommenders, pay fees or request fee waivers, preview your application, and submit. Some colleges use the Coalition App or their own institutional application instead. See [Filling Out the Common App and Coalition App](chapters/12-common-app-coalition-app/index.md).

**Example:** From the Common App dashboard, a student adds colleges, sees each one's requirements, fills out the profile section and education section once, and tracks each submission confirmation.

### What are the possible admission decisions?

Colleges send several kinds of decisions. An **acceptance** offers you a place. A **denial** means the college won't offer admission this year. A **deferral**, given to early applicants, moves your application into the regular round for another review. A **waitlist** means the college may offer you a place later if spots open after admitted students respond. Each one has a next step: you can compare and accept offers, send updates after a deferral, or send a letter of continued interest if you're waitlisted. See [Handling Decisions](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md) and the [Decision Response Guide](sims/decision-response-guide/index.md).

### When do I have to choose a college?

Most colleges ask admitted students to commit by **May 1**, often called National College Decision Day. By then you accept one offer, usually by replying in the applicant portal and paying an enrollment deposit, and decline the others. Early Decision is the exception: if you're admitted ED, you commit when you're admitted, usually in December. Use March and April to compare award letters side by side, attend admitted student days, and revisit campuses if you can. If you're truly torn near the deadline, you can ask a college for a short extension. See [Making Your Final Choice](chapters/19-making-your-final-choice/index.md) and the [Final Choice Matrix](sims/final-choice-matrix/index.md).

### What is summer melt?

Summer melt is when a student who has been admitted and committed to a college doesn't show up in the fall. It usually happens because of the summer tasks that come after the decision: a missed housing deadline, an unexpected bill, a confusing loan form, a missing health record, or no one to ask for help once high school is over. Research by Benjamin Castleman and Lindsay Page found that 10 to 40 percent of college-intending students in the districts they studied didn't enroll that fall, with the highest rates among lower-income students. The fix is a plan: track summer deadlines as carefully as application deadlines. See [Getting Ready for Fall](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md).

### What is demonstrated interest?

Demonstrated interest is the evidence you give a college that you would actually enroll if admitted. It includes visiting campus, attending an information session, talking with a representative at a college fair, interviewing, opening the college's emails, and writing a specific "Why us?" essay. Colleges care because they need to predict how many admitted students will enroll, a share called yield. Not every college tracks it. Some large universities and the most selective colleges say they don't consider it, while many mid-sized private colleges do. Check the Common Data Set, which reports whether a college considers interest. See [Demonstrated Interest](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md).

## Technical Detail Questions

### What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?

An **unweighted GPA** treats every course the same, usually on a 4.0 scale where an A is 4, a B is 3, and so on. A **weighted GPA** adds extra points for harder courses such as honors, AP, IB, or dual enrollment, so an A in an AP class might count as 5 instead of 4. Schools weight courses in different ways, which is why many colleges recalculate your GPA using their own method, often counting only core academic courses. Your transcript and your school profile tell colleges which system your school uses. See [Grade Point Average](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md) and try the [GPA Calculator](sims/gpa-calculator/index.md).

**Example:** A student with an A in AP Chemistry and a B in regular English has an unweighted GPA of 3.5 and, on a common 5-point scale for AP, a weighted GPA of 4.0.

### What is course rigor?

Course rigor is how challenging your schedule is compared with what your high school offers. Colleges judge it **in context**: they read your school profile to see which advanced courses were available, so a student at a school with five AP courses isn't expected to take fifteen. Rigor shows whether you sought out challenge in the subjects that matter for college. It's often weighed alongside GPA, and a strong grade in a hard class usually counts more than a perfect grade in an easy one. Counselors often rate rigor on the counselor recommendation form. See [Course Rigor](chapters/02-how-colleges-evaluate/index.md) and the [Rigor in Context](sims/rigor-in-context/index.md) MicroSim.

### What is the difference between AP, IB, and dual enrollment?

All three are ways to take college-level work in high school. **Advanced Placement (AP)** courses follow a College Board curriculum and end with an AP exam scored 1 to 5, which many colleges accept for credit or placement. The **International Baccalaureate (IB)** is a curriculum of courses and exams, available as individual courses or as the full two-year IB Diploma. **Dual enrollment** means taking an actual college course, often through a local community college, which earns a college transcript. Each college sets its own credit policy for all three, so a course that earns credit at one may not at another. See [Advanced Placement](chapters/07-senior-year-course-load/index.md) and the [Advanced Pathways Compared](sims/advanced-pathways-compared/index.md) MicroSim.

**Example:** A student who earns a 4 AP score in Calculus AB might get college credit in high school at one university and only placement at another, which matters later for AP credit transfer.

### What is the difference between the SAT and the ACT?

Both are standardized college admissions tests, and U.S. colleges that accept scores accept either one equally. The **SAT** has a Reading and Writing section and a Math section, each scored 200 to 800, for a total of 400 to 1600. It's now digital and adaptive. The **ACT** covers English, math, and reading, with science and writing sections that may be optional depending on the format, and gives a composite score from 1 to 36. Many students do about equally well on both. Taking an official practice test of each is the best way to choose. A concordance table lets you compare scores across the two. See [The SAT and the ACT](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md) and the [SAT-ACT Converter](sims/sat-act-converter/index.md).

### What is superscoring?

Superscoring is a college practice of combining your highest section scores from different test dates into a new total. If you scored your best math in March and your best reading in June, a superscoring college uses both. Not every college superscores, and some superscore the SAT but not the ACT, so check each college's policy. Superscoring is one reason a retest can be worthwhile even if your total doesn't rise: improving one section can raise your superscore. See [Superscoring](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md) and try the [Superscore Calculator](sims/superscore-calculator/index.md).

**Example:** SAT scores of 650 Reading and Writing / 600 Math in March and 610 / 680 in June superscore to 1330.

### What is Score Choice?

Score Choice is a College Board option that lets you pick which SAT test dates to send to a college, instead of sending every score you've earned. The ACT lets you choose which test dates to send in a similar way. Some colleges, however, ask you to send all scores, so read each college's testing policy before you send anything. Score Choice affects which scores a college sees, and superscoring affects how the college combines them. Together they mean that a weak test day usually doesn't have to count against you. See [Sending Scores](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md).

### What is the difference between self-reported scores and an official score report?

Self-reported scores are scores you type into the testing section of your application yourself. An official score report is sent directly by the College Board or ACT. Many colleges now accept self-reported scores for review and ask for official reports only after you're admitted and decide to enroll, which saves you sending fees. Other colleges still require official reports by the application deadline. Your self-reported scores must match your official ones exactly, so copy them from your score report. Check each college's requirements and record them in your tracker. See [Self-Reported Scores](chapters/12-common-app-coalition-app/index.md) and [The Official Score Report](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md).

### What is the CSS Profile?

The CSS Profile is a detailed financial aid application from the College Board that about 250 colleges, mostly private, require in addition to the FAFSA to award their own grants. It asks for more information than the FAFSA, such as home equity and, at many colleges, a noncustodial parent's finances. It opens October 1, costs $25 for the first college and $16 for each additional one, and fee waivers are granted automatically to many lower- and middle-income families. Each college sets its own CSS Profile deadline, often earlier than expected for early applicants. Many colleges, including the University of Iowa, require only the FAFSA. See [The CSS Profile](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

### What is the Pell Grant?

The Pell Grant is the federal government's main grant for undergraduate students with financial need. Like other grants, it doesn't have to be repaid. Eligibility is based on the Student Aid Index from your FAFSA, so filing the FAFSA is the only way to receive it. The amount depends on your need and your enrollment status, and the maximum award is set by the federal government each year. A Pell Grant follows you to any eligible college, so it appears on every award letter you receive. See [Grants](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

### What is the difference between subsidized and unsubsidized loans?

Both are federal Direct Loans borrowed by the student. With a **subsidized loan**, the federal government pays the interest while you're enrolled at least half-time, so the amount you owe doesn't grow during college. Subsidized loans are available only to students with financial need. With an **unsubsidized loan**, interest starts building as soon as the money is paid out, and it's added to the balance if you don't pay it while in school. Unsubsidized loans are available regardless of need. Both have yearly borrowing limits. If you must borrow, use subsidized loans first. See [Federal Loans](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md).

**Example:** A $5,000 unsubsidized loan at 6% interest builds about $300 of interest in a year of college, while a subsidized loan of the same size doesn't grow while you're enrolled.

### What is a Parent PLUS Loan?

A Parent PLUS Loan is a federal loan that a parent borrows to help pay for a dependent student's college costs. Parents can borrow up to the full cost of attendance minus other aid, and approval depends on a credit check rather than income. PLUS loans usually carry higher interest rates and fees than student Direct Loans, and the parent, not the student, is legally responsible for repaying them. Some award letters list a Parent PLUS Loan as if it were part of your aid package. It isn't free money; it's borrowing. See [Parent PLUS Loans](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md).

**Example:** An award letter that lists a $12,000 Parent PLUS Loan, like any private loan, is offering borrowing, not reducing what the family owes.

### What is work-study?

Federal Work-Study is a form of self-help aid that lets students with financial need earn money through a part-time job, usually on campus. The amount on your award letter is the most you can earn, not money you receive up front, and you only get it if you find a job and work the hours. Work-study pay arrives as a paycheck during the year, so it usually goes toward books and personal expenses rather than the tuition bill. When comparing award letters, don't count work-study as reducing what you owe the college in August. See [Work-Study](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md).

### What does it mean for a college to meet full need?

A college that **meets full need** promises to cover 100% of each admitted student's demonstrated financial need, which is the cost of attendance minus the student's expected family contribution as the college calculates it. That doesn't mean college is free. The college decides how much your family can pay, and it may meet need with a mix of grants, work-study, and loans. Colleges that meet full need without loans are rare and generous. If a college doesn't meet full need, the shortfall is called **unmet need**, which your family must cover from savings, income, or extra borrowing. See [Demonstrated Financial Need](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md) and the [Need and Aid Calculator](sims/need-and-aid-calculator/index.md).

### What is the difference between need-blind and need-aware admissions?

At a **need-blind** college, your ability to pay is not considered when you're admitted. At a **need-aware** (or need-sensitive) college, ability to pay may be considered for some decisions, usually for the last part of the class when the aid budget is nearly spent. Applying for aid at a need-aware college can slightly lower your chances at the margin, but not applying when you need aid is far worse, because you could be admitted without aid you can't do without. Need-blind doesn't guarantee that a college meets full need. Colleges publish their policy on their financial aid websites. See [How Colleges Use Financial Information in Admission](chapters/14-understanding-financial-aid/index.md).

### What is the Common Data Set?

The Common Data Set is a standard report that many colleges publish each year with the same questions in the same order, so you can compare schools fairly. It includes admission rates by round, the middle 50% ranges for test scores and GPA, how much weight the college gives each part of the application, whether it considers demonstrated interest, financial aid statistics, and graduation and retention rates. Search for the college's name plus "Common Data Set" to find it, usually on the institutional research website. It's more reliable than marketing materials. See [The Common Data Set](chapters/04-researching-your-list/index.md) and the [Research Tool Matrix](sims/research-tool-matrix/index.md).

**Example:** Section C7 of the Common Data Set shows whether a college rates first-generation student status, demonstrated interest, or the essay as very important, important, considered, or not considered.

### What is the FERPA waiver?

The FERPA waiver is a choice on the Common App about whether you give up your right, under the federal Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, to see your recommendation letters after you enroll. Waiving that right tells colleges your recommenders wrote freely, knowing you wouldn't read the letter, which makes colleges trust the letters more. Most counselors advise waiving it, and some teachers prefer to write only for students who have. Not waiving doesn't disqualify you, but it can make a reader wonder about the letter. The choice applies to every college on your list and can't be changed once you've invited your first recommender. See [The FERPA Waiver](chapters/11-letters-of-recommendation/index.md).

### How much do applications cost, and what are fee waivers?

Application fees vary by college, typically ranging from nothing to around $90 per application, which adds up quickly across a list of eight to twelve schools. **Fee waivers** let eligible students apply without paying. On the Common App you can request one if you meet criteria such as receiving free or reduced-price lunch, receiving an SAT or ACT fee waiver, or having a family income within certain guidelines, and your counselor confirms it. Many colleges also waive fees during special periods or for students who visit or apply early. See [Fees and Waivers](chapters/12-common-app-coalition-app/index.md).

### What is an institution code?

An institution code is the number that identifies a college to a testing agency or aid system, so your records reach the right place. The College Board and ACT each use their own codes for sending test scores, and the FAFSA uses a separate **federal school code** for each college. Entering the wrong code can send your scores or aid information to a different school with a similar name. Look codes up from the official source each time, and copy the college's exact name and location. Record them in your tracker. See [Institution Codes](chapters/12-common-app-coalition-app/index.md).

## Common Challenge Questions

### I'm not sure what I want to major in. Is that a problem?

No. Many students start college undeclared, and many who declare a major change it. Most colleges, especially liberal arts colleges, let you explore during your first year or two before choosing. Being undecided affects your application in only a few places. Some colleges admit students directly into specific programs, such as engineering or nursing, with separate requirements, so check whether a major you might want needs a direct application. If a college asks a "Why this major?" question, answer honestly about the subjects you want to explore. Choose colleges with strength across several areas you're curious about. See [Intended Major](chapters/03-finding-your-fit/index.md).

### My test scores are lower than I hoped. What should I do?

First, compare your score with each college's middle 50% range. A score that's low for one college may be strong for another. At test-optional colleges, you can apply without scores, and your application is read on everything else. Then decide whether a retest makes sense. If you have time before your deadlines, a specific plan to improve, and a likely gain of at least a few points, a retest can help, especially at colleges that superscore. Use free official practice tests and an error log to target your weakest question types. You don't need an expensive tutor. See [The Retesting Decision](chapters/06-sat-act-question/index.md).

**Example:** A student whose ACT is 24 might apply test-optional to a college where the 25th percentile is 28 and send the score to one where the range is 21–27.

### My grades dropped one semester. Will it ruin my chances?

Usually not. Colleges look at your grade trend, and many students have one hard semester. A rising trend after a dip can even show resilience. If something outside your control caused the drop, such as an illness, a family crisis, or moving schools, you can explain it briefly and factually in the **Additional Information** section of the Common App, or ask your counselor to mention it in their letter. Don't make excuses, and don't write a full essay about it. Keep the explanation to a few sentences and focus on what changed. See [The Additional Information Section](chapters/12-common-app-coalition-app/index.md).

### I can't think of anything interesting to write my essay about. What should I do?

Almost everyone feels this way at first. The book's advice is to stop looking for a dramatic topic and start collecting material. Keep a running essay material list of small moments, habits, objects, jobs, family routines, and things you think about when no one's asking. Then look for a moment that reveals how you think or what you value. Small moment essays, about something ordinary told honestly, are often the strongest because they feel specific and real. Avoid overused topics like the winning goal or a service trip unless you have a fresh angle. See [Finding Your Story](chapters/09-personal-essay/index.md) and the [Essay Prompt Matcher](sims/essay-prompt-matcher/index.md).

**Example:** Brainstorming about a weekend job at a bike shop might lead to a personal story about fixing a stranger's flat tire in the rain, a better topic than a winning goal.

### How do I keep my essay from sounding generic?

Generic essays tell the reader what to think ("I learned the value of hard work"). Specific essays show a scene and let the reader draw the conclusion. Replace general statements with concrete details: what you saw, said, did, and noticed. Write in your own voice, the way you'd explain it to a friend, rather than in formal "essay language." End with reflection that goes beyond a lesson anyone could claim. If your essay could be swapped with another student's by changing the name, it needs more of you. Reading it aloud helps you hear where it stops sounding like you. See [Show, Don't Tell](chapters/09-personal-essay/index.md) and practice in [Show, Don't Tell Practice](sims/show-dont-tell-practice/index.md).

**Example:** Instead of "I am passionate about science," an opening hook might read "At 2 a.m., the yeast in my closet finally started to bubble."

### Can I reuse the same essay for several colleges?

You can adapt essays, but don't recycle them blindly. The **recycled essay trap** is submitting a "Why us?" essay written for one college to another with only the name changed. Readers notice, and sometimes the wrong college name slips through. Many prompts overlap, though, so it's smart to reuse your research process and some core material, then rewrite the college-specific parts. A community essay or a "Why this major?" essay can often be adapted across colleges if you replace the details with each college's real programs and opportunities. See [Managing Many Supplements](chapters/10-supplemental-essays/index.md) and the [Why Us Specificity Checker](sims/why-us-specificity-checker/index.md).

### My family can't afford the colleges I like. Should I still apply?

Often, yes, but do the math first. Many expensive colleges give generous need-based aid, so their net price can be lower than the sticker price suggests. Run each college's **net price calculator** with your family before finalizing your list. Make sure your list includes at least one college you're confident you can afford, such as an in-state public university or a college with strong merit aid for students like you. File the FAFSA, and the CSS Profile where required, on time. Then compare actual award letters in the spring. See [Financial Fit](chapters/03-finding-your-fit/index.md) and the [Cost of Attendance Builder](sims/cost-of-attendance-builder/index.md).

### I was deferred in Early Decision or Early Action. What now?

A deferral means the college wants to review you again with the regular applicants. It isn't a rejection, but it also isn't a promise. Read the deferral letter carefully and follow its instructions. Many colleges invite a short update, often called a letter of continued interest, with new grades, awards, or accomplishments. Some ask you not to send anything extra. Make sure your mid-year report goes out, keep your grades strong, and finish your other applications as planned. If you were deferred in Early Decision, you're released from the binding commitment, so you can compare any later offer with your others. Confirm this in the deferral letter. See [Deferral](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

### I was put on a waitlist. Is there anything I can do?

Yes, but plan as if you won't get in. First, accept your spot on the waitlist if you want to stay on it. Then send a short **letter of continued interest** to your regional admissions officer, confirming that you still want to attend, sharing meaningful updates since you applied, and, only if it's true, saying you'd enroll if admitted. Don't flood the office with materials. Meanwhile, commit to another college by May 1 with an enrollment deposit, because waitlist offers can come late or not at all. If you're later admitted, you may accept and withdraw from the first college. See [Waitlist Strategy](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

**Example:** A waitlisted student might send one letter in April with a new award and spring grades, while depositing at another college by the May 1 decision deadline.

### I got rejected from my dream school. How do I deal with it?

Let yourself be disappointed. That's normal, and it means you cared. Then remember what a denial actually tells you: at selective colleges, there are far more qualified applicants than spots, so a denial usually reflects the college's needs and numbers, not a judgment of your worth. The book encourages seeing rejection as a question of fit. Students thrive at many colleges, and research on outcomes shows that what you do in college matters more than where you go. Lean on your support network, limit comparison on social media, and turn your attention to the colleges that said yes. See [Keeping Perspective](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

### How do I cope with the stress of waiting for decisions?

Waiting is hard because you can't control the outcome, so focus on what you can control. Check your portals on a schedule, perhaps once a week or on known decision dates, rather than constantly. Set boundaries on social media, where decision posts invite comparison. Keep up a self-care routine with sleep, exercise, and time with friends. Lean on a support network of family, friends, and your counselor. Celebrate milestones like submitting your last application, not just acceptances. If anxiety starts affecting your sleep, eating, or schoolwork, talk to a counselor or trusted adult. See [Waiting and Managing Anxiety](chapters/17-waiting-and-anxiety/index.md) and [What Can I Control?](sims/what-can-i-control/index.md)

**Example:** During the waiting period, a student might check portals only on Fridays and plan a dinner with friends on each known decision release date.

### Can a college take back my acceptance?

Yes. A **rescinded admission** is an acceptance that a college withdraws after offering it. Offers are conditional, and colleges rescind them most often for a serious drop in senior grades, failing or dropping courses without notice, disciplinary problems, dishonesty on the application, offensive behavior including on social media, or double depositing. Rescinded offers are rare, and a college will usually contact you before taking action. To protect your offer, keep your grades steady, tell colleges about major schedule changes, stay honest, and deposit at only one college. See [Rescinded Admission](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

## Best Practice Questions

### How should I organize all my deadlines and materials?

Build your system before you write a single essay. Start a **deadline tracker**, a spreadsheet with one row per college listing its round, deadline, testing policy, essays, recommendation requirements, and aid forms. Add supporting document deadlines and aid priority deadlines, and set calendar reminders a week ahead. Share it with a parent or counselor if that helps. Create a **dedicated email address** for applications, a digital folder for drafts and transcripts, and a secure record of your logins. These steps take an afternoon and save you from missed deadlines later. See [Getting Organized](chapters/05-getting-organized/index.md) and the [Organized Setup Checklist](sims/organized-setup-checklist/index.md).

**Example:** Priya's tracker shows Hillcrest's CSS Profile is due February 15, weeks before she'd have guessed.

### How do I research a college beyond its website?

Combine data with experience. For data, use the federal **College Scorecard** for cost, graduation rates, and earnings, and the college's **Common Data Set** for admission rates, test ranges, and what it weighs. Graduation and retention rates tell you whether students thrive and stay. For experience, visit campus when you can, attend an information session, take a virtual tour, talk to representatives at college fairs, and ask current students about daily life. Marketing materials show a college at its best, while data and real conversations show what it's actually like. See [Researching and Narrowing Your College List](chapters/04-researching-your-list/index.md) and compare colleges with the [College Outcomes Comparison](sims/college-outcomes-comparison/index.md).

### How do I narrow a long list down to eight to twelve colleges?

Start with your personal priorities: the size, setting, programs, cost, and culture that matter most to you. Cut colleges that fail a must-have, such as not offering your intended major or being far out of financial reach after you run the net price calculator. Then compare the remaining colleges on fit and outcomes using data and, where possible, visits. Finally, check the balance: make sure you have two or three reaches, four to six targets, and two or three affordable safeties you'd be glad to attend. Write down why each college is on your list. If you can't, it probably shouldn't be. See [Narrowing the List](chapters/04-researching-your-list/index.md) and the [List Narrowing Funnel](sims/list-narrowing-funnel/index.md).

### How do I write strong activity descriptions in 150 characters?

Lead with an action verb, cut filler, and quantify impact. Each Common App activity description has 150 characters, so every word has to carry meaning. Say what you did and what changed because of it, using numbers where you can, such as people served, money raised, or hours worked. Skip words like "member of" or "responsible for," and use abbreviations sparingly so it still reads clearly. Your position title goes in a separate field, so don't repeat it. List your most important activities first, since readers often weigh the top entries most. See [Writing the Activities List](chapters/08-activities-and-resume/index.md) and try the [Activity Entry Builder](sims/activity-entry-builder/index.md).

**Example:** "Trained 6 new hires; ran weekend shifts; earned promotion to shift lead after 1 yr" says far more than "Worked at a grocery store."

### How and when should I ask a teacher for a recommendation?

Ask early, in person, and with materials ready. The best time is the end of junior year or the first weeks of senior year, at least a month before your earliest deadline, since popular teachers get many requests. Ask face to face if you can, and give the teacher an easy way to say no. Then send a **recommender packet**: a brag sheet with your goals, highlights from their class, and activities; your list of colleges and deadlines; and how the letter is submitted. Invite them through the Common App, send a polite reminder a week before the deadline, and write a thank-you note afterward. See [Asking for a Recommendation](chapters/11-letters-of-recommendation/index.md).

### How do I write a strong "Why us?" essay?

Be specific enough that the essay could only be about this college. Research the college's distinctive programs, courses, professors, research opportunities, traditions, and student organizations, using its website, information sessions, and conversations with students. Then connect two or three of those specifics to your own interests and experiences, showing what you'd do there and contribute. Avoid praise anyone could write, like "beautiful campus" or "excellent academics," and avoid rankings and location alone. A good test: if you could swap in another college's name and the essay would still work, it's too general. See [The Why Us Essay](chapters/10-supplemental-essays/index.md) and the [Why Us Specificity Checker](sims/why-us-specificity-checker/index.md).

**Example:** School-specific research might turn up a first-year marine biology field course and a student-run ocean research club, two details that tie directly to a student's tide-pool project.

### How should I prepare for a college interview?

Treat it as a conversation, not a test. First, find out what kind of interview it is: an evaluative interview counts in admission, while an informational one mainly helps you learn. Prepare by reviewing your application and why you're interested in the college, and practice answering common questions aloud, such as why this college, what you do outside class, and what you'd study. Prepare two or three real questions to ask your interviewer. Dress neatly, arrive or log on early, and for virtual interviews test your setup in advance. Afterward, send a short thank-you note. See [Interviews](chapters/13-interviews/index.md) and practice with the [Interview Practice Deck](sims/interview-practice-deck/index.md).

**Example:** Interview preparation might include a 20-minute practice interview with a counselor, using questions like "Tell me about yourself" and "Why this college?"

### How should I compare financial aid award letters?

Put every letter into the same format, because colleges present aid differently. For each college, list the full cost of attendance, then separate **free money** (grants and scholarships) from **borrowed money** (loans) and **earned money** (work-study). Subtract only the free money from the cost to find your real net price. Then estimate the four-year cost, check whether grants renew each year, and estimate your total debt at graduation. A letter that looks generous can rely heavily on loans. See [Reading and Comparing Award Letters](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md), and use the [Award Letter Decoder](sims/award-letter-decoder/index.md) and [Award Comparison Chart](sims/award-comparison-chart/index.md).

**Example:** College A's $20,000 out-of-pocket cost after grants beats College B's $18,000 "net cost" if B's figure already subtracted $6,000 in loans.

### Can I ask a college for more financial aid?

Yes. You can submit a financial aid appeal, also called a reconsideration request. Appeals work best when you have new information or evidence, such as a change in circumstances like a job loss, a medical cost, or a divorce, or a better offer from a comparable college. Contact the financial aid office, ask how it handles appeals, and send a polite, specific request with documentation. Don't call it a negotiation, and don't expect every college to match. Some colleges with fixed policies won't. Appeal before May 1 so you can still use the answer in your decision. See [Asking for More Aid](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md) and [Aid Reconsideration Requests](chapters/16-comparing-award-letters/index.md).

### Which scholarships are worth applying for?

Prioritize by expected value: the award amount times your realistic chance of winning, compared with the time the application takes. Local scholarships from community groups, employers, and your high school often have fewer applicants and better odds than national contests with huge prizes. Check eligibility carefully so you don't waste time on awards you can't win. Reuse and adapt scholarship essays when prompts overlap. Remember that automatic merit scholarships from colleges often add up to far more than outside awards. Start with the college's own aid, then add outside scholarships. See [Choosing Which to Apply For](chapters/15-scholarships/index.md) and the [Scholarship Priority Calculator](sims/scholarship-priority-calculator/index.md).

**Example:** A $1,000 local award you have a 1-in-5 chance of winning is worth $200 in expected value, more than a $10,000 national award with a 1-in-1,000 chance.

### How do I spot a scholarship scam?

Legitimate scholarships don't charge you to apply or to receive them. Warning signs of a scam include application or "processing" fees, guarantees that you'll win, requests for your bank account or Social Security number up front, pressure to act immediately, notices that you've won a scholarship you never applied for, and offers to fill out the FAFSA for a fee. The FAFSA itself is always free at studentaid.gov. Find scholarships through your counselor, the college's financial aid office, and reputable free search engines. See [Scholarship Scams](chapters/15-scholarships/index.md) and test yourself with [Scholarship or Scam?](sims/scholarship-or-scam/index.md)

### How do I make my final college choice?

Compare your offers side by side on the things that matter most: net price and four-year cost, total debt at graduation, academic fit for your interests, outcomes like graduation rates, and how you felt on campus. Attend admitted student days or revisit your top options to test your impressions, and talk with current students. Give each factor a weight that reflects your priorities, then see which college comes out ahead and whether that matches your gut. Decide by May 1, accept one offer, pay the deposit, and politely decline the others so their spots go to waitlisted students. See [Making Your Final Choice](chapters/19-making-your-final-choice/index.md) and the [Final Choice Matrix](sims/final-choice-matrix/index.md).

**Example:** A side-by-side comparison of two finalists might show one is $8,000 cheaper per year, while an admitted student day at the other confirms its engineering program is a better fit.

### What do I need to do over the summer after I commit?

Treat summer like another application season with its own deadlines. Watch your new college email and portal, because that's where housing, orientation, health forms, and bills appear. Complete the housing application and roommate selection by their deadlines, register for orientation, submit immunization and health records, take any required placement tests, and send AP or IB scores and your final transcript so credit is applied. Accept or decline loans, set up tuition payment, and register for classes. Missing these steps is how summer melt happens. See [Getting Ready for Fall](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md) and the [Summer Countdown](sims/summer-countdown/index.md).

**Example:** A student might complete the housing application in May, attend orientation in June, take a math placement test in July, and finish course registration before move-in day.

## Advanced Topic Questions

### Should I apply Early Decision if I need financial aid?

It can work, but only with care. Early Decision is binding, so you won't be able to compare aid offers from other colleges. Before applying, run the college's net price calculator with your family, and check whether the college meets full need. If your family is confident the estimated net price is affordable, ED can raise your chances at many colleges. If the aid offer falls short of what the net price calculator estimated and makes attending impossible, you can ask the college for an Early Decision release, but release isn't automatic and it's a last resort, not a strategy. If affordability is uncertain, Early Action or Regular Decision keeps your options open. See [Early Decision](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) and [Early Decision Release](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

### How does Restrictive Early Action work?

Restrictive Early Action (REA), also called Single-Choice Early Action, is a non-binding early round that limits where else you can apply early. If you're admitted, you're free to decline. In exchange for applying, you agree not to apply early to most other private colleges. Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Stanford are among the colleges that use a version of it. The exact restrictions differ by college; some allow early applications to public universities, for example. Read each REA policy word by word before you build your early strategy, because breaking it can cost you both applications. See [Restrictive Early Action](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md).

### How do I combine Early Decision II, Early Action, and Regular Decision into one strategy?

Think of the rounds as layers. Apply Early Action to colleges that offer it, especially targets and safeties, so you have some answers by winter. Use Early Decision, with its binding commitment, only for one clear first choice you can afford. If you're deferred or denied in ED, or you identify your first choice later, **Early Decision II**, with a January deadline, gives you another binding option elsewhere. Regular Decision covers the rest of your list. Watch for conflicts: you can't apply ED to two colleges, and Restrictive Early Action limits other early applications. Map every college's round and deadline in your tracker. See [Comparing the Rounds](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) and the [Application Round Explorer](sims/application-round-explorer/index.md).

### How do I weigh a cheaper college against a more prestigious one?

Look past the name to what each option means for your future. Compare four-year net cost and expected **total debt at graduation**, then compare outcomes that matter to you: graduation rates, retention rates, strength in your intended field, and support for students like you. Research suggests that for many students, what you do in college, such as engaging with professors, internships, and research, matters more for later success than the college's selectivity. Heavy debt limits your choices after graduation. A prestigious name may be worth a modest cost difference if it offers a program you truly can't get elsewhere, but rarely a large debt. See [Comparing Offers](chapters/19-making-your-final-choice/index.md) and the [College Outcomes Comparison](sims/college-outcomes-comparison/index.md).

**Example:** Over four years, a $10,000 yearly price gap becomes $40,000, which might mean graduating with no debt versus graduating with a loan payment of several hundred dollars a month.

### How can I use a competing offer to ask for more aid?

Some colleges will reconsider aid when you show a better offer from a comparable college, especially one they see as a competitor. Send the financial aid office a polite letter that states your interest in their college, explains the gap, and attaches the competing award letter. Compare net price and grants, not loans, since a larger loan isn't a better offer. Be honest: only ask colleges you'd actually attend, and don't exaggerate the other offer. Results vary widely. Some colleges have firm policies and won't change, so treat any increase as a bonus. See [Competing Offer Leverage](chapters/18-handling-decisions/index.md).

### How do I build a complete application plan for one college?

Bring every chapter together for that college. Start with its admission rate, round, and deadlines. Then gather its testing policy and middle 50% range, required essays and supplements, recommendation requirements, interview options, and aid forms and priority deadlines. Add its net price estimate and scholarship options, and plan each task backward from its deadline in your tracker. The book's capstone does this for the University of Iowa, from its November 2 Early Action deadline through orientation and move-in. Build one for your top college, then repeat for the rest of your list. See [The Capstone Application Roadmap](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md) and the [Personal Application Roadmap](sims/personal-application-roadmap/index.md).

### How do I know if information about a college's policies is current?

Admissions and aid policies change every year. Testing policies, essay prompts, deadlines, FAFSA dates, and fee rules have all shifted in recent years. Treat any book, website, or forum post, including this one, as a starting point, and verify each detail on the college's official admissions and financial aid websites or, for federal aid, on studentaid.gov. Check the date on the page, and when in doubt, email the admissions office. Record where and when you confirmed each policy in your tracker. See [Verifying Current Policies](chapters/20-getting-ready-for-fall/index.md).

### How does admission rate differ from my own chance of getting in?

A college's admission rate is the share of all applicants it admits, which is an average across very different applicants and rounds. Your own chance depends on how your record compares with admitted students, which round you apply in, your intended major, and the college's priorities. At a college that admits 60% of applicants, a student well below its range may have far lower odds, while at a college admitting 10%, even top applicants face real uncertainty. Use the admission rate to gauge selectivity, then use the middle 50% range and Common Data Set to judge your own position. See [Admission Rate](chapters/01-college-landscape-and-rounds/index.md) and the [Admission Rate Calculator](sims/admission-rate-calculator/index.md).
